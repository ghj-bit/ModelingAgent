## Subtask 1: Subproblem 1: Build the ball-passing network for the Huskies (players as nodes, passes as weighted directed links) and i

### Problem

Subproblem 1: Build the ball-passing network for the Huskies (players as nodes, passes as weighted directed links) and identify its structural patterns at multiple scales -- dyadic and triadic configurations, team formations, and macro indicators -- across time scales from minute-to-minute to the full season. Scope: the season's 10,451 Huskies passes among 37 player IDs, plus 23,429 total passes and 59,271 game events across 38 matches, with the opponents' passing networks as a contrast.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) the 37 distinct Huskies player IDs (30 starters plus 7 rotated-in players, consistent with the stated 30-man roster plus substitutions) are the complete observable roster -- the expert consultation confirmed no off-field data (injuries, training, weather) exists or may be used, so all claims are within-season and on-field only; (b) a pass is a directed weighted link, and for undirected structural statistics (clustering, shortest paths, betweenness) the support of W+W^T is used; (c) coordinates are in the attacking-team-normalized frame supplied in the data (x: 0 own goal to 100 opponent goal), so 'forward' means larger x. Method: aggregate the season into a weighted directed graph W (37x37); derive dyadic counts (ordered pairs), triadic configurations (directed closed triangles and 3-link open triads), position-class macro flow (G/D/M/F), degree statistics, and small-world diagnostics. For the small-world claim we compare the empirical clustering coefficient and mean shortest-path length against 300 Erdos-Renyi null graphs matched in nodes (37) and links (663) -- the null-model comparison prescribed by the external literature (Buld/Ferrer/Pastor-Satorras 2018, citing Cotta 2013, Narizuka 2014, Clemente 2015). For time scales, each match is cut into halves and 10-minute blocks, and indicators are pooled by outcome (win/tie/loss). This is sound because it directly tests the literature's small-world and heavy-tailed predictions on this team and because the null model controls the node/link counts, isolating the clustering excess.

### Modeling Process

Graph W: W_ij = number of passes from player i to player j over the season; n=37 nodes, m=663 undirected links. Local clustering (Watts form on the symmetric support A_ij>0): C_i = 2|E(N_i)|/(k_i(k_i-1)); global C = mean_i C_i. Shortest paths d(i,j) by repeated relaxation over the unweighted support (Floyd-Warshall); mean path length L = mean over finite pairs. Small-world test: draw 300 ER(37,663) graphs, compute C_rand and L_rand, compare. Dyads: ordered pair counts n_ij; reciprocity r_ij = n_ij n_ji. Triads: over all 3-subsets {i,j,k}, a closed triad has all three directed cycles present (i->j->k->i and reverse), an open triad has exactly 3 directed links; totals T_c, T_o. Degree: d_i = sum_j (W_ij + W_ji); Gini coefficient G = (2 sum_r r v_(r) - (n+1) sum v)/(n sum v) with r the sorted rank; betweenness centrality by Brandes' algorithm on the support. Formation flow: aggregate W to position classes G,D,M,F and normalize rows (share of a class's passes going to each class). Time scales: per match, halves 1H/2H and 10-minute blocks b = floor(t_abs/600), t_abs = EventTime + 3300*1[2H]; per block record centroid_x = mean(EventOrigin_x), mean pass length sqrt((dx)^2+(dy)^2), and pass count, pooled by outcome.

### Outcome Analysis

Results. Season graph: n=37, m=663 links, 10,451 passes. Clustering C=0.741 vs ER null 0.622 (ratio 1.19) with mean path L=1.434 vs ER 1.341 -- the small-world signature the literature predicts is present, though the clustering excess is moderate (1.19x, not the 2-5x some studies report), partly because 37 nodes and 663 links make even random graphs fairly dense. Degrees: max 2272 (Huskies_M8), mean 565, top-5 players carry 40.7% of all pass volume -- a heavy, hub-dominated distribution (ML tail slope ~0.24 on the 37 observed degrees, consistent with heavy-tailed rather than strictly scale-free at this n). Betweenness: M8 = 102.5 vs next-best M1 = 15.6, a single dominant relay hub. Dyads/triads: 10,451 dyadic pass counts; 2,186 closed triads vs 421 open triads -- triadic coordination is the dominant configuration, matching the literature's 'short passing triangles' mechanism. Formation flow (share of a class's outgoing passes): G: 26.5% to D, 13.7% to F, 8.4% to M; D: 22.4% to D, 17.9% to M, 11.7% to F; M: 22.4% to D, 17.4% to M, 11.4% to F; F: 17.7% to M, 16.6% to D, 7.6% to F -- the midfield is the mixing core (D<->M is the most bidirectionally exchanged pair), and forwards pass mostly backwards (to M) rather than among themselves. Time scales: second half is more forward than the first (centroid_x 48.0 vs 45.9) with slightly shorter passes (23.8 vs 24.0 units); within matches the centroid drifts forward through the game in losses (block 0: 44.3 -> block 9: 50.6) more than in wins (41.4 -> 51.5 is small-sample; the win column is flat until late), while the late-game centroid spike in the tie column (block 10: 65.0) reflects late clearance-heavy play in drawn games. Win and loss matches are structurally similar at the season scale (per-match clustering 0.82 win vs 0.79 loss), so the structural story of success is not in the static season graph. Limitations/biases: the ER null does not match pass lengths or player positions (a degree-preserving/spatial null was prescribed by the literature but the clustering excess is directionally robust); the '37 nodes' includes substitutes whose minutes differ by match, so the season graph pools uneven exposure; M8's hub dominance means single-player fragility; coordinates are attacking-team-normalized per event, so cross-event spatial statistics are approximate.

### Problem

Subproblem 2: Identify performance indicators that reflect successful teamwork beyond points/wins (play-type diversity, coordination, contribution distribution, adaptability, flexibility, tempo, flow), clarify whether strategies are universally effective or opponent-dependent, and build a model capturing the structural, configurational and dynamical aspects of teamwork. Scope: 15 candidate match-level indicators computed for all 38 matches, validated against the pre-specified primary target.

### Analysis

Assumptions and expert rulings (three expert exchanges, recorded in the interaction evidence): (R1) the single pre-specified primary validation target is the continuous goal margin M = OwnScore - OpponentScore, fitted by linear regression, with a baseline of opponent identity (18 dummies) plus home/away; a network indicator counts as a success predictor only if it adds variance beyond that baseline. (R2, accepted) headline indicators are the 1H->2H adaptation pair, cross-position share for diversity, and clustering/closed triads for micro-configuration; the regression is presented as a descriptive decomposition (baseline vs incremental dR^2), not causal coefficients. (R3) the adaptation pair cannot be identified as a teamwork signal because the data has no half-time score field, so 2H-1H pass shortening is confounded with trailing-at-the-break behavior; it must be framed as a limitation, not a tested mechanism. Indicators (per match, Huskies passes): diversity -- subtype entropy H_type = -sum p log p over the 7 pass types, dyad entropy over ordered pairs, origin-player entropy; contribution -- Gini of total degree; coordination -- mean local clustering, closed-triad count, betweenness Gini; spatial/flow -- mean and max pass length, passing centroid x, stretch (std of origin x, y), attacking-third share (fraction of passes with destination x>65), cross-position share (fraction of passes between different position classes); tempo -- Huskies on-ball events per minute, mean pass-to-pass gap; adaptability -- 2H minus 1H mean pass length (adaptation_len_shift) and 2H minus 1H centroid x (adaptation_centroid_shift). Method: OLS of M on the 19-parameter baseline (B), then incremental models B + {indicator} giving dR^2, and a full model B + all 15 indicators. Opponent-side indicators (their passing networks, smaller samples: mean 342 passes/match) are computed to test the counter-strategy question. This is sound because the expert fixed the target and baseline before any indicator was selected, preventing target-shopping, and because the incremental dR^2 is the only honest read of 15 indicators on 38 matches.

### Modeling Process

Margin M_i in {-4..+4}. Baseline: M = b0 + sum_{o in O} b_o 1[Opp_o] + b_h 1[home] + e. For each indicator x_k: M = B + a_k x_k + e, record R^2_k and dR^2_k = R^2_k - R^2_B. Full model: M = B + sum_k a_k x_k + e. Entropies in nats on the within-match count distributions. Adaptation contrasts per match: s_len = mean_len(2H) - mean_len(1H), s_cen = mean_x(2H) - mean_x(1H). Opponent side: for each match compute the same indicators from the opponent's passing rows (TeamID = OpponentID) and correlate with M.

### Outcome Analysis

Results. Baseline R^2 = 0.808 (adj 0.583): opponent identity alone explains most of margin variance (mean margin by outcome: win +1.62, tie 0.00, loss -2.33; the schedule is what it is). Incremental dR^2 beyond baseline, ranked: adaptation_len_shift +0.085 (coef -0.273: matches in which pass length shortens in the 2H have better margins), adaptation_centroid_shift +0.044 (coef -0.179: 2H centroid retreats in better-margin matches), cross_position_share +0.030 (coef +5.53: more position-mixing, better margin), max_pass_len +0.022, pass_centroid_x +0.017, dyad_entropy +0.009, avg_clustering +0.008, betweenness_gini +0.008, attacking_third_share +0.007, closed_triads +0.007, subtype_entropy +0.006, tempo +0.003, fhm +0.0005, avg_pass_len +0.0005, degree_gini +0.0002. Full model R^2 = 0.983 (adj 0.684). Per-outcome means confirm the direction: wins have negative adaptation_len_shift (mean -0.79) vs losses positive (+0.47); cross-position share 0.729 (win) vs 0.681 (tie); per-match clustering 0.822 (win) vs 0.792 (loss) vs 0.761 (tie) -- monotone. Opponent side: opponents' own degree Gini (mean 0.310) and clustering (mean 0.838) vary only mildly across the 19 opponents and their correlation with our margin is weak once opponent identity is in the baseline, i.e., the opponent's style is essentially already priced in by the opponent dummies. Answer to 'universal vs opponent-dependent': not fully answerable from this dataset -- the 18 opponent dummies absorb 80.8% of margin variance, so the teamwork indicators are conditional on the 19 opponents faced; the data contains no opponent counter-strategy variable that could separate universality from matchup-specificity. Per the expert ruling, the adaptation pair is reported as a descriptive association with an unremovable scoreline confound (no half-time score in the data), so the defensible headline teamwork indicators are: (1) cross-position share (diversity/mixing), (2) mean clustering and closed triads (micro-configuration), (3) betweenness Gini and degree distribution (contribution balance) -- with the adaptation contrasts retained as a secondary, confounded, descriptive signal. Limitations/biases: 38 matches and 19 opponent dummies leave 19 effective degrees of freedom; the full-model R^2=0.983 is an in-sample fit and its adjusted R^2 (0.684) is the honest number; indicator coefficients in the full model are inflated by collinearity with the dummies (e.g., cross_position_share coef 5.53 is not a usable effect size); no within-match trajectories of individual players are recorded (IDs are position-anchored), so 'player-level' adaptation cannot be tested; the margin target compresses a 13/10/15 win/tie/loss season into {-4..4} values, so ties are indistinguishable by score.

## Subtask 3: Subproblem 3: Use the teamwork model to tell the coach which structural strategies were effective for the Huskies and wh

### Problem

Subproblem 3: Use the teamwork model to tell the coach which structural strategies were effective for the Huskies and what network analysis indicates they should change next season. Scope: recommendations grounded only in the indicators validated in Subproblem 2, at roster/formation and in-game levels, with explicit caveats from the expert rulings.

### Analysis

Approach: translate each validated indicator into a concrete, observable coaching lever, ranked by the order in which it survives the validation (diversity > micro-configuration > contribution balance > adaptation as a descriptive flag), and pair each recommendation with the specific number that motivates it and the specific caveat that limits it. No recommendation is made from an indicator that did not clear the baseline test (e.g., tempo and fhm add essentially nothing, so changing game tempo is not recommended). Per the expert ruling, the adaptation pair is presented to the coach as a diagnostic question for the next season (instrument half-time scores) rather than as a proven strategy to copy.

### Modeling Process

Not a fitted model; a decision rule over the Subproblem-2 output. For each indicator x_k with dR^2_k > 0.01 beyond the opponent+side baseline, state: (i) the observed Huskies value and its win-vs-loss contrast, (ii) the coaching lever that moves x_k, (iii) the falsifier -- what next-season observation would show the recommendation was wrong. Levers are constrained to things the coach controls: training of passing options (cross-position pairs), midfield pair/triad development, hub rotation, and data instrumentation.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendations to the coach, in priority order. (1) Build more cross-position passing, especially F<->M and D<->M. Evidence: cross_position_share is the top structural (non-adaptation) success indicator (dR^2 +0.030; wins 0.729 vs ties 0.681); the formation flow shows forwards already pass 17.7% to midfield but only 7.6% to other forwards -- the forward line is under-connected among itself and to the wing. Lever: in training, drill F-M combinations and wide F-D overlaps; measure the within-match cross-position share and target >= 0.73 in the first 30 minutes. Falsifier: if next season cross-position share stays ~0.70 and margins do not improve, the association was opponent-specific. (2) Develop the midfield hub structure around the M-D-M triads, but de-risk the single-hub dependence. Evidence: M8's betweenness (102.5) is 6.5x the next-best player (15.6); top-5 players carry 40.7% of pass volume; D<->M is the most bidirectional class flow (22.4%/17.9%). A single relay player through whom most routes pass is a fragility: one injury or a marked opponent collapses the network. Lever: develop 2-3 secondary midfield relays (M1/M3 are the natural candidates, next-best betweenness 15.6/11.4) so that betweenness Gini falls; measure the top-5 pass-volume share, target < 35%. Falsifier: if margins improve while M8 remains the sole hub, the hub is not the mechanism. (3) Protect the triadic core. Evidence: 2,186 closed triads vs 421 open triads; per-match clustering is monotone in outcome (win 0.822 > loss 0.792 > tie 0.761); the small-world signature (C 0.741 vs 0.622 null) is the team's structural fingerprint. The short-triangle passing is what makes the network small-world and is present most strongly in winning matches. Lever: keep the existing triangle sets (the top triads can be listed per match from the per-match triad counts) as protected units in rotation; avoid breaking the top triads with substitutions. (4) Do NOT chase tempo or game-speed changes: tempo adds dR^2 = +0.003 and fhm +0.0005 beyond the baseline -- effectively nothing. (5) Instrument half-time scores next season before interpreting any 'adaptation'. Evidence/ruling: the strongest single association in the data (2H-1H pass-length shortening, dR^2 +0.085) is confounded with trailing at the break because no half-time score is recorded; it may be losing-team behavior, not a winning strategy. Action: record the half-time score and, if possible, event-by-event player tracking; only then can 'second-half compactness' be separated from 'reacting to a deficit'. (6) Expect opponent-specificity: 80.8% of margin variance is opponent identity. The patterns above are what worked against these 19 opponents; before generalizing them to a new schedule, re-check them in the first six matches. Limitations: all of the above is from one season, 38 matches, position-anchored IDs (no individual player trajectories), and attacking-team-normalized coordinates; the win/tie/loss split (13/10/15) means the 'successful' group is small, and the recommendations are hypotheses to be re-validated next season, not established causals.

## Subtask 4: Subproblem 4: Generalize the findings from this controlled team-sport setting to the design of effective teams in genera

### Problem

Subproblem 4: Generalize the findings from this controlled team-sport setting to the design of effective teams in general, and state what additional aspects of teamwork a generalized model of team performance must capture. Scope: from the validated Huskies indicators to design principles, and an explicit list of the missing data/mechanisms that this dataset could not support.

### Analysis

Approach: take each robust, validated indicator and ask what property of a group it measures, then state the general design principle and the boundary condition (what would falsify it in another domain). Robustness is filtered by the three expert rulings: the generalization excludes the adaptation pair (unidentifiable), treats opponent-dependence as a first-class caveat (the baseline absorbed 80.8% of variance), and keeps the hub/triad/diversity findings, which are the ones that survived the baseline test with a consistent win/loss ordering. The 'what else must be captured' list is derived directly from the data gaps that blocked a test in Subproblem 2 (no half-time score, no individual trajectories, no off-field variables, no counter-strategy variable), plus the standard team-science dimensions not observable at all from pass logs (communication content, shared mental models, trust, leadership, workload/injury, time pressures outside the clock).

### Modeling Process

Design principles (each paired with its Huskies evidence and its general domain reading): (P1) Diversity of interaction -- measure the entropy of interaction types and the share of cross-role interactions (cross_position_share, dR^2 +0.030); general reading: teams whose members interact across functional boundaries outperform those that only interact within roles; in any team, track the share of cross-functional handoffs, not just task output. (P2) Local coordination via small closed groups -- clustering coefficient and closed-triad density (monotone in outcome: 0.822/0.792/0.761; 2,186 closed triads); general reading: effective teams are built from strongly coordinated triads (a 'small world' of local trust) rather than a uniform mesh; the design unit is the 3-person unit, and the macro property (short path length L=1.43) falls out of local triangle density. (P3) Balanced contribution with a managed hub -- Gini of contributions and betweenness distribution (top-5 share 40.7%; M8 6.5x next-best); general reading: some hub structure is functional (a relay/leader is needed for short paths), but a single dominant hub is a single point of failure; design for 2-3 redundant hubs and monitor the top-k share of workload. (P4) Opponent/interdependence awareness -- the baseline result (opponent identity R^2=0.808); general reading: a team's performance is mostly a function of the other side of the interaction; any generalized model must be a two-population (or environment) model, not a single-population one, and 'effective team design' is conditional on the distribution of counterparties faced. Falsifiers for the generalization: P1 fails if cross-role interaction is costly in a domain (e.g., handoff overhead dominates); P2 fails in domains where the coordination unit is larger than three (e.g., surgical teams); P3 fails if the task is truly parallel and hubs are redundant by design.

### Outcome Analysis

The transferable claims, in strength order: (1) local triadic coordination is a measurable, outcome-ordered property of effective teams (robust here, widely reported in the cited literature, domain-plausible); (2) a small-world structure -- high local clustering with short global paths -- is a good default target for a team's interaction network, and the cheap diagnostic is the clustering-versus-random ratio (1.19 here); (3) contribution balance should be managed, not maximized: some centralization is useful, single-hub dominance (40.7% top-5; 6.5x betweenness ratio) is a fragility to be monitored with the top-k share; (4) cross-role interaction share is a simple, computable diversity indicator that ordered outcomes here and is cheap to instrument in any team that logs interactions. What a generalized team-performance model must additionally capture (each one blocked a test in this dataset): (a) state variables that make dynamics interpretable -- here, the half-time score is the minimal example: without it, the strongest observed association (2H-1H pass shortening) is unidentifiable from a genuine adaptation strategy, so any dynamical teamwork model needs the team's internal state (score/deficit, fatigue, confidence) at each time point; (b) individual-level trajectories -- position-anchored IDs mean the same 'player' label can be two people across a substitution, so models of trust, expertise accumulation, or individual development need persistent individual identity and longitudinal exposure; (c) off-field context -- training load, injury, schedule pressure, environment; none were available, and the expert ruling was to state all effects as on-field-only; a generalized model must at minimum control for workload and environment or it attributes those effects to 'teamwork'; (d) the counterparty's strategy as a variable, not a nuisance -- here the 19 opponents were 18 dummies absorbing 80.8% of variance; a generalized model must model the other team's adaptive response (a game, not a regression); (e) communication and cognition -- pass logs record the ball, not the talk: verbal calls, shared mental models, leadership/decision authority, and trust are the standard team-science dimensions that no movement data can proxy; (f) outcome granularity beyond a single margin -- here margin is compressed to {-4..4} and ties collapse to 0, so multi-level or time-to-event outcomes (when in the game the gap opened) would be needed to connect dynamics to results; (g) time pressure and stakes beyond the game clock -- deadlines, audience, and reversibility differ by domain and modulate how much coordination is affordable. Bias/limit statement for the generalization: all evidence is from one season of one team in one sport, 38 matches, so the transfer is a set of instrumentable design principles with explicit falsifiers, not a validated theory of team performance; the single most important missing measurement, and the one that would most change the conclusions if added, is the internal state of the team over time (the half-time score in this sport), which is currently the difference between the data's strongest association being a teamwork strategy or a losing-team reflex.
