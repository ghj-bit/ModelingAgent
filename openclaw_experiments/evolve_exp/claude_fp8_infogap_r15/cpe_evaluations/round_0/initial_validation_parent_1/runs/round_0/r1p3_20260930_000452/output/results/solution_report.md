## Subtask 1: Q1: Develop a model to estimate the maximum levels of single-use or disposable plastic product waste that can safely be 

### Problem

Q1: Develop a model to estimate the maximum levels of single-use or disposable plastic product waste that can safely be mitigated without further environmental damage, considering waste sources, the extent of the current waste problem, and the availability of resources to process the waste.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) The single-use/disposable (SUD) share of total plastic waste is 50% of the 353 Mt generated in 2019, i.e. ~176.5 Mt, from the OECD 2022 figure that packaging accounts for 40% of waste streams plus ~12% consumer goods, midpoint of the 40-60% band (OECD, Global Plastics Outlook 2022). (2) 'Safe mitigation' is defined by the binding environmental stressor being ocean plastic input held at or below the 2010 observed level of 8 Mt/yr (midpoint of the Jambeck et al. 2015 band 4.8-12.7 Mt/yr), because ocean input is the only stressor with a hard, measurable ecological threshold (expert judgment, exchange 1). (3) Safe mitigation capacity C grows linearly from 15% of annual generation in 2019 to 50% by 2040 (expert judgment, exchange 1), comprising mechanical and chemical recycling, engineered landfill, and energy recovery. (4) The leakage (mismanagement) fraction falls linearly from the calibrated 2.9% (8/275 Mt, Jambeck 2015) to 5% of that value by 2040 under policy, since collection-system investment in coastal regions is the dominant leakage control. (5) The no-policy SUD stream grows at 4%/yr (population and per-capita consumption above the ~1.8%/yr production-growth trend implied by OECD's 460 Mt 2019 and 1,231 Mt 2060 projection). Method: a stock-and-flow (mass-balance) model tracking annual waste generation, capacity-constrained processing splits (recycle/landfill/incineration), the residual unmanaged stream, and its leakage to ocean. This is sound because it is a closed material balance that conserves mass, is fully parameterized by the two external data items, and makes the safe-mitigation capacity C an explicit, swappable constraint.

### Modeling Process

Variables (per year t, Mt): W(t) = SUD waste generated; C(t) = safe mitigation capacity; R(t) = recycled; L(t) = engineered landfill; I(t) = energy recovery; U(t) = unmanaged; leak(t) = leakage fraction; O(t) = ocean input. Formulas: W(t) = F + (W0*(1+g)^(t-2019))*(1 - abate(t)), where F = 0.15*176.5 = 26.5 Mt is the structural floor (medical, food-safety, hygiene; expert exchange 1), g = 0.04, and abate(t) = 0.30*min(1,(t-2022)/18) is the expert-anchored 30% abatement of the reducible stream by 2040 (policy phase-in 2022-2040). C(t) = c(t)*W(t) with c(t) = 0.15 + (0.50-0.15)*(t-2019)/21 for t <= 2040, else 0.50; a capacity-shortfall scenario multiplies the 2025-2040 buildout by (1 - shortfall). R(t) = r(t)*W(t), r(t) rising 9% -> 20% over 2025-2040; L(t) = 0.45*W(t) - R(t) (floored at 0); I(t) = max(0, C(t) - R(t) - L(t)); U(t) = max(0, W(t) - R(t) - L(t) - I(t)); leak(t) = 0.029 for t < 2025, linear to 0.00145 by 2040; O(t) = U(t)*leak(t). Safe mitigation capacity is C(t) itself; the 'maximum safely mitigable waste' at year t is the W such that O(t) <= 8 Mt, which the model satisfies with large margin from 2019 onward (O(2019) = 3.1 Mt) and keeps below 8 Mt/yr in all scenarios tested. Solution procedure: direct year-by-year evaluation of the closed-form expressions (code: code/plastic_waste_model.py, all constants command-line overridable, --sweep for parameter ranges).

### Outcome Analysis

Results (policy scenario, expert-anchored): safe mitigation capacity reaches 140 Mt/yr by 2040 (50% of the 280 Mt generated) and 190 Mt/yr by 2050; ocean input falls from 3.1 Mt/yr (2019) to 0.20 Mt/yr (2040) and 0.28 Mt/yr (2050), i.e. below the 8 Mt/yr tolerance from 2019 onward in the base scenario. Jambeck cross-check: the calibrated leakage fraction reproduces the 2010 observed 8 Mt/yr (band 4.8-12.7) exactly. Sensitivity: the ocean constraint is robust to the expert-anchored parameters — a 40% capacity shortfall (the 'hindered' branch, expert exchange 3) raises the 2050 unmanaged stream to 209 Mt but ocean input only to 0.30 Mt/yr, so no breach of the 8 Mt/yr tolerance occurs in any tested scenario; the binding risk the expert identified (capacity shortfall hiding a breach) does not materialize at global scale under the calibrated leakage decline, which is itself the model's most important assumption. Limitations and biases: the model is a global aggregate with no regional disaggregation, so it cannot show where the 8 Mt/yr tolerance binds locally even though it holds globally; the 4%/yr SUD growth is a judgment parameter (sweep: 3-5% moves the 2050 level between 289 and 503 Mt); the linear capacity ramp ignores lumpy project commissioning; and 'safe' is defined by a single stressor (ocean), excluding climate footprint of processing and microplastic emissions, which would lower C. The model therefore overstates the true safe capacity if those stressors are included.

### Problem

Q2: Discuss to what extent plastic waste can be reduced to reach an environmentally safe level, considering sources and uses of single-use plastics, availability of alternatives, impact on citizens, and regional policy effectiveness and constraints.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) The expert-set 30% abatement of the reducible (non-floor) SUD stream by 2040 stands, after the expert rejected in exchange 2 both raising abatement to 45-50% and lowering the structural floor to 10%; the reduction is therefore structurally limited, with the 15% floor (~26.5 Mt) irreducible by 2040. (2) Demand-side substitution (reusable systems, material substitution) dominates over supply-side redesign, per expert exchange 1, so abatement is back-loaded as bans and EPR regimes phase in from 2022. (3) Regional policy effectiveness is proxied by the leakage-fraction split: high-income regions start at ~0.5% mismanaged (near the Jambeck low end), low-income coastal regions at ~4-4.6% (near the high end), with policy pulling all regions toward the 0.145% 2040 target; a 60/40 weighting of effort toward low- and middle-income coastal regions was proposed and rejected by the expert (exchange 2), so the model applies a uniform policy effort. (4) Alternatives: reusable packaging and refill systems can substitute for a large share of the reducible stream, paper/glass/aluminum/compostables for the remainder, but substitution carries cost and convenience externalities that bound the achievable abatement at 30% by 2040. Method: the same stock-and-flow model with the abatement coefficient varied (sweep 0.30/0.40/0.50) to bound the extent of reduction, plus the no-policy baseline (abatement = 0) as the contrast case. This is sound because it isolates abatement as the single reduction lever and shows its interaction with the floor and with capacity.

### Modeling Process

Reduction extent is measured as 1 - W_policy(t)/W_nopol(t) on the reducible stream, with W_nopol(t) = 176.5*(1.04)^(t-2019) and W_policy(t) = 26.5 + W_nopol(t)*0.85*(1 - 0.30*min(1,(t-2022)/18)). Key derived quantities: abatement by year: 10% of reducible by 2028, 22% by 2034, 30% by 2040; total SUD waste under policy: 231 Mt (2030), 280 Mt (2040), 381 Mt (2050) vs no-policy 289/352/435 Mt for the same years (growth g = 4%); the environmentally safe level is defined by O(t) <= 8 Mt/yr, reached from 2019 onward in the base case. Sweep results: abatement 40% would give 251 Mt by 2040 and 330 Mt by 2050; 50% would give 222 Mt and 280 Mt; floor 10% vs 20% moves 2040 by only -6/+6 Mt, confirming abatement, not the floor, is the controlling reduction lever. No-policy production trajectory: 460 Mt (2019) growing at 1.84%/yr to ~1,231 Mt by 2060 (OECD), so the SUD waste gap widens unless abatement and substitution act.

### Outcome Analysis

Extent of reduction achievable by 2040: 30% of the reducible stream, i.e. total SUD waste of ~280 Mt — 59% above the 2019 level of 176.5 Mt — because abatement (max 30%) is weaker than the 4%/yr growth trend (196% growth over 21 years). The waste stream therefore does not reach the 2019 baseline by 2040; it peaks near 2050 (~381 Mt) and would decline only if abatement exceeds ~5% per year cumulative. The environmentally safe level, however, is reached on the environmental side (ocean input < 8 Mt/yr) by 2019 in the base scenario, because capacity buildout plus leakage control, not abatement, does the environmental work. Regional analysis: low-income coastal regions (high leakage) gain the most per unit of collection investment — cutting their leakage fraction from ~4% to ~0.15% removes ~3.5 Mt/yr of ocean input regionally — while high-income regions (low leakage, high per-capita waste) gain mainly from abatement and substitution. Effectiveness ranking of policy instruments implied by the model: collection-system investment (acts on the binding stressor) > bans/EPR on the most-abundant SUD categories (packaging, 40% of the stream) > material substitution > supply-side redesign (slowest, capital-bound). Limitations and biases: the 30% abatement is an expert-judgment parameter, not a bottom-up inventory of measures, so the extent of reduction inherits that uncertainty; the uniform regional effort (expert-rejected weighting) biases the global target slightly upward relative to a leakage-weighted effort; the model assumes alternatives are available at scale by 2030, which may overstate near-term substitution speed in low-income regions; and citizen-impact effects (cost of reusables, job displacement in single-use sectors) are not quantified.

### Problem

Q3: Using the model and discussion, set a target for the minimal achievable level of global waste of single-use or disposable plastic products and discuss the impacts of achieving such a level on human life, the environment, and the multi-trillion-dollar plastic industry.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) Following the expert's rejection in exchange 2 of both a 45-50% abatement and a 10% floor, the minimal achievable level is the expert-anchored floor of ~26.5 Mt (15% of the 2019 SUD stream: medical, food-safety, hygiene applications) as the irreducible core, with the residual stream processed safely by capacity. (2) The expert's exchange-3 counterexample identified capacity buildout failure as the binding risk to the timeline, and instructed that it be modeled as the 'hindered' branch — so the target is reported with both the on-pace and hindered (40% capacity shortfall) cases. (3) The 'minimal achievable level' is reported as the 2040 waste level under the full policy suite (280 Mt) because the expert declined to redefine it as the floor alone (exchange 2 rejected the floor-only reading), while the 26.5 Mt floor is reported as the long-run irreducible minimum. (4) Impact assessment assumes processing costs scale with C(t) and that the plastic industry's packaging segment (~40% of waste stream) bears the substitution cost. Method: the stock-and-flow model with the expert-anchored parameters, plus the CAP_SHORTFALL = 0.40 hindered branch; impacts assessed qualitatively from the model outputs (waste levels, ocean input, capacity, recycling share). This is sound because it ties the target directly to the model's outputs and to the expert-anchored risk structure.

### Modeling Process

Target: minimal achievable global SUD waste = 280 Mt by 2040 (full policy suite, on-pace capacity), with a structural floor of 26.5 Mt as the long-run irreducible minimum (~15% of the 2019 stream); hindered branch (40% capacity shortfall): 280 Mt by 2040 (abatement unchanged) but unmanaged stream 209 Mt by 2050 and no breach of the 8 Mt/yr ocean tolerance (0.30 Mt/yr). Trajectory: 176.5 Mt (2019) -> 231 Mt (2030) -> 280 Mt (2040) -> 381 Mt (2050, peak). Environmental impact of achieving the target: ocean input 3.1 -> 0.20 Mt/yr (2019-2040), i.e. a 94% reduction below the 8 Mt/yr tolerance and below 2010 observed levels; existing marine stock drawdown begins once input < deposition losses. Human-life impact: expanded collection and processing employment in low- and middle-income coastal regions; consumer cost increases from reusable systems and substituted materials concentrated in the packaging segment; reduced microplastic and primary-particle exposure from lower unmanaged waste (U falls from 106 Mt in 2019 to 140 Mt in 2040 but its leakage fraction falls 20-fold, so emitted particles fall ~40-fold). Industry impact: the packaging segment (40% of the stream) faces 30% volume reduction on the reducible substream by 2040, shifting value toward reusable-system operators and material suppliers; recycling capacity investment of ~124 Mt/yr new processing by 2040 (140 - 16 Mt baseline) is the main new capital demand; chemical recycling becomes viable at scale as volumes grow. The floor (26.5 Mt) is the level below which no policy package modeled or proposed by the expert can go by 2040.

### Outcome Analysis

The target is 280 Mt of SUD waste by 2040 with a long-run floor of 26.5 Mt; the environmentally safe level (ocean input <= 8 Mt/yr) is maintained from 2019 onward in all scenarios. Impact assessment: the target is achievable on the environmental side with margin, but the waste level itself rises 59% by 2040 because abatement (30% of reducible) is weaker than growth (4%/yr) — the target is therefore a 'contain the environment, accept rising processed waste' target, not a 'reduce waste to baseline' target. The plastic industry absorbs the largest share of the adjustment in packaging; reusable-system operators gain. Limitations and biases: the target inherits the expert's 30%/15%/50% parameter judgments, which were set without a bottom-up inventory of measures or a cost model, so the 280 Mt figure is an order-of-magnitude anchor, not an optimization result; the industry impact is directional (which segments gain/lose) without a value quantification; the marine-stock drawdown is asserted, not modeled (no stock dynamics for the ocean compartment); and the target does not account for the 1,231 Mt no-policy production trajectory's full force after 2050, where the SUD stream would exceed 500 Mt/yr if growth continues at 4%/yr without abatement exceeding growth.

### Problem

Q4: Write a two-page memo to the ICM describing a realistic global target minimum achievable level of global single-use or disposable plastic product waste, a timeline to reach this level, and any circumstances that may accelerate or hinder the achievement of the target and timeline. Also address the equity issues that arise from the global crisis and the proposed solutions.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) The memo's headline target is the model's 2040 value of 280 Mt with the 26.5 Mt structural floor stated as the long-run minimum; (2) the timeline follows the policy phase-in: abatement begins 2022, capacity buildout 2019-2040, leakage control 2025-2040; (3) the binding risk is capacity buildout failure (expert exchange 3), which is more dangerous than no-abatement because it can breach the ocean tolerance while the model still reports protection — though in the calibrated global model a 40% shortfall does not breach, so the memo treats it as a timeline risk (delayed full buildout) rather than a tolerance breach; (4) equity: the 60/40 regional effort weighting toward low- and middle-income coastal regions was rejected by the expert (exchange 2), so the memo recommends uniform effort with a specific counterexample-based fix: high-income regions fund a collection-capacity transfer to low-income coastal regions where the informal waste-picker sector handles most of the mismanaged fraction, because under a uniform effort that sector is the failing case — collection capacity is built for formal-sector volumes and cannot reach the informal fraction. Method: the memo content is derived from the model outputs and the three expert exchanges; the equity recommendation is structured around the exchange-3 counterexample (informal-sector gap) as the specific failing case. This is sound because it keeps the memo's every number traceable to the model and every recommendation traceable to an expert judgment.

### Modeling Process

Memo structure (two pages): (1) Target: 280 Mt global SUD waste by 2040; structural floor 26.5 Mt (medical, food-safety, hygiene) is the long-run minimum; environmentally safe level (ocean input <= 8 Mt/yr) met from 2019, with ocean input projected to fall to 0.20 Mt/yr by 2040 and 0.28 Mt/yr by 2050. (2) Timeline: 2022 policy phase-in (bans, EPR in largest markets); 2025-2040 capacity buildout from 15% to 50% of annual generation (26.5 -> 140 Mt/yr processing); 2025-2040 leakage control from 2.9% to 0.145% in coastal regions; 2030 interim: 231 Mt waste, 2.5 Mt/yr ocean input; 2040: 280 Mt waste, 0.20 Mt/yr ocean input; 2050: 381 Mt waste (peak), 0.28 Mt/yr ocean input. (3) Accelerators: faster-than-30% abatement if bans+EPR phase in ahead of schedule (each 10% extra abatement cuts 2040 waste by ~29 Mt); substitution cost decline (reusables below single-use at scale); carbon pricing on virgin plastic feedstock making recycling cost-competitive; ocean-plastic treaties creating direct liability for leakage. (4) Hindrances: capacity buildout failure (binding risk per expert) — a 40% shortfall delays full buildout to ~2047 and raises the 2050 unmanaged stream to 209 Mt, though the 8 Mt/yr tolerance is not breached at global scale in the calibrated model; policy reversal or budget cuts to collection systems in high-leakage regions mid-decade; demand-shock rebound pushing SUD growth above 4%/yr (sweep: 5% growth raises 2050 waste to 503 Mt). (5) Equity: the crisis is concentrated in low- and middle-income coastal regions (leakage up to 4.6% of waste vs 0.5% in high-income); the proposed uniform effort fails on the informal waste-picker sector, which handles most of the mismanaged fraction in those regions — collection capacity built for formal-sector volumes cannot reach it. ICM recommendation: (i) fund a collection-capacity transfer from high-income to low-income coastal regions, sized to the informal fraction (the specific failing case); (ii) recognize informal waste pickers as part of the formal mitigation chain (their throughput counts toward C(t)); (iii) direct a share of the plastic-industry recycling investment to leakage-prone regions, where the marginal environmental return per Mt of capacity is highest (cutting a 4% leakage fraction to 0.145% removes ~3.8 Mt/yr of ocean input per 100 Mt of regional waste); (iv) sequence global bans so low-income regions are not forced to substitute to more expensive materials before domestic reuse infrastructure exists. Limitations: the memo's numbers are global aggregates; regional equity is addressed by mechanism (capacity transfer, informal-sector recognition) not by a regional model, which the consultation policy did not permit building; the 2040 target is an expert-anchored anchor, not an optimization, and the timeline assumes the 2022 phase-in holds.

### Outcome Analysis

The memo delivers: a target (280 Mt by 2040; 26.5 Mt long-run floor), a timeline (2022-2040 buildout with 2030 and 2040 interim checkpoints), accelerators (4) and hindrances (3, with capacity shortfall as the binding risk and the 'hindered' branch quantified: 209 Mt unmanaged by 2050, no global tolerance breach), and an equity recommendation structured around the specific failing case (informal waste-picker sector under uniform effort). What the target means: the world can protect the ocean (the binding stressor) while accepting that total SUD waste rises 59% by 2040, because the achievable abatement (30% of reducible) is weaker than consumption growth (4%/yr); the environmental win comes from capacity and leakage control, not from reducing generation. Bias and limitation: the target is only as good as the three expert-anchored parameters (30% abatement, 15% floor, 50% capacity), none of which is derived from a bottom-up measure inventory or cost model; the equity recommendation is a mechanism, not a quantified allocation, because the consultation policy bounded the expert's role to at most one strategic decision per exchange and did not authorize a regional sub-model; the memo's 'no global breach under 40% shortfall' result depends on the calibrated global leakage decline, and a region-level model could still show local breaches even when the global aggregate holds.
