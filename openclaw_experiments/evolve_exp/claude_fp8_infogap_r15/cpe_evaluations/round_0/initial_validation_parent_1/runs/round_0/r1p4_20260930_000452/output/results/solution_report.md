## Subtask 1: Subtask 1 (scope of the issue): quantify how many people are at risk of becoming environmentally displaced persons (EDPs

### Problem

Subtask 1 (scope of the issue): quantify how many people are at risk of becoming environmentally displaced persons (EDPs) — i.e. persons whose nation's land disappears and who must relocate cross-border — in the at-risk island nations named in the problem (Tuvalu, Kiribati, Marshall Islands, Maldives); quantify the value of the cultures of those at-risk nations and how the culture is at risk of irreversible loss; and describe how both answers change over time under plausible emissions scenarios. This is the ICM-F's first briefing requirement: the scope of the EDP issue in terms of both the number of people at risk and the risk of loss of culture.

### Analysis

Approach: a four-module, scenario-driven model (code/model_scope.py) built on the gathered empirical data (output/data/external_data.md) without any further web search. Module A (exposure) projects national habitability loss from IPCC AR6 sea-level-rise curves under SSP1-1.9 (0.38 m by 2100), SSP2-4.5 (0.56 m), SSP5-8.5 (0.77 m), with a ~2 m ice-sheet-loss tail scenario for stress-testing. Module B (scope) converts exposure into an EDP-at-risk population using a probability-weighted trigger. Module C (culture) scores each nation's cultural heritage on a cardinal 0-100 index and projects irreversible loss as the population disperses. Module D (hosts) converts the at-risk population into a feasible, obligation-weighted resettlement plan. Assumptions, all sweepable command-line parameters: (i) a 2100 evaluation horizon with 2050/2060 milestones, consistent with Groundswell's 2050 anchor and AR6's 2100 projections; (ii) a STAGED trigger — 'at risk' from the year probability-weighted habitability loss exceeds 15% (relocation-planning onset, 25% of population counted at risk), rising to full probability-weighted relocation risk once loss exceeds a 35% adaptive-capacity exhaustion threshold; (iii) the habitability trigger is the EARLIEST of land loss and freshwater-lens brackening (per the expert's Round-3 counterexample: brackening of the thin atoll aquifer at ~0.5 m rise makes islands uninhabitable well before the 35% land-loss threshold, so habitability fails on water, not submersion); (iv) a cardinal, not ordinal, cultural-heritage index (the ICM-F briefing needs a scalar loss curve); (v) host absorption capacity bounded two ways — 1% of host population (classical refugee-burden convention) and 2% of national GDP spent on 10-year integration at ~$250k per EDP. These choices were settled through a three-round expert consultation (see results/interaction_evidence.md): the expert confirmed in Round 1 that horizon, trigger, host-capacity anchor, and cultural-value basis are modeling choices the ICM-F must supply (not settled by the text); rejected the Round-2 trio of specific assumptions, which led to the staged trigger, the historical+current emission blend, and the policy-lever fade; and in Round 3 supplied the freshwater-lens counterexample that redefined the exposure trigger.

### Modeling Process

Variables: n = nation in {Tuvalu (pop 11,300; max elev 3 m; 1 language), Kiribati (130,000; 3 m; 24 languages), Marshall Islands (42,000; 3 m; 24 languages), Maldives (526,000; 2.4 m; 1 language)}; s = emissions scenario; y = year. (1) Sea-level rise: SLR(s,y) = AR6 base curve (0 at 2020, 0.05/0.10/0.20/0.30/0.42/0.55/0.68/0.80 m at 2030-2100) rescaled so SLR(s,2100) = 0.38/0.56/0.77 m for SSP1-1.9/SSP2-4.5/SSP5-8.5, plus optional tail t·(y-2080)/20 for y>=2080. (2) Land loss: L(s,y) = 0.8·[1 - (1 - SLR/r_crit)^k], r_crit = max_elev, with k = ln(0.5)/ln(1 - 0.5/r_crit) so the urban-core loss is 50% at 0.5 m (World Bank anchor: 50-80% of major urban areas at 0.5 m rise; 0.8 damps urban to national). (3) Freshwater failure: W(s,y) = clip((SLR - 0.35)/(0.65 - 0.35), 0, 1) — the atoll lens (Ghyben-Herzberg ~30 m for a 3 m island) brackens between 0.35 m (yield stress) and 0.65 m (full brackening). (4) Effective habitability loss: E(s,y) = max(L(s,y), W(s,y)). (5) Weighted loss with scenario odds w = {SSP1-1.9: 0.2, SSP2-4.5: 0.5, SSP5-8.5: 0.3}: WLoss(y) = sum_s w_s·E(s,y). (6) Staged trigger: EDP_pop(n,y) = 0 if WLoss <= 0.15; = 0.25·pop(n) if 0.15 < WLoss <= 0.35; = pop(n)·[sum of w_s over s with E(s,y) > 0.35] if WLoss > 0.35. (7) Cultural index: C(n) = 100·[2·ln(1+Lang(n)) + 3·IICH(n) + 5·(pop_in_country/pop_baseline)] / [2·ln(25) + 6 + 5], where Lang is the language count and IICH the UNESCO intangible-heritage count (Tuvalu 1/1, Kiribati 24/1, Marshalls 24/0, Maldives 1/2). (8) Irreversible cultural loss: Loss(n,y) = max(0, 100·(1 - C_y/C_0)) where C_y = C(in_country) + (displaced/pop)·(1 - 0.3·(1-PE))^((y-2030)/30)·C_resid, C_resid the population-share-free part of the index, and PE the policy effectiveness in [0,1] (the policy lever: effective fade = 0.3·(1-PE) per 30-year generation). Solution procedure: evaluate all four nations at y = 2030-2100 in 10-year steps for each of the three AR6 scenarios, aggregate by scenario odds, and sweep the parameters (P_TRIG, onset, disperse_rate, POLICY_LEVER, TAIL) via command line (e.g. --sweep POLICY_LEVER=0,0.5,1.0).

### Outcome Analysis

Scope results (probability-weighted AR6 mix, base parameters): EDP-at-risk of the four atoll nations is 0 through 2050; ~177,325 by 2060 (staged early onset, water- and land-driven); ~212,790 by 2080; and ~567,440 by 2090-2100 — essentially all of Tuvalu (11,300), Kiribati (130,000), Marshall Islands (42,000) and ~80% of the Maldives (420,800 of 526,000). The ~2 m ice-sheet tail pushes the 2100 total to 709,300 (all of the Maldives). This is 0.40% of Groundswell's 143M internal climate migrants by 2050 — confirming cross-border nationless EDPs are an existential, not numerical, minority of total climate displacement, which justifies treating them as a distinct policy flow. Culture results: without any policy (PE=0), irreversible cultural loss by 2100 is 26.4% (Tuvalu), 5.0% (Kiribati), 15.4% (Marshall Islands), 11.5% (Maldives); with a fully effective cultural-protection policy (PE=1) it falls to 5.2%/0%/0%/0% — the policy lever is worth roughly 20 percentage points for Tuvalu, the most vulnerable single-language small-population culture. The single most consequential finding: habitability fails on water, not submersion. The freshwater-lens trigger (Round-3 expert counterexample) is what moves the 2080 figure from 177,325 to 212,790 and makes the 2060 onset water-driven for the Pacific atolls; a pure land-loss model would understate Tuvalu/Kiribati risk by ~16% at 2080. Limitations and biases: (i) the elevation-distribution land-loss curve is calibrated to a single urban anchor (50-80% at 0.5 m) and may understate risk on small ridges that hold out after the shoreline and freshwater lens fail, or overstate it for the Maldives' 1192 coral atolls where Malé's sea walls are already planned; (ii) the 35% exhaustion and 15% onset thresholds are ICM-F-supplied assumptions, not derived — a 25/35 vs 35/45 shift moves the 2060 onset by a decade; (iii) the culture index's language-count weighting may mis-rank Kiribati (24 languages, ~130k speakers — a single dispersal could halve the language count within two generations regardless of policy) versus the Maldives (1 language, 526k speakers — the most robust culture in the set), so the index overstates Maldives' relative cultural fragility; (iv) the 30%/generation dispersal fade is an anchor, not a measured rate; (v) the ~2 m tail is a stress test, not a central estimate. Biases: the model is biased toward earlier 'at risk' classification (staged onset), which is the conservative choice for a policy clock; the scenario odds (0.2/0.5/0.3) favor the middle path, so the point estimates are not worst-case.

### Problem

Subtask 2 (proposed policies): propose international policies addressing EDPs in terms of both human rights (the right to resettle and to participate fully in life in the new home) and cultural preservation; specify which countries take which EDPs and on what obligation basis; and identify who should have a say in deciding where nationless EDPs make a new home (the individuals, an intergovernmental organization like the UN, or the absorbing states' governments). This is the ICM-F's second briefing requirement.

### Analysis

Approach: a rights-and-obligation policy package of five instruments, each derived from the scope model's outputs (the at-risk population by nation and year, the cultural-loss curve, and the host-capacity table) rather than asserted exogenously. The allocation design responds directly to the problem's question of whether 'worst offenders' (disproportionate historical and current greenhouse-gas contributors) should carry a higher obligation: the model quantifies an obligation weight as a 50/50 blend of cumulative historical emissions and current per-capita-adjusted contribution (US 0.190, EU 0.165, China 0.100, Japan 0.058), and the allocation rule combines it with geographic proximity. The 'who gets a say' question is answered by a tiered governance structure (EDP consent as a hard constraint, UN as the coordinating authority, host states as implementation partners with capped, not absolute, acceptance). Assumptions: EDPs retain full refugee-equivalent rights under the proposed UN instrument; integration cost is ~$250k per EDP over 10 years; cultural-protection programs (language education, community corridors, heritage documentation) are fundable at a fraction of the integration cost; and no host's acceptance is coerced — obligation is expressed as a binding financial and quota commitment in exchange for rights recognition.

### Modeling Process

Policy instruments (each a function of the scope model's outputs): (P1) EDP status and rights — a UN-recognized 'environmentally displaced person' status conferring refugee-equivalent rights (asylum, work, education, family reunification) in the host country, triggered by the scope model's exhaustion condition (nation-level habitability loss > 35% weighted). (P2) Obligation-weighted resettlement quotas — each host's share of the EDP flow = 0.5·proximity + 0.5·(normalized emission obligation), where obligation = 0.5·historical_emission_share + 0.5·current_per_capita_share. For the 2100 flow of 567,440 EDPs: India 40% (226,990), US 19% (106,577), EU 17% (97,887), China 13% (75,293), Japan 11% (60,693); proximate hosts (Australia, NZ, Fiji, Sri Lanka, Malaysia, Indonesia) take residual shares. Quotas are bounded by the cost-capacity constraint: quota <= 2%·GDP·(host_pop/host_pop)/($250k/EDP) i.e. 2% of GDP spent over the 10-year integration horizon; all allocations in the base plan fit inside cost-capacity (India's 226,990 is 84% of its 268,800 cost-capacity; the rest are far below theirs). (P3) Cultural-protection fund — a per-EDP levy (a fraction of the $250k integration cost) financing language-education and community-corridor programs in the host, modeled as the policy lever PE that reduces the dispersal fade from 0.3/generation to 0.3·(1-PE)/generation; the fund's size is set to achieve PE=0.5 as the baseline commitment (worth ~10 percentage points of cultural loss for Tuvalu). (P4) Pre-arranged migration corridors — bilateral agreements (e.g. Tuvalu-NZ, the existing Pacific precedent) that let a source nation negotiate its destination before the exhaustion trigger, reducing the administrative and cultural shock of forced relocation; modeled as reducing the effective dispersal fade for the corridor nation's cohort. (P5) Tiered governance — decision authority over destination is allocated as: (a) the EDP individuals hold a hard veto (informed consent is a precondition of any transfer); (b) the UN (ICM-F as secretariat) holds the coordinating and quota-allocation authority, applying the P2 rule; (c) host states hold implementation authority within their capped quota and may not refuse a cohort inside the quota without triggering the financial backstop (P6). (P6) Financial backstop and worst-offender surcharge — hosts that refuse a quota-bounded cohort pay a surcharge into a collective fund; the surcharge is weighted by the same emission-obligation metric, so the worst offenders finance both the quota and the refusal penalty. Solution procedure: evaluate P2's allocation for the 2060 (177,325), 2080 (212,790) and 2100 (567,440) flows; check each allocation against the population-mode (1% of host pop) and cost-mode (2% of GDP) capacities; compute the P3 fund size as the per-EDP levy times the flow at each milestone; and verify the tiered governance satisfies the consent constraint for every cohort.

### Outcome Analysis

The policy package is feasible in the base case: every 2100 allocation fits inside the host's cost-capacity (India at 84% is the binding constraint; US/EU/China/Japan are all under 8% of theirs), and the population-mode capacity (1% of host population) is exceeded only by India's cost-mode allocation, so the binding bound is cost, not numbers — which is the correct bound for a rights-based instrument. The obligation blend materially shifts the burden toward current emitters relative to a pure-history rule (China's share rises from 8.5% to 10% of the obligation metric; the US falls from 21% to 19%), which is a more defensible answer to the 'worst offenders' question than historical emissions alone. The cultural-protection fund is the highest-leverage instrument: the model shows a fully effective policy (PE=1) cuts Tuvalu's 2100 cultural loss from 26.4% to 5.2%, and even a partial policy (PE=0.5) halves the loss for the smaller nations; because the fund is a fraction of the integration cost, its marginal cost is small relative to the loss it averts. Limitations and biases: (i) the 50/50 proximity/obligation split and the 50/50 historical/current blend are judgment calls (the expert rejected the Round-2 trio, and these are the most defensible alternate reading, not validated values); (ii) assigning 40% of the Pacific EDP flow to India is a model artifact of India's 7% emission share plus 1.4B population — it is not validated for political feasibility, and a real ICM-F would likely route the Pacific flow through the proximate hosts (Australia, NZ, Fiji) with the obligation hosts carrying the financial backstop; (iii) the $250k/EDP integration cost is an anchor, and a 2x error in it halves the cost-capacity and would make India's allocation infeasible; (iv) the consent-veto governance is idealized — in practice a hard individual veto over a cohort transfer is administratively difficult and may be implemented as a strong preference with a UN mediation step; (v) the model does not capture host-country public opposition (refugee-hosting backlash), which could make the population-mode capacity, not the cost-mode, the binding constraint in some hosts. Biases: the policy package is biased toward an obligation-weighted (emissions-based) allocation, which is the more morally defensible but politically harder path; a pure-proximity package would be easier to adopt but would leave the 'worst offenders' question unanswered.

## Subtask 3: Subtask 3 (model development and policy impact): describe the development of a model used to measure the potential impac

### Problem

Subtask 3 (model development and policy impact): describe the development of a model used to measure the potential impact of the proposed policies, and explain how the model was used to design and/or improve the proposed policies. This is the ICM-F's third and fourth briefing requirements: the model itself, and the model-policy feedback loop.

### Analysis

Approach: the model was developed iteratively in three rounds, each round's output feeding the next round's design, so that the model is not a static calculator but the instrument by which the policies were designed and tested. Round 1 established the scope (who is at risk, when, and how much culture is at stake) and exposed the three parameters the ICM-F must supply (horizon/trigger, host-capacity anchor, cultural-value basis). Round 2 tested a first-generation policy trio (obligation allocation, cardinal culture index with fixed fade, single exhaustion threshold) against the expert's judgment, which rejected all three; the model was then re-parameterized (staged trigger, emission blend, policy-lever fade) and re-run, and the delta between the two runs is itself a policy-impact measurement (the staged trigger moves the policy clock 20 years earlier; the policy lever makes cultural loss a function of policy effectiveness instead of an exogenous decay). Round 3 stress-tested the re-parameterized model against the expert's counterexample (the freshwater lens), which broke the exposure mechanism; the model was re-built with the earliest-of trigger and re-run, and the 2080 delta (177,325 -> 212,790) is the measured impact of the counterexample on the scope. The same re-run also showed the policy lever's impact curve (PE = 0/0.5/1) across all four nations, which is the direct input to the P3 fund sizing. The model was used to design the policies in two specific ways: (a) the P3 fund size is set to the PE that achieves a 50% reduction in the worst-case cultural loss (Tuvalu), not an arbitrary budget; (b) the P2 quota bound is set to the cost-capacity (2% of GDP) rather than the population-burden (1% of population) because the model showed cost, not numbers, is the binding constraint for the obligation hosts.

### Modeling Process

Model development as a three-iteration loop: M_0 -> [expert judgment R2] -> M_1 -> [expert counterexample R3] -> M_2, where each M_i is the parameterized scope/culture/host model (code/model_scope.py) and the arrows are the re-parameterizations. Impact measurement: for each policy instrument p, Impact(p) = [metric(M_2 with p active) - metric(M_2 with p inactive)], where metric is the at-risk population (for the trigger), the cultural-loss % (for the policy lever), or the quota feasibility (for the obligation blend). Concretely: (i) Staged-trigger impact: AtRisk(2060 | staged) - AtRisk(2060 | single 35% threshold) = 177,325 - 0 = +177,325 people brought into the policy clock 20 years earlier; AtRisk(2080 | staged) = 177,325 vs 212,790 under the freshwater trigger (the staged trigger alone, before R3, gave 177,325). (ii) Freshwater-trigger impact: AtRisk(2080 | earliest-of) - AtRisk(2080 | land-only) = 212,790 - 177,325 = +35,465 (a +20% upward revision of the at-risk population, concentrated in the Pacific atolls). (iii) Policy-lever impact (cultural loss, 2100): Loss(Tuvalu | PE=0) = 26.4%; Loss(Tuvalu | PE=0.5) = 17.0%; Loss(Tuvalu | PE=1) = 5.2% — a 21.2-point reduction from full policy, of which the first 9.4 points (PE 0 -> 0.5) are the fund's baseline commitment. (iv) Obligation-blend impact: China's allocation weight under historical-only = 0.085/(sum) vs under the 50/50 blend = 0.100/(sum); the blend raises China's share by ~1.7 points and lowers the US share by ~2 points, moving the plan toward current emitters. (v) Quota-feasibility impact: under cost-capacity, all 2100 allocations are feasible (max utilization India 84%); under population-capacity (1% of host pop), India's allocation (226,990) exceeds 1% of its own population only if the EDP flow is attributed to India's population, so the binding bound is cost — this is why P2 uses the cost-capacity as the quota ceiling. The loop closed when the R3 counterexample was incorporated and the model re-run: no further counterexample was requested (the consultation is exhausted at 3 rounds), and the remaining two R3 candidates (India allocation feasibility; Kiribati language-count mis-ranking) are recorded as model limitations rather than re-engineered, since the consultation bound is reached.

### Outcome Analysis

The model-policy feedback loop worked in both directions the ICM-F asked for: the model was used to design the policies (P3 fund size from the PE impact curve; P2 quota bound from the cost-vs-population capacity comparison), and the policies were used to re-parameterize the model (the policy lever PE is the model's representation of P3; the staged trigger is the model's representation of the earlier policy clock). The measured impacts are concrete and defensible: the staged trigger brings 177,325 people into the policy clock by 2060 instead of 2080 (a 20-year advance); the freshwater trigger revises the 2080 at-risk population up by 20% (+35,465); the policy lever is worth 21.2 points of cultural loss for Tuvalu at full effectiveness, with the baseline fund (PE=0.5) capturing 9.4 of those points. Limitations and biases: (i) the impact measurements are differences of model outputs, not of observed outcomes — the model's own parameter uncertainty (thresholds, fade rate, integration cost) propagates into the impact numbers, so a 2x error in the $250k cost or a 10-point shift in the thresholds would change the P2 feasibility and the P3 fund size; (ii) the three-iteration loop was bounded by the three-round consultation, so the model was stress-tested by exactly one counterexample (the freshwater lens) and one judgment rejection (the R2 trio); a fourth round might have broken the allocation mechanism (India 40%) or the culture index (Kiribati language count) as the R3 question anticipated, and those are recorded as limitations, not resolved; (iii) the impact of P4 (pre-arranged corridors) and P5 (tiered governance) is not separately quantified in the model — P4 is represented only as a reduced dispersal fade for the corridor nation, and P5 is a governance structure the model does not simulate; (iv) the loop is biased toward the parameters the expert accepted or did not reject — the freshwater trigger and the staged onset are now load-bearing, and a future expert could break them in turn; (v) the model does not measure the impact of the policies on the host countries' own populations (labor-market, housing, social-cohesion effects), which is the largest unmodeled side of the impact question.

## Subtask 4: Subtask 4 (importance of implementation and implications): explain, backed by the analysis, the importance of implementi

### Problem

Subtask 4 (importance of implementation and implications): explain, backed by the analysis, the importance of implementing the proposed policies, and state the implications of accepting or rejecting them. This is the ICM-F's fifth briefing requirement: the case for action and the cost of inaction, both grounded in the model's outputs.

### Analysis

Approach: the importance of implementation is argued from the model's three time-scale findings — the scope finding (567,440 people at risk by 2100, 177,325 already in the policy clock by 2060), the culture finding (26.4% irreversible loss for Tuvalu without policy, reducible to 5.2% with a fully effective policy), and the feasibility finding (every 2100 allocation fits inside host cost-capacity in the base case). The implications of acceptance and rejection are stated symmetrically: acceptance commits the international community to a bounded, financeable obligation (the P2 quotas and P3 fund) in exchange for rights recognition and cultural preservation; rejection leaves the 567,440 EDPs without a legal status, a funding stream, or a destination, and the culture loss becomes irreversible on a 30-year generation timescale. The asymmetry is the argument: the cost of action is bounded (2% of GDP for the obligation hosts, a fraction of that for the cultural fund) and recoverable in the form of the EDPs' economic contribution over the integration horizon, while the cost of inaction is unbounded (statelessness, irreversible cultural extinction, and the political cost of an unmanaged cross-border flow that will occur regardless of the policy, since the ~0.18-0.23 m near-term sea-level rise is committed across all scenarios). Assumptions: the EDPs' economic contribution over the 10-year integration horizon partially offsets the $250k per-EDP cost (a standard refugee-economics result, not modeled in detail); the committed near-term rise (0.18-0.23 m by 2050) will produce a de-facto EDP flow even without the formal policy; and the political cost of an unmanaged flow exceeds the financial cost of the managed one.

### Modeling Process

Case-for-action as a comparison of two model runs: (A) acceptance — the M_2 model with P2 quotas active, P3 fund at PE=0.5, P4 corridors for the Pacific nations, P5 tiered governance, and P6 financial backstop. Outcomes: 567,440 EDPs resettled by 2100 under a rights-based status; cultural loss bounded at 17.0% (Tuvalu), 4.2% (Kiribati), 0% (Marshall Islands), 0% (Maldives) at PE=0.5; total host financial commitment = sum over hosts of quota·$250k = 567,440·$250k = $141.9B over 10 years, of which the P3 fund is a fraction (set to achieve PE=0.5, ~$5B/yr order of magnitude as a share of the obligation hosts' GDP); all quotas inside cost-capacity (max utilization India 84%). (B) rejection — the M_2 model with P2-P6 inactive: the same 567,440 EDPs are at risk (the exposure is physical, not policy-dependent) but have no legal status, no funded destination, and no cultural-protection program; cultural loss runs to 26.4% (Tuvalu), 15.4% (Marshall Islands), 11.5% (Maldives), 5.0% (Kiribati) by 2100; the de-facto flow still occurs (the committed near-term rise of 0.18-0.23 m by 2050 produces displacement regardless), but unmanaged — i.e. the EDPs move without rights, without funding, and with the full 30%/generation dispersal fade. The delta (A) - (B): cultural loss averted = 9.4 points (Tuvalu), 15.4 points (Marshall Islands), 11.5 points (Maldives); the financial commitment = $141.9B over 10 years (0.03% of the obligation hosts' combined GDP over the horizon); the political cost avoided = the unmanaged-flow cost, which the model does not quantify but which the Groundswell reconciliation (143M internal migrants by 2050) suggests is the dominant unmodeled risk. The asymmetry ratio: bounded cost of action ($141.9B, 0.03% of host GDP) vs unbounded cost of inaction (statelessness + irreversible culture loss + unmanaged flow). Solution procedure: run M_2 under both policy configurations, aggregate the outcomes, and state the delta; the comparison is a single model run with the policy instruments toggled, not a new model.

### Outcome Analysis

The analysis shows implementation matters on three scales. On the human-rights scale, acceptance is the difference between 567,440 people having a legal status, a funded destination, and full participation rights in their new home, and the same 567,440 people being stateless with no destination — the physical exposure is identical in both runs, so the policy is the sole determinant of the rights outcome. On the cultural scale, acceptance (PE=0.5 fund) averts 9.4 points of Tuvalu's irreversible loss (26.4% -> 17.0%) and 15.4 points for the Marshall Islands (15.4% -> 0%), where a language with no speakers after dispersal is extinct on a 30-year generation timescale and cannot be recovered by any later policy. On the financial scale, the cost of action is bounded and small relative to the obligation hosts' capacity ($141.9B over 10 years is 0.03% of their combined GDP), while the cost of inaction is unbounded because it includes the unmanaged-flow political cost and the irreversible cultural loss, neither of which has a price tag the model can assign. Implications of acceptance: the international community assumes a binding, emissions-weighted obligation (the P2 quotas) that is financeable in the base case but binds India at 84% of cost-capacity, making India's participation the single point of failure in the plan; it commits the P3 fund, which is the highest-leverage instrument (9.4 points of Tuvalu loss averted for a small fraction of the integration cost); and it establishes the tiered governance (P5) that gives EDPs a consent veto, which is the most politically difficult element and the one most likely to be watered down in negotiation. Implications of rejection: the 567,440 EDPs remain at risk with no legal recourse; the cultural loss runs to its no-policy value (26.4% Tuvalu by 2100); the de-facto unmanaged flow still occurs (the near-term rise is committed), so the world pays the political cost of the flow without the rights and cultural benefits of the managed one; and the 'worst offenders' question goes unanswered, which is a credibility loss for the UN's climate-migration mandate. Limitations and biases: (i) the $141.9B figure assumes the full 567,440 flow is absorbed by 2100; a slower, staggered flow (more realistic) would spread the cost and reduce the peak-year burden but extend the cultural-loss window; (ii) the '0.03% of host GDP' is a ratio, not a budget impact — the relevant comparison is against each host's annual climate or development budget, not total GDP, and on that scale the commitment is larger; (iii) the model does not quantify the unmanaged-flow political cost, so the asymmetry argument rests on the bounded-vs-unbounded structure rather than a measured cost-of-inaction; (iv) the analysis is biased toward acceptance because the rejection case inherits the model's own parameter uncertainty, whereas the acceptance case is partially hedged by the cost-capacity feasibility check; (v) the 567,440 figure is the probability-weighted AR6 central estimate, and the ~2 m tail (709,300) would raise both the cost of action and the cost of inaction by ~25%, preserving the asymmetry but enlarging the absolute stakes.
