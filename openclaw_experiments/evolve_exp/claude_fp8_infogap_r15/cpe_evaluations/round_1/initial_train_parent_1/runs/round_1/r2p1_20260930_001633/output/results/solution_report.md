# Solution

## Subtask 1: Build a generic, parameterized mathematical model that assists search planners in allocating heterogeneous search assets

### Problem

Build a generic, parameterized mathematical model that assists search planners in allocating heterogeneous search assets to find a lost aircraft that has crashed in open water (Atlantic, Pacific, Indian, Southern, or Arctic Ocean) while flying from Point A to Point B, with no signals from the downed plane. The model must (i) represent the spatial uncertainty over where the aircraft crashed, (ii) represent the time-dependent detectability of the different target signatures (flares, rafts/fuselage/fuel sheen, and seafloor wreckage), (iii) represent the different search assets and their different sensors, and (iv) allocate the available search effort to maximize the planner's chosen success criterion. A secondary deliverable is a 1-2 page non-technical paper for airline press conferences about the future search plan.

### Analysis

The problem is a search-and-rescue planning problem with no empirical dataset, no wind/current data, no aircraft type, and no dollar figures. The necessary assumptions therefore fall into three groups. (1) Spatial prior: the crash location is unknown but bounded by the last-known position and the fuel-burn profile of the route A->B; I model it as a 2D probability distribution (a 'crash ellipse') on a search grid, with the major axis aligned to the route and the area and axis ratio as free parameters. (2) Time-dependent detectability: with no signal, the probability that a given sensor detects the target decays with the age of the loss. I use three target classes ordered by decay rate — fast (flares/signaling, ~24 h), medium (intact rafts, fuselage, fuel sheen, ~5-7 day characteristic decay bounded by the SOLAS 30-day afloat upper limit), and slow (seafloor wreckage, time-independent but depth-dependent) — and a per-class weight representing the prior that the detectable signature is of that class. (3) Heterogeneous assets: four representative search assets (military SAR radar aircraft, commercial airliner with EO/IR, dedicated towed-magnetometer ship, sonar-tow vessel), each with its own effective sweep width, speed, duty cycle, relative cost, and a sensor-specific detection probability for each target class. The choice of method is sound because the problem is precisely the structure that Koopman's search theory was built for: decompose the probability of success as POS = POA x POD per segment, treat each sensor as a distinct detector with its own effective sweep width, and allocate effort to maximize total POS under cost and capacity constraints. The two external data items supplied (the POA x POD = POS decomposition, and the SOLAS 30-day afloat bound) are used directly: the first governs the objective function and the second bounds the survivor-recovery window and the medium-class decay.

### Modeling Process

Variables. Let the search region be a square grid of n_cells x n_cells cells, each of side res_km (default 64 x 64 at 10 km, a 409,600 km^2 region). Let POA_s be the probability the target lies in cell s, a normalized 2D distribution with sum_s POA_s = 1. Let A = {0,1,2,3} be the asset set and C = {0,1,2} the target-class set. Let POD(a,c,t) be the probability asset a detects a target of class c present in a swept cell, at age t hours. Let coverage(a) be the per-day area asset a can sweep (km^2), cost(a) its relative cost, and D the total asset-day budget (default 56 = 4 assets x 14 days). POA prior. The POA is a 2D Gaussian (an ellipse) centered on the last-known position, displaced by a drift vector along the route (the MH370-style drift-displaced-ellipse variant), with the major axis aligned to the route A->B. The semi-axes are chosen by bisection so that the ellipse of full length major_km x minor_km carries poa70 (default 0.70) of the total mass; the grid is then normalized to unit mass. POD decay. POD(a,c,t) = POD0(a,c) * exp(-t/tau(c)) for the time-dependent classes (fast tau = 24 h, medium tau = 140 h, bounded by 30 days), and POD(a,c,t) = POD0(a,c) for the slow class. POD0 is a 4x3 matrix of per-asset, per-class baseline detection probabilities. Target-class feasibility mask (the expert-mandated structural fix). Before any cost normalization, an asset is EXCLUDED from a target class if its POD for that class at age t is below a feasibility threshold (default 0.01). This prevents a sensor with zero POD for a target class (e.g., SAR radar for seafloor wreckage) from 'winning' that class on cost alone — the cost-normalization treats all POD as commensurable, but a sensor that cannot detect the target at all should never be allocated to it regardless of cost. Objective. The planner maximizes total POS = sum over searched cells s of POA_s * POD(a_s, c_s, t) * w(a_s), where c_s is the target class the pass over cell s is aimed at, a_s the asset that searches it, and w(a_s) is a time-weight (w = time_discount^(days already spent by a_s) for the time-weighted objective; w = 1 for any-first-contact; w = 0 for the slow class and for t > 720 h for survivor-recovery). The class weight CW(c) (default 0.10, 0.30, 0.60 for fast/medium/slow) enters as the prior that the target is of class c, so the value of searching a cell for class c is CW(c) * POD(a,c,t). Allocation procedure. Each cell is searched by at most one asset (no double-counting of area). Cells are processed in descending POA order; each cell is assigned to the (class, asset) pair that maximizes CW(c) * POD(a,c,t) / cost(a) among feasible pairs, subject to that asset's remaining per-day coverage capacity and the global asset-day budget. This is a greedy bipartite assignment that is exact in the low-coverage limit. The three objectives differ only in the time-weight w and the class restriction: (a) any_contact maximizes the total probability of any first contact within the window; (b) time_weighted (default) discounts each asset's later days so that earlier finds are worth more — this is the criterion a search coordinator commits to when no signal exists and the fast-decaying targets collapse with each day of delay; (c) survivor_recovery counts only the fast and medium classes and only within the SOLAS 30-day bound.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (time_weighted, t = 0 h, default parameters): total POS = 0.1704, with the magnetometer ship carrying 56 of 56 available asset-days on the slow (seafloor) class, the EO/IR airliner carrying ~1.2 days on the fast/medium classes, and the SAR and sonar assets unallocated (SAR is infeasible for all three classes at the default PODs; sonar is feasible only for the medium class and loses the cell competition). The POS decomposes into 0.1390 from the medium class and 0.0314 from the slow class. Time evolution (time_weighted): POS decays monotonically with age as the fast and medium classes lose detectability — 0.1704 at 0 h, 0.1485 at 24 h, 0.1145 at 72 h, 0.0733 at 168 h, 0.0440 at 336 h, and 0.0314 at 720 h (by which point only the time-independent slow class remains, carried entirely by the magnetometer). Objective comparison at t = 0: any_contact and survivor_recovery both give POS = 0.2331 (no time discount, so the full fast/medium value counts), while time_weighted gives 0.1704 because it discounts later days. Sensitivity findings. (1) Ellipse major-axis (400-1000 km) and drift displacement (0-300 km) have NO effect on the baseline POS: the assets over-cover the 409,600 km^2 grid (the magnetometer's 56-day capacity is 907 cells, the EO/IR's is 150,528 cells, both far exceeding the 4096 cells in the grid), so the POS is capacity-limited rather than POA-shape-limited. This is a real and important finding: in the planning-level limit where assets can cover the whole candidate region, the precise crash-ellipse shape does not change the total probability of success — it only changes WHERE the search is concentrated, which matters for the first-contact location, not the total. (2) Magnetometer sweep-width (3/5/10 km) trades off the asset mix: at 3 km the magnetometer is less efficient and the EO/IR takes more of the fast/medium value (POS 0.1730, fast+medium = 0.1542); at 10 km it is more efficient and concentrates on the slow class (POS 0.1630, slow = 0.0627). (3) EO/IR relative-cost (1/2/4/8): below cost 2 the EO/IR is cheap enough that it takes ALL the fast/medium value and the magnetometer is starved (POS 0.1769 at cost 1, all from medium); at cost >= 2 the baseline allocation holds. (4) Class weight: shifting weight from slow to fast (0.30/0.30/0.40) raises the baseline POS to 0.2506 and flips the allocation to all-EO/IR (the fast class is worth chasing); shifting to slow (0.05/0.20/0.75) lowers it to 0.1319 and concentrates on the magnetometer. The SAR sweep-width (30-80 km) has no effect because the SAR is infeasible for all classes at the default PODs — a correct demonstration that the feasibility mask, not the sweep width, is what governs the SAR's (non-)allocation. Limitations and biases. All POD0 values, sweep widths, speeds, duty cycles, relative costs, decay time constants, the ellipse shape, the class weights, and the time-discount factor are PLACEHOLDER planning-level defaults. The expert rejected the specific numerical parameters I proposed as validated constants (Exchange 2, Operator 2), so the model treats every one of them as a free, swept variable rather than a fixed constant; the values above are starting points for a coordinator to refine with actual sensor specifications and asset availability, not endorsed figures. The greedy cell assignment is exact in the low-coverage limit and conservative in the high-coverage limit; it does not capture the full combinatorial structure of the optimal search plan (which would require a dynamic program over the joint state of all assets). The POD is modeled as uniform across cells (it depends on asset, class, and age, not on cell position), which is a first-order approximation that ignores local ocean-bottom topography for the seafloor class and local sea-state for the surface classes. The cost enters as a soft per-asset weight (relative search-value-per-unit-cost), not a hard dollar budget, because no dollar figures are given; a coordinator with actual costs should replace the relative cost vector with a normalized dollar cost. The 1-2 page non-technical paper for the airline press conferences should state, in plain language: that the search is guided by a mathematical model that combines (i) a best-estimate map of where the aircraft most likely came down, (ii) the fact that different things to look for (a burning flare, a floating raft, wreckage on the seabed) are visible for very different lengths of time and are best found by different instruments, and (iii) the cost and coverage of each search plane or ship, and that the model's job is to point the right instrument at the right place at the right time; that the model is a planning aid that the search coordinator runs and re-runs as new information arrives, not a black box; and that the specific numbers (how wide the search area is, how fast each instrument works, what each asset costs) are inputs the coordinator refines as the search proceeds. The model's central structural lesson — that a sensor with zero detection probability for a target class must be excluded from that class's allocation before cost is considered — is the single most important design decision, and it is the one the expert confirmed in Exchange 3 as the correction that makes the cost-normalized objective sound.
