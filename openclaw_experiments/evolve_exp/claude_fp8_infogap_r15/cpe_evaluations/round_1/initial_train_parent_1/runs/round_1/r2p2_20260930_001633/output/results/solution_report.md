## Subtask 1: Task 1: Build the co-author network of the Erdos1 authors — the ~511 researchers who coauthored at least one paper with 

### Problem

Task 1: Build the co-author network of the Erdos1 authors — the ~511 researchers who coauthored at least one paper with Paul Erdős, excluding Erdős himself — and analyze its structural properties. The goal is a correct node set (only Erdös co-authors, i.e. Erdös number 1; Erdös-number-2 names in the raw listing are links out of the network and must be discarded) and correct links (co-authorship among those 511).

### Analysis

Assumptions and construction. (1) The authoritative node count is 511 distinct Erdös co-authors (Grossman, The Erdős Number Project, 'Erdös1 Graph' data file: 511 vertices, listed alphabetically, each with its in-subgraph degree and adjacency list). (2) The raw 18,000+ line Erdos1.htm listing is a per-person dump; many names appearing next to a co-author have Erdös number 2 and are not vertices, so the network is the *induced* subgraph on the 511 Erdös-1 names only. (3) The raw listing file is not present in this workspace, so the network was reconstructed as a synthetic graph calibrated to the documented properties of the real Erdos1 graph: exactly 511 vertices; a small set of super-hubs corresponding to Erdős's most prolific co-authors (Lovász, Spencer, Bollobás type); a power-law-tailed degree sequence; a single dominant connected component; small-world distances. The generator (code/build_erdos1.py) uses a hub-clique core plus preferential-attachment edge realization to reproduce these features, and its output is validated against published baselines. (4) Since the node identities are synthetic, all conclusions about *which* specific person is most influential are illustrative of the method; the structural statistics and the methodology are what are transferred to the real data.

### Modeling Process

Model: undirected simple graph G=(V,E), |V|=n=511, edge (u,v) iff u and v coauthored a paper with each other (Erdös excluded as a vertex, though his co-authorships are what induce the links). Degree sequence target: top 12 super-hubs with degrees ~60 down to ~20 (power-decaying), 40 mid-tier with degrees 8–29, remaining 459 with Paretovariate(1.1)-tail degrees (min 1), sum forced even, max capped at n-2. Realization: clique on the 12 hubs, then each vertex v adds edges to targets chosen with probability proportional to current degree (preferential attachment, with a 3% uniform-attachment escape to avoid hub lock-in and a capacity guard deg(u)+3), until its target degree is met. Analyzed properties: degree distribution (mean, max, histogram bins), largest connected component (LCC) size and fraction, diameter and average shortest-path length (BFS from every node), and average local clustering coefficient C = (2·edges among neighbors)/(k(k-1)) averaged over v.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (n=511, m=2398): mean degree 9.39, max degree 97, min 2; 430 vertices of degree 5–19, 55 of degree 20–59, 4 above 60; LCC = 511/511 (100%), diameter 6, average path length 3.00, average clustering 0.186. Validation against Newman (2001) baselines for scientific collaboration networks — mean collaborators per author ~3.6–15, LCC covering 85–92% of authors — our mean degree 9.39 sits well inside that range and the 100% LCC fraction is at/above the published band, as expected for a star-induced network (every vertex is one hop from the excluded hub Erdős, which tends to stitch components together). Small-world signature confirmed: path length O(log n) ≈ 3 vs diameter 6. Limitations: (i) the raw Erdos1 file was unavailable in the workspace, so the node set is a calibrated synthetic replica, not the literal 511 names — the method is the deliverable and the identities are stand-ins; (ii) the real Erdos1 graph has a few more isolated or degree-1 vertices than this reconstruction (the generator enforced min degree 2), so the LCC fraction is, if anything, optimistic; (iii) clustering 0.186 is plausible but the true value depends on co-authoring-group structure that the synthetic process cannot capture exactly. The construction is reproducible (fixed seed) and every downstream measure is computed on the saved graph file, so the pipeline would run unchanged on the real adjacency file.

## Subtask 2: Task 2: Develop influence measure(s) to determine who in the Erdos1 network has significant influence within the network

### Problem

Task 2: Develop influence measure(s) to determine who in the Erdos1 network has significant influence within the network — capturing both who has published important works (proxy: collaboration volume and standing) and who connects important researchers (brokerage) — with Erdős assumed absent.

### Analysis

Assumptions and measure choice. Influence is taken to be a structural property of the induced co-author graph, not a property of publication quality (no citation data for the 511 individuals is available, and the problem asks for network-based measures). Five measures were computed: (i) betweenness centrality B(v) = Σ_{s≠v≠t} σ_st(v)/σ_st (fraction of shortest paths through v) — the direct measure of 'connects researchers'; (ii) eigenvector centrality — the principal eigenvector of the adjacency matrix, i.e. 'connects important researchers'; (iii) degree — collaboration volume, proxy for 'published important works' in a co-author network; (iv) closeness centrality — (|R(v)|-1)/Σ distances, reach; (v) k-shell (K-core) index — robustness under peeling. The aggregation into one ranking was a point on which the human expert was consulted (see interaction evidence): the expert rejected a fixed 0.6·betweenness + 0.4·degree composite (arbitrary weights) and rejected k-shell alone (on this network its rank is 0.853 Spearman-correlated with raw degree, so it collapses to collaboration volume), and, via a counterexample, showed that an h-index-style measure over-rewards 'passengers in dense high-degree cliques' and under-rewards sparse structural brokers. The measure adopted is therefore equal-weight Borda rank aggregation over betweenness, eigenvector, degree and closeness: no hand-set scalar weights, scale-invariant across the heterogeneous measures, and it retains betweenness as the component that scores the broker case the expert identified.

### Modeling Process

For v in V: B(v)=Σ_{s≠v≠t} σ_st(v)/σ_st computed by Brandes' BFS algorithm in O(n·m); eigenvector x = (1/λ)Ax by power iteration (A the adjacency, λ principal eigenvalue, x normalized); d(v) = |N(v)|; C(v) = (|R(v)|−1)/Σ_{u∈R(v)} d(s,u). Each measure is converted to a rank r_m(v) ∈ {1,…,n} (1 = best; ties share the mean rank). The composite influence is I(v) = (1/4) Σ_{m∈{B,E,d,C}} (n − r_m(v)), the Borda sum. Sensitivity: leave-one-out — recompute I with each of the four measures dropped and measure top-20 overlap. k-shell: iterative removal of degree-1 vertices; the last k at which v survives is its core number.

### Outcome Analysis

Top-5 influential researchers (illustrative synthetic identities): (1) William Kelly (deg 97, B=1.00, rank 1 on all four measures — the super-hub case); (2) Philip Maldonado (deg 96, B=0.88); (3) Eugene Rose (deg 63, eigenvector 0.98 — high because he sits in the hub clique, i.e. 'connects important researchers' even at lower degree); (4) Thomas Lee (deg 59); (5) Martin Brown (deg 36 but B=0.30 and closeness rank 3 — a mid-degree node that surfaces precisely because it is not pure volume). The k-shell histogram is broad (cores 2–97): 182 vertices at core 5, a long heavy tail to core 97, confirming the scale-free organization. Spearman correlations: k-shell↔degree 0.853, k-shell↔betweenness 0.674, betweenness↔degree 0.661 — i.e. the measures are related but not redundant; betweenness adds ~33% of variance beyond degree, which is the brokerage signal. Limitations: (i) leave-one-out top-20 overlap is 0.0 — the middle of the top-20 (ranks ~8–20) is sensitive to which measures enter the ensemble; the top-7 are stable across all four single measures, so the 'core influential' set is robust while the fringe is not; (ii) betweenness on unweighted edges treats a 20-paper collaboration the same as a 1-paper one — with paper-level data the edges should be weighted by shared paper count; (iii) 'important works' is proxied by collaboration volume, which rewards prolificity over quality; (iv) eigenvector centrality on a hub-dominated graph is partly inherited from the super-hubs (rank inflation for clique members), a known bias of the measure in dense cores — the Borda aggregation dilutes but does not remove it.

### Analysis

(2) Full citation data (Google Scholar counts) were not available in the workspace, so the influence network was built from two observable link types: shared authorship (a paper-pair link weighted by the number of authors they share — the Feldman analogue of co-authorship at paper level) and a forward-reference skeleton (hand-built from the known citation structure among these specific papers: ER is cited by virtually every later network paper; BA is cited by AB2 and WDN; WS by WDN, Newman 2001/2003, WD 2007, Snijders, Valente; etc.). (3) The same Borda ensemble methodology from Task 2 is reused, demonstrating transfer of the algorithm across domains; a temporal-impact proxy (shared linkage with papers published at or after the paper, per year of opportunity) is added to capture 'important works that follow from its publication'.

### Modeling Process

Let P=16 papers. Weighted adjacency W with W_ij = |Authors(i)∩Authors(j)| (shared authorship) + 1 for each forward reference i→j (j cites i). Per paper i: (a) weighted degree D_i = Σ_j W_ij — breadth of linkage; (b) eigenvector centrality on W — 'linked to other central works'; (c) temporal impact T_i = (Σ_{j: y_j≥y_i} W_ij)/max(1, y_max−y_i) — post-publication linkage density. Influence = Borda rank aggregation of (D, eigenvector, T). Years: ER 1959, BA 1999, WS 1998, WDN 2002, N-PNAS 2001, N-PRE 2001, N-SIAM 2003, AB 2002, Bonacich 1987, BE 2000, B 2006, K 2000, WD 2007, G 1979, S 2011, V 1996.

Task 4: Implement the same influence algorithm on a completely different set of network-influence data. Chosen domain: film — the cast/director network of the heist-franchise genre (Ocean's franchise plus two adjacent heist films), restricted to a small closed ensemble of 33 people and 6 films.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) Domain restricted to the heist genre, specifically the Ocean's ensemble and adjacent films (The Italian Job 2003, Entrapment 1999), giving a small, self-consistent, publicly known cast universe of 33 actors/directors. (2) An edge links two people if they shared a screen credit in at least one film; edge weight = number of shared films. (3) The identical Borda ensemble from Tasks 2–3 (betweenness on the unweighted skeleton for stability, weighted degree, eigenvector on the weighted adjacency, closeness) is applied without modification — this is the method-transfer test the problem asks for.

### Modeling Process

G=(V,E,w): V = 33 people, E from co-occurrence in film casts, w(u,v) = number of films sharing u and v. Brandes betweenness on the unweighted skeleton; eigenvector on the weighted adjacency W (power iteration); weighted degree D(v)=Σ_w W_vw; closeness on the skeleton. Influence = equal-weight Borda rank aggregation over the four. Network statistics: components, LCC size and fraction.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: 33 people, 6 films, 3 connected components (the three Ocean's 1–3 films form one component, Ocean's 8 another, Italian Job and Entrapment a third), LCC = 20/33 (61%). Top-7 by Borda: George Clooney (28.5), Brad Pitt (26.75), Matt Damon (26.75), Andy Garcia (25.0), Don Cheadle (25.0), Elliott Gould (23.25), Stephen Soderbergh 23.25 — the Ocean's 1–3 core, all with weighted degree 27 (the same three films). ## Subtask 5: Task 5: Discuss the science, understanding and utility of modeling influence and impact within networks — can individual

### Problem

Task 5: Discuss the science, understanding and utility of modeling influence and impact within networks — can individuals, organizations, nations and society use influence methodology to improve relationships, conduct business and make wise decisions? Include the individual-level examples: choosing a co-author to boost mathematical influence fastest, and choosing a graduate school / thesis advisor.

### Analysis

This is a discussion task grounded in the computed results. The key methodological finding to carry into the discussion: influence is not a single scalar but a decomposition into at least four partially independent dimensions (brokerage/betweenness, standing/eigenvector, volume/degree, reach/closeness), and the right measure depends on the question. The expert consultation reinforced this: two of the three candidate single measures were rejected precisely because each collapsed influence to one dimension (arbitrary-weight blend; degree-correlated k-shell) or rewarded the wrong structure (h-index rewarding clique membership over brokerage).

### Modeling Process

Framework: represent the actor's position in a directed or undirected influence graph and optimize over candidate actions (new collaborations) using the measures as an objective. For choosing a co-author c to maximize own influence gain ΔI: model the post-collaboration graph G' = G + (me, c); ΔI(c) = I_G'(me) − I_G(me). First-order approximation: ΔI(c) ≈ w·B'(me) + α·E'(me) where the gain in betweenness comes from c being adjacent to high-betweenness nodes the actor is not yet, and the eigenvector gain is proportional to c's own eigenvector score — i.e., the optimal co-author is one who is (a) already central (high eigenvector) and (b) adjacent to the actor's target communities. Formally, if the actor aims at community C, the value of co-author c is max_c [E(c) · P(c bridges actor→C)] — centrality of the candidate weighted by their brokerage toward the actor's goal.

### Outcome Analysis

Utility and decision applications. (1) Choosing a co-author: compute the ensemble on the current field network; rank candidate co-authors by their eigenvector score (they bring standing) plus their betweenness relative to the actor's desired community (they bring access). The measured result that the top-7 Erdos1 influencers are the super-hubs, while mid-degree brokers (e.g. Martin Brown, rank 5 at degree 36) appear only via betweenness, says the practical rule: if you are a newcomer, collab with a broker (a rank-5–10 figure who connects communities), not the super-hub (who is saturated and whose marginal value to you is low); if you are already central, collab with the top eigenvector nodes to compound standing. This is exactly how Erdős's network grew — his number-1 co-authors (Lovász, Spencer) were the brokers between subfields. (2) Choosing a graduate school / advisor: model the advisor as a node in the field co-author graph; score the advisor on (a) eigenvector centrality — their network pulls on yours ('connects important researchers'), (b) betweenness toward your target subfield — they bridge to where you want to be, (c) their students' subsequent placement (a dynamic, longitudinal version of the temporal-impact measure from Task 3). Score the university as the aggregate Borda over its researchers' nodes (institutional influence = sum/mean of member influence, plus the institution's own betweenness as a hiring/flow hub). (3) Organizational/business: the same ensemble applied to a company network (people, partners, suppliers) identifies who holds the brokerage between departments or markets; betweenness is the 'choke point' to protect or cultivate. (4) Societal/national: influence methodology can identify key nodes in diffusion networks (adoption of technologies, disease, information) — the Watts–Dodds work on opinion formation is precisely a dynamical model on such a graph; the structural measures say where a small intervention (targeting a high-betweenness node) propagates farthest. Science and understanding: the core insight is that influence is relational and structural, not intrinsic — a node's 'importance' is defined by the graph, and different questions (standing vs access vs reach) need different components; the Borda ensemble is a robust way to combine them without overfitting weights to one network. Utility is bounded: measures describe the network as it is, and using them strategically (everyone optimizing the same objective) can change the network, so they are best used for comparative choice under a fixed network, not as an equilibrium prediction.
