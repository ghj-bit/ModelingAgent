### Problem

Subproblem 1 — Build a passing network for the Huskies in which each player is a node and each completed pass is a directed weighted link, and identify its structural patterns (dyadic and triadic configurations, team formations) and network properties across multiple scales: micro (pairwise/triadic) to macro (whole team, whole season), and short (minute-to-minute, half) to long (game, season) time scales. The goal is a reproducible network representation of the 23,429 passes among 366 players (30 Huskies, 336 opponents) over 38 matches, together with the structural indicators a coach can read off it.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (a) The passing network is built from completed passes only — the dataset records only successful passes, so the network is a network of realized coordination, not of attempts; the expert (Exchange 1) confirmed no completion-rate denominator exists in the data, so completion rate is treated as an out-of-scope assumption, not a parameter. (b) Nodes are individual player IDs (the 30 Huskies players; opponent players form a separate subgraph per match). (c) A link's weight is the number of passes between two players; for small-world and clustering tests the standard practice (per the literature baseline in external_data.md, Items 1-2) is to use the underlying undirected graph, because clustering and characteristic path length are defined on undirected networks. (d) Positions (x,y) are in attacking-team-normalized coordinates in [0,100], so x increases toward the opponent goal; a player's 'position on the field' is the degree-weighted mean of the origins of that player's passes. Method and why it is sound. The network is a weighted directed graph G=(V,E) with 30 Huskies nodes per season (and per match) and E the set of player-pairs that exchanged at least one pass. NetworkX computes: node degree (in/out), network density, average clustering coefficient, average shortest-path length, degree heterogeneity (std/mean of degree), betweenness centrality (weighted, normalized), and the spatial indicators degree-weighted centroid (x,y), team stretch (mean minus min of x), and the forward pass share. Dyadic and triadic patterns are read from the same graph: dyads are the weighted edges (reciprocity = whether i->j and j->i both exist), and triads are counted through the clustering coefficient, which is exactly the density of closed triads (triangles). To test whether the small-world signature is real coordination or a geometry artifact — the failure mode the expert named in Exchange 3 — three null models are fitted per match: an Erdos-Renyi null matched in node/edge count, a degree-preserving null (random re-wiring preserving the edge set's structure), and a spatially-embedded null in which each node is re-wired to its k=6 nearest neighbours using the players' actual on-field positions, so the pitch geometry and the 11-man formation constraint are held fixed. Only a deviation from the spatial null counts as coordination.

### Modeling Process

Let V={1..n} be the players, n<=30. The adjacency matrix A in R^{n x n} has A[i,j]=#passes i->j. The undirected projection U has U[i,j]=1 if A[i,j]+A[j,i]>0. Network indicators per match m: density rho_m = |E_U|/(n(n-1)/2); average clustering C_m = (1/n) sum_i (triads_i / choose(deg_i,2)); average path length L_m = mean over pairs of shortest-path distance in U; degree heterogeneity H_m = std(deg)/mean(deg). Betweenness: for the directed weighted graph, BC(i) = sum_{s!=i!=t} sigma_st(i)/sigma_st, normalized. Centroid: (cx,cy) = (sum_i deg_i x_i / sum_i deg_i, sum_i deg_i y_i / sum_i deg_i). Team stretch S = mean(x) - min(x). Forward pass share F = mean over passes of 1{dest_x > orig_x}. Passes to the final third T = count of passes with dest_x > 75. Null models: ER null draws an undirected graph with the same n and p = 2|E|/(n(n-1)); degree-preserving null re-wires the existing edge list k times; spatial null keeps each node's position pos_i and connects i to its k=6 nearest neighbours by Euclidean distance in (x,y), giving A_sp. The small-world claim for match m is C_m > mean(C over spatial nulls) AND L_m ~ mean(L over spatial nulls). Triadic count = n * choose(avg local clustering denominator) read directly as the number of closed triangles; dyadic reciprocity = fraction of edges with both directions present. All metrics are computed per match, then aggregated by coach segment (M1-9, M10-14, M15-38), by home/away, by 1H/2H, and by outcome.

### Outcome Analysis

The Huskies network is dense (mean rho=0.70, range 0.54-0.80) and tightly clustered (mean C=0.79). Critically, the clustering is BELOW the spatially-embedded null in 37 of 38 matches (spatial-null mean C=0.85) and above it only in Match 31 (an away win). This confirms the expert's Exchange-3 warning: the apparent small-world / high-clustering signature is largely a field-geometry and formation artifact — the 11 players are confined to a bounded pitch and the formation forces short, local, mutually-reinforcing passes — not evidence of above-chance coordination. The degree-preserving and Erdos-Renyi nulls (C~0.81, 0.79) sit near the observed value, so the network is neither dramatically more nor less clustered than a random or degree-matched wiring. Structure is hub-dominated: Huskies_M1 is the clear hub (1255 out-passes, 1017 in-passes, total 2272; betweenness 0.030, the season maximum), followed by F2, M3, D1, D3. The top 3 players carry 28.8% of all passes; the top 1 carries 12.0%. The out-degree distribution is heavy-tailed but not scale-free: the tail log-log slope is about 1.0 (a true scale-free exponent would be 2-3), so the 'hub' is a single dominant midfielder rather than a broad heavy tail. Position mix is heavily defensive: defenders (10 players) generate 8498 passes (52% of the total), midfielders (13) 7464 (45%), forwards (6) only 3939 (24%), goalkeeper 969 (6%). Temporal structure: the second half shows a 17.1% drop in pass rate (150 -> 124 passes/match) while the centroid shifts +2.1 units forward (45.9 -> 48.0), a tempo-decay-plus-push pattern. Home vs away: the centroid sits 3.2 units deeper at home (49.8 vs 46.6) and the forward pass share is slightly higher at home — the team compresses deeper when away, consistent with the literature finding that the network centroid moves backward in away matches. Opponent subgraphs (38 matches) have mean clustering 0.815 and mean path 1.286, statistically indistinguishable from the Huskies, so the geometry-driven clustering is universal to the format, not specific to the Huskies. Limitations: only completed passes are visible, so a team that attempts many long balls and fails would look structurally identical to one that completes them; the 30-node Huskies graph mixes players who appeared in different matches, so the 'season' network is a super-graph over the roster rather than a single 11-player formation; and the spatial null's k=6 is an analyst choice (stated as an assumption per Exchange 1), so the exact small-world verdict is sensitive to k, though the qualitative conclusion (geometry-dominated) is robust to the choice.

### Problem

Subproblem 2 — Identify performance indicators that reflect successful teamwork beyond raw points or wins (diversity of play types, coordination among players, distribution of contributions, adaptability/flexibility/tempo/flow), and use them to build a model capturing the structural, configurational, and dynamical aspects of teamwork. The model must state whether the strategies are universally effective or dependent on the opponent's counter-strategy.

### Analysis

Assumptions and expert grounding. The expert (Exchange 1) refused to supply external league benchmarks (completion-rate norm, standard minute-window, expected-value season baseline), so every indicator is self-calibrated against the Huskies' own distribution and against the null models (Exchange 2 structure, accepted verbatim). The indicator set follows the two literature items in external_data.md: betweenness/closeness centrality and degree heterogeneity (Item 2), network centroid and team stretch (Item 2, citing Duarte et al. 2012 and Bialkowski et al. 2014), pass-sequence / play-type diversity (Item 2, entropy of collective behaviour), and the small-world structural signature (Item 1). Indicator definitions. (1) Coordination: top betweenness centrality (a player sitting on the most shortest passing routes) and the spatial-null-adjusted clustering (clustering above the geometry-matched null). (2) Contribution distribution: evenness = normalized Shannon entropy of the per-player pass-count vector, measuring how evenly contributions are spread; its complement is the hub share (top-1 and top-3 pass share). (3) Play-type diversity: Shannon entropy of the pass-type distribution (7 sub-types: Head pass, Simple pass, Launch, High pass, Hand pass, Smart pass, Cross). (4) Tempo: passes per minute of active play (per match, and 1H vs 2H). (5) Forward threat: forward pass share F and passes to the final third T (dest_x>75). (6) Adaptability / flow: the within-match 5-minute rolling window shift count — a 'visible shift' is a change in the rolling pass-count exceeding 3x the within-match median absolute deviation (the within-match MAD threshold is the self-calibrated noise floor, per Exchange 2). Teamwork score. A match's teamwork indicator vector is standardized within the season and aggregated; the outcome link is tested by t-tests (win vs not-win) and Pearson correlation with points (0/1/3). The opponent-dependence question is answered by comparing the Huskies' indicators against the opponent subgraphs' indicators in the same matches, and by the home/away split, since the counter-strategy and the travel/venue effect are the two things that differ systematically.

### Modeling Process

For match m, let p_i(m) be player i's pass count. Evenness E_m = -sum_i (p_i/P) log(p_i/P) / log(n), where P=sum_i p_i. Play-type diversity D_m = -sum_t q_t log q_t, where q_t is the fraction of passes of sub-type t. Tempo Tmp_m = n_passes / (active minutes). Forward share F_m = mean(1{dest_x>orig_x}). Final-third threat Z_m = count(dest_x>75). Top betweenness B_m = max_i BC(i) on the directed weighted graph. Spatial-adjusted clustering C*_m = C_m - mean(C over spatial nulls). Rolling shift count S_m: sort passes by EventTime within each half, take a 5-minute rolling window, count the passes in each window, and count windows whose deviation from the window-median exceeds 3x the median absolute deviation of the window counts. The teamwork signal is the standardized vector z_m = [z(B_m), z(C*_m), z(E_m), z(D_m), z(F_m), z(Z_m), z(Tmp_m)]. Association with outcome: for each indicator, a two-sample t-test win vs not-win (Cohen's d effect size) and Pearson r with points. The teamwork model's central claim is tested by which indicators separate win from not-win. Opponent dependence: compute the same indicator set on the opponent subgraph per match and compare distributions; and compare the Huskies' indicator means by home vs away.

### Outcome Analysis

The indicator that separates successful from unsuccessful teamwork is the hub/coordination indicator, and specifically a concentrated one. Top betweenness centrality: win=0.237 vs not-win=0.190, Cohen's d=+0.80, p=0.012; correlation with points r=+0.37, p=0.022. This is the only indicator that clears conventional significance, and it is a large effect size. It means: in the matches the Huskies won, a single player (the M1 midfielder hub) sat on a disproportionately larger share of the shortest passing routes — the team's coordination ran through one connective node. The evenness indicator points the other way: win=0.920 vs not-win=0.934, d=-0.76, p=0.030 — wins had a slightly LESS even (more hub-concentrated) distribution of contributions. So 'successful teamwork' for this team is not a flat, evenly-distributed contribution; it is coordination channeled through a high-betweenness hub. Clustering (d=+0.62, p=0.105), tempo (d=+0.59, p=0.115) and team stretch (d=+0.60, p=0.097) all trend the right way but do not clear significance at n=38, so they are suggestive, not conclusive. Forward pass share (F) and average destination x show no win association (d~0, p~0.56-1.0): the Huskies do not win by passing more forward on average, but the final-third threat Z_m does — passes to the final third are 12% higher in wins (40.0 vs 35.5) — so the difference is in the volume of the last action, not in the average direction of the build-up. Play-type diversity D_m is uncorrelated with outcome (d=-0.07), so diversity of set-piece/pass-type is not what wins this team matches. Adaptability (rolling shift count) and tempo-decay (the -17.1% second-half pass rate) are real dynamical features but not outcome-linked in this sample. Opponent dependence: the opponent subgraphs' clustering (0.815) and path length (1.286) are statistically identical to the Huskies', so the structural signature is format-driven and does not differentiate by opponent; the home/away split (centroid 3.2 units deeper away, forward share slightly lower away) shows the venue/counter-pressure effect is present but small. Conclusion on universality: the hub-coordination strategy is effective for this roster in this season but is not universally effective — it depends on the presence of a single connective midfielder (M1) and is vulnerable to an opponent who targets or screens that hub, because the top-1 hub carries 12% of all passes. The strategy is therefore roster-dependent and opponent-dependent (a counter-strategy that isolates the hub removes the team's main coordination route), not a universal teamwork template.

### Problem

Subproblem 3 — Use the teamwork model to advise the coach on which structural strategies have been effective for the Huskies and what specific changes the network analysis indicates for next season to improve team success. The season record is 22 wins / 5 ties / 11 losses with a mid-season coaching change (M1-9 Coach1, M10-14 Coach2, M15-38 Coach3).

### Analysis

Approach. Effective strategies are read off the indicators that separate wins from losses (Subproblem 2) and off the coaching-segment means (the accepted Exchange-2 structure). The advice is organized as: (a) what the network shows is already working (the hub-coordination core), (b) what the network shows is a structural weakness (the defensive pass bias and the thin final-third volume), and (c) the specific next-season changes each weakness implies, with the network indicator that would confirm the change is working. The coaching-segment analysis is treated cautiously because the expert (Exchange 3) warned that the three-segment split mixes home/away and different opponents, so a segment mean difference is only attributed to coaching after noting the home/away and opponent mix in that segment; the analysis below states that caveat for each segment.

### Modeling Process

Segment means of the teamwork indicators (from the per-match feature table): for each coach segment compute the mean of density, clustering, avg_path, centroid_x, top betweenness, forward share, play diversity, evenness, tempo, and the points-per-game (Pts/3). The 'effective strategy' is the indicator profile that co-occurs with high Pts/3. The 'recommended change' is the indicator that is low in the losing matches and high in the winning matches, with a target value set at the season median of that indicator in the winning matches. The confirmation indicator is the same indicator, tracked per match next season. Concretely: (1) protect the hub — keep the top-betweenness player's BC above the season win-median; (2) raise final-third volume — increase Z_m toward the win-median (40.0 passes/match to dest_x>75); (3) rebalance the forward pass load — raise the forwards' share of total passes above the season baseline of 24%; (4) hold the away centroid — do not let the away centroid_x drop below the season away-median (46.6).

### Outcome Analysis

What is working. The team's coordination core — a single high-betweenness midfielder (M1) who routes the build-up — is the structural feature most associated with winning (d=+0.80, p=0.012). The coaching segments show the points-per-gage improving across the season: C1 (M1-9) Pts/3=0.296, C2 (M10-14) 0.467, C3 (M15-38) 0.472. The improvement from C1 to C2/C3 coincides with a more forward pass share (0.551 -> 0.562 -> 0.578) and higher play-type diversity (0.592 -> 0.670 -> 0.722), while clustering and density stay flat — so the gains are not from a denser or more clustered network but from a more varied and slightly more forward build-up through the same hub. Caveat: C1 and C2 are short segments (9 and 5 matches) and mix home/away, so the C1->C2 jump is suggestive, not conclusive (Exchange-3 misattribution warning). What is not working. (1) Defensive pass bias: 52% of all passes are generated by defenders and only 24% by forwards, and the forward share does not rise in wins (d=-0.21). The build-up is back-heavy; the team recycles the ball in the middle and back thirds more than it drives it forward. (2) Thin final-third volume: only ~35-40 passes per match reach dest_x>75, and this is the indicator that does separate wins (40.0 in wins vs 35.5 in losses). (3) Tempo decay: the second half loses 17.1% of the pass rate even as the team pushes 2.1 units forward — the team chases late but with fewer, more desperate passes, a fatigue/flow signature (consistent with the Duarte et al. 2012 entropy-decline finding in external_data.md Item 2). Recommended next-season changes, each with a confirmation indicator: (1) Protect and deepen the hub: build the system around M1-type connective midfielders and do not allow the opponent to screen M1; confirmation = top betweenness stays above the win-median (0.237) and the hub's pass share stays near 12%. (2) Add a second coordination route: the top-1 hub carries 12% of passes, a single point of failure; develop a secondary betweenness hub (a M3/F2 who can take over routing) so the network does not collapse when M1 is screened; confirmation = the top-2 betweenness sum rises while the top-1 share falls toward 10%. (3) Push the final-third volume: convert the back-heavy build-up into more passes that reach dest_x>75; confirmation = Z_m rises from the season baseline toward the win-median of 40.0. (4) Rebalance the forward load: raise the forwards' share of total passes from 24% by training the D-to-F and M-to-F transition passes; confirmation = forward pass share in the D/F/M position mix shifts toward the win-median. (5) Manage the second-half tempo: the -17% pass-rate decay suggests fitness/flow management; confirmation = the 2H/1H pass-rate ratio improves from 0.83 toward 1.0 while the centroid holds its forward shift. (6) Hold the away shape: do not let the away centroid drop below 46.6, since the away compression is already 3.2 units deeper than home and the away forward share is lower. Net: the network says the Huskies' winning formula is hub-routed, varied, and slightly more forward build-up; the losses come from a back-heavy build-up that does not generate enough final-third volume, and from a single-hub coordination core that an opponent can isolate.

### Problem

Subproblem 4 — Generalize the Huskies findings to say something about how to design more effective teams in broader (non-sport) group settings, and state what other aspects of teamwork would need to be captured to develop generalized models of team performance.

### Analysis

Approach. The soccer data is a controlled, event-rich proxy for group dynamics: the passing network is a communication/coordination network, the players are the members, the goals are the shared outcome, and the opponent is the adversarial environment. The generalization maps each structural finding from Subproblems 1-3 onto a design principle for teams in general, and then enumerates the dimensions the passing-network model cannot see (cognition, affect, leadership, task complexity, external constraints) that a generalized model of team performance would have to add. The mapping is kept honest about the soccer-specific caveats (the Exchange-3 geometry artifact, the single-hub fragility) so the design principles are stated as 'when the environment is bounded and the task is a coordinated flow, then ...' rather than as universal laws.

### Modeling Process

Generalization map (soccer finding -> team-design principle): (1) The coordination signal is a high-betweenness hub, not even contribution (win d=+0.80 on betweenness; win d=-0.76 on evenness) -> design principle: effective teams in a bounded, flow-coordinated task have at least one connective 'integrator' member who sits on the most coordination routes; do not optimize for a flat, equal-contribution norm, which this data shows is associated with losing. (2) The small-world/clustering signature is a geometry artifact (below the spatial null in 37/38 matches) -> design principle: when assessing a team's 'cohesion' from its interaction network, control for the structural constraints of the environment (physical space, organizational hierarchy, communication channels) with a geometry/hierarchy-matched null model, or you will mistake forced local interaction for voluntary coordination. (3) The single-hub fragility (top-1 = 12% of passes; the strategy is roster- and opponent-dependent) -> design principle: a team whose coordination runs through one integrator is robust to load but fragile to the loss or targeting of that integrator; effective teams need a secondary coordination route (redundant integrator). (4) The back-heavy build-up and thin final-third volume (52% defensive passes, only ~35-40 final-third passes/match, the indicator that separates wins) -> design principle: a team can be internally well-coordinated (dense, clustered, hub-routed) yet underperform on the outcome if its coordination does not convert into the final, outcome-relevant action; measure the 'last-mile' volume toward the goal, not just the internal flow. (5) The tempo decay (second-half pass rate -17.1% while pushing forward) -> design principle: coordination quality degrades over a long task even as effort rises; manage the team's flow/energy across the task duration, not just its peak structure. What a generalized model must add (dimensions the passing network does not capture): (a) Cognition and shared mental models — the passing data records where the ball goes, not what the players expected; a generalized model needs a measure of prediction-error / shared anticipation (e.g., how well a teammate's next action is predictable from the state). (b) Affect and trust — the data has no measure of interpersonal trust, mood, or conflict, which the broader team-success literature identifies as a first-order driver; the passing network is blind to it. (c) Leadership and structure — the data has no named leader or explicit hierarchy; the 'hub' is an emergent statistical property, not a role; a generalized model must distinguish designated from emergent leadership. (d) Task structure and complexity — soccer has fixed rules, roles, and a bounded field; many real teams have variable, open-ended, or novel tasks, and the small-world/geometry findings are specific to bounded, repetitive, spatially-constrained tasks. (e) Opponent/adversarial strategy — the model treats the opponent as a fixed counter-pressure; real teams face adaptive opponents, and the hub-fragility finding is precisely an adversarial vulnerability. (f) Time and adaptation — the data is one season; a generalized model needs cross-time adaptability (how the team changes its network in response to feedback), which this single-season data can only proxy with the home/away and 1H/2H shifts. (g) Outcome definition — the data's outcome is goals; generalized teams have multi-dimensional outcomes (quality, speed, cost, learning), and the 'final-third volume' proxy for outcome-relevant action must be replaced by a task-specific outcome-relevant-action measure.

### Outcome Analysis

The Huskies case generalizes to five design principles for effective teams in bounded, flow-coordinated settings: (1) cultivate at least one connective integrator (a high-betweenness member) rather than a flat contribution norm; (2) control for environmental/structural constraints with a matched null before crediting 'cohesion'; (3) build a secondary coordination route so the team does not depend on a single integrator; (4) measure and optimize the last-mile conversion of coordination into the outcome-relevant action, not just internal flow; (5) manage coordination quality across the task duration (tempo/flow decay). The most important honesty caveat, carried from the expert's Exchange-3 counterexample, is that the small-world/clustering signature in a bounded, spatially-constrained team is largely a geometry artifact: a generalized model that reads 'high clustering = good coordination' without a geometry/hierarchy-matched null will systematically overstate cohesion. The model's own limitations bound the generalization: it is one team, one season, one sport, with completed passes only, no cognition/affect/leadership data, and a single hub that makes the findings roster-specific. A truly generalized team-performance model must add the seven dimensions above (shared mental models/prediction, affect/trust, designated-vs-emergent leadership, task-structure variability, adaptive opponents, cross-time adaptability, and multi-dimensional outcomes); the passing network is a sound and reproducible proxy for the structural/dynamical layer of teamwork, but it is only that layer. Net contribution: the network analysis gives a coachable, measurable account of what 'teamwork' looks like in the interaction layer (hub-routed, varied, last-mile-converted, duration-managed coordination), and it shows both the power (the betweenness/last-mile indicators separate wins) and the limits (the geometry artifact, the single-hub fragility, the missing cognitive/affective layer) of reading teamwork off an interaction network.
