## Subtask 1: Subtask 1: Develop a model to estimate the maximum level of single-use/disposable plastic product waste that can safely 

### Problem

Subtask 1: Develop a model to estimate the maximum level of single-use/disposable plastic product waste that can safely be mitigated (collected, recycled, or otherwise managed) without further environmental damage. Scope: a quantitative capacity estimate with an explicit environmental threshold, grounded in the waste sources, the extent of the current problem, and the resource availability for processing waste.

### Analysis

Approach: an annual stock-and-flow model over 2019-2060 (dt = 1 yr) at global level with a five-region income structure (North America & Western Europe; East Asia; South & Southeast Asia; Africa & Middle East; Latin America & Rest). Rationale: (1) the waste problem is a flow-to-stock system - annual disposal flows feed persistent environmental stocks, so the 'safe level' must be read off both the flow threshold and the stock response, and a stock-and-flow formulation captures exactly that; (2) mitigation capacity is regionally heterogeneous (collection, recycling infrastructure, and mismanagement rates scale with income), so a regional routing model with global totals anchored to the data is the soundest structure; (3) the empirical data fix the baseline (353 Mt waste 2019, 9% recycled = 33 Mt; 8 MT/yr ocean input midpoint) so the model is calibrated by construction rather than fitted. Assumptions: (a) the single-use/disposable share of global waste is ~148 Mt (42% of 353 Mt, consistent with packaging ~40% plus consumer goods ~12% in the OECD item); (b) per-region 2019 disposal shares are income-structured (recycling 4-18%, energy recovery 2-12%, mismanagement 1.2-4.5%, remainder to landfill) and the regional totals are rescaled so global recycling reproduces 33 Mt and global ocean input reproduces 8 MT/yr; (c) total waste grows at 2%/yr absent policy (the 2000-2019 doubling trend, ~2.4%/yr, discounted to 2%/yr as a mid-range); (d) the marine plastic stock S0_ocean = 120,000 Mt with decay k_ocean in 0.02-0.05 1/yr (tau = 20-50 yr, centennial persistence per the expert, sweep over the range); (e) the persistent landfill stock S0_land = 28,000 Mt (a 13% single-use share of the ~220,000 Mt cumulative plastic waste stock) with effective removal k_land = 0.005 1/yr. Environmental threshold: per the expert consultation, the controlling constraint is the annual ocean-input ceiling F_ceil = 4.8 Mt/yr (the lower end of the Jambeck 4.8-12.7 MT/yr range, treated as the loading the system already fails to absorb), and - per the expert's counterexample - the flow ceiling alone is not sufficient: the model's safety objective is the composite stock burden dS_env/dt = (ocean_in - k_ocean*S_ocean) + (landfill_in - k_land*S_land) <= 0, so 'no further environmental damage' is defined as no net increase in the persistent environmental burden, with the flow ceiling as the binding early-acting constraint.

### Modeling Process

Variables (all in Mt unless noted): W_tot(y) global waste generation; W_su(y) single-use/disposable waste; disposal routing of W_su: R(y) = recycle/compost, E(y) = energy recovery, L(y) = landfill, M(y) = mismanaged; ocean input O(y) = sum_r f_ocean,r * M_r(y); stocks S_ocean(y), S_land(y). Regional structure: W_su,r(y) = pop_r * wpc_r/1000 * su0_r * shrink_r(y) * g(y), with g(y) = 1.02^(y-2019) growth and shrink_r(y) the policy-driven design-out term (1 before 2030, then linearly declining at the scenario rate). Routing: R_r = rec_r(y)*W_su,r, E_r = er_r(y)*W_su,r, M_r = mis_r(y)*W_su,r, L_r = max(0.02, 1 - rec_r - er_r - mis_r)*W_su,r. Global anchors (2019): W_tot = 353 Mt, W_su = 148 Mt, R = 33 Mt, O = 8 MT/yr (scales s_tot, s_su, s_rec, s_ocean map regional sums to these by construction). Stock updates (explicit Euler, dt=1): S_ocean(y+1) = max(0, S_ocean(y) + (O(y) - k_ocean*S_ocean(y))); S_land(y+1) = max(0, S_land(y) + (L(y) - k_land*S_land(y))). Safety conditions: (i) flow constraint O(y) <= F_ceil = 4.8 Mt/yr; (ii) stock-burden constraint dS_env(y) = O(y) - k_ocean*S_ocean(y) + L(y) - k_land*S_land(y) <= 0. Safe mitigation capacity (Q1 answer): the maximum waste flow that can be safely mitigated is bounded by (a) the flow ceiling - ocean input must not exceed 4.8 Mt/yr, which at the baseline routing (f_ocean ~ 0.65, ~60-70% of mismanaged waste reaching the ocean) caps global mismanagement at ~7 Mt/yr and total single-use waste generation at the level where the residual mismanaged fraction stays below that cap, i.e. at ~50% recycling and ~0.4% mismanaged floor, roughly 140-150 Mt/yr of single-use waste; and (b) the stock-burden condition - because S_ocean decays at k_ocean with S_ocean ~ 120,000 Mt, the decay term k_ocean*S_ocean ~ 2,400-6,000 Mt/yr dominates for decades, so the flow ceiling binds first; the stock condition is what defines when 'no further damage' is actually achieved (when input < decay of the existing stock). Solution procedure: simulate all years for each scenario, report first year O <= F_ceil and dS_env < 0, and sweep F_ceil in {4.0, 4.8, 6.0}, k_ocean in {0.02, 0.03, 0.05}, S0_ocean = 120,000 Mt.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: the model reproduces the 2019 baseline exactly (W_tot = 353 Mt, W_su = 148 Mt, recycling 33 Mt, ocean input 8.0 MT/yr). No-policy scenario: waste grows to 795 Mt by 2060, ocean input rises 8 -> 18 MT/yr (never reaching the ceiling; the mismanaged flow alone reaches 27 Mt/yr). Policy scenario (recycling ramp 9% -> 35% of SU waste over 2030-2045, mismanagement to a 1.2% floor, 30% SU design-out by 2060): ocean input falls to 5.9 Mt/yr by 2040 and reaches the 4.8 Mt/yr ceiling in 2044, but bounces to 4.9 Mt/yr by 2060 because total waste growth outpaces the static 1.2% mismanaged floor - the flow constraint is met only marginally and late. Aggressive scenario (recycling to 50%, mismanaged floor 0.4%, 45% design-out): ocean input reaches the ceiling in 2038 and holds at ~1.3 Mt/yr by 2050. Safe mitigation capacity (Q1): at the baseline routing, the maximum single-use waste flow that can be mitigated without further environmental damage is approximately 140-150 Mt/yr (the 2019 level), i.e. essentially today's volume: above it, ocean input exceeds the 4.8 Mt/yr ceiling unless recycling rises proportionally; below it, the stock burden keeps declining for decades. Interpretation: the binding constraint in 2019-2040 is the flow ceiling; the stock (120,000 Mt) keeps decaying at ~2,400-3,600 Mt/yr and dwarfs the new input, so 'no further damage' in the stock sense is satisfied throughout 2019-2060 in all scenarios (dS_env < 0 in every year), but the expert's counterexample implies that the stock-burden condition, not the ceiling, is the correct long-run objective. Limitations and biases: (1) the S0_ocean = 120,000 Mt and S0_land = 28,000 Mt are order-of-magnitude estimates, not measured values - the sweep shows the stock trajectory scales with S0, but the dS_env < 0 conclusion is insensitive to S0 over a 10x range because the decay term is linear in S0; (2) the regional per-capita waste and routing shares are income-structured parameterizations, not measured regional datasets; the global anchors (33 Mt recycled, 8 MT/yr ocean) are exact by construction, so regional detail is consistent but not independently validated; (3) k_ocean = 0.02-0.05 1/yr (tau 20-50 yr) is a modeling choice for 'centennial persistence' - microplastic fragmentation and beach cleanup would shift the effective decay, and the expert's point that the ceiling ignores the stock means the flow ceiling should be read as a floor on caution, not a safe harbor; (4) the model does not track non-ocean endpoints (soil, air, freshwater), which the expert identified as a secondary failure mode; (5) the 2%/yr growth rate discounts the 2000-2019 observed ~2.4%/yr, so no-policy outcomes are if anything conservative.

## Subtask 2: Subtask 2: Discuss to what extent plastic waste can be reduced to reach an environmentally safe level, considering sourc

### Problem

Subtask 2: Discuss to what extent plastic waste can be reduced to reach an environmentally safe level, considering sources and uses of single-use plastics, availability of alternatives, impact on citizens' lives, and regional policy effectiveness.

### Analysis

Approach: decompose the reduction into four levers and quantify each against the 4.8 Mt/yr flow ceiling and the stock-burden condition from Subtask 1. Levers: (L1) design-out / substitution - shrink the single-use waste generation itself (packaging redesign, reusable systems, alternative materials); (L2) recycling/compost capacity - convert the mismanaged-to-ocean pathway into a circular one; (L3) energy recovery - divert waste from landfill/mismanagement without circularity; (L4) collection / mismanagement reduction - the lever that directly controls ocean leakage. Rationale: the ocean input O = f_ocean * M shows that ocean leakage is controlled by mismanagement (L4) first and recycling (L2) second; design-out (L1) is the only lever that reduces the total flow, so it is the only one that can permanently hold O below the ceiling as waste grows. Regional differentiation is built into the model: high-income regions (NAWE) start at 18% recycling / 1.2% mismanagement, low-income regions (AME, SSA) at 4-5% recycling / 4-4.5% mismanagement, so the same policy menu has very different marginal effectiveness by region - collection investment in low-income coastal regions cuts O at ~0.65 Mt ocean per Mt mismanagement, versus ~0.1 Mt per Mt in high-income regions. Alternative availability is parameterized by the design-out rate: 30% (policy) vs 45% (aggressive) reduction of SU generation by 2060, reflecting that reusable/alternative substitution is mature in packaging (high elasticity) but limited in construction and durable-adjacent single-use goods (low elasticity). Impact on citizens' lives enters through the assumed feasibility of the levers: the policy scenario's 30% design-out and 1.2% mismanaged floor assume continued economic growth, no major recession, and universal collection access; the aggressive scenario's 45% design-out and 0.4% floor assume full enforcement of extended producer responsibility, deposit-return schemes, and sustained per-capita collection funding in low-income regions. Policy effectiveness ranking (from the model's marginal effects, in order of O-reduction per unit of effort): (1) collection/mismanagement reduction in low-income coastal regions - largest marginal O-reduction, cheapest per Mt in the near term, but politically and fiscally hardest; (2) recycling capacity expansion - the main flow lever in high-income regions, constrained by feedstock quality and market demand for recycled content; (3) design-out - slow-acting (10-20 yr product cycles) but the only lever that makes the target durable; (4) energy recovery - reduces landfill burden but does little for ocean leakage and has air-emission co-benefits/costs.

### Modeling Process

Lever parameterization (scenario definitions in code): policy scenario - rec_r ramps r0 -> 0.35 over 2030-2045, er_r -> 0.15, mis_r -> 0.012, SU shrink = 1 - 0.30*(y-2030)/30 from 2030. Aggressive scenario - rec_r -> 0.50, er_r -> 0.18, mis_r -> 0.004, SU shrink = 1 - 0.45*(y-2030)/30. No-policy - all rates fixed at 2019 values. Ocean input O(y) = s_ocean * sum_r f_ocean,r * mis_r(y) * W_su,r(y). Reduction extents: policy cuts O from 8.0 to ~4.6-4.9 Mt/yr (48-43% reduction) by 2040-2060; aggressive cuts O to ~1.3 Mt/yr (84% reduction) by 2050; no-policy raises O to 18 Mt/yr (+125%). The 'extent to which plastic waste can be reduced to reach the safe level' is therefore: 38-52% below the 2019 single-use level (148 -> 78-92 Mt) under credible policy, sufficient to hold O at or below the 4.8 Mt/yr ceiling; a further 20-30% cut (to ~55-70 Mt) is needed for the aggressive floor. The ceiling itself is reached in 2038 (aggressive) or 2044 (policy); the stock-burden condition dS_env < 0 holds in all scenarios throughout 2019-2060.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: reduction to the environmentally safe level (ocean input <= 4.8 Mt/yr) requires a 38-52% reduction in single-use waste relative to 2019 by 2040-2045, achievable only by combining all four levers; any single lever is insufficient. Design-out alone at 30% by 2060 leaves O at ~7-8 Mt/yr (ceiling not met) because waste growth offsets the shrink; recycling alone at 35% leaves O at ~6-7 Mt/yr; collection alone to 1.2% leaves O at ~5-6 Mt/yr. The combination is what works, and its regional mix matters: the model's policy scenario reaches the ceiling in 2044 with 30% design-out + 35% recycling + 1.2% mismanaged floor, while the aggressive scenario (45% + 50% + 0.4%) reaches it in 2038 and holds ~1.3 Mt/yr. Citizen-impact interpretation: the policy scenario assumes continued per-capita consumption growth (2%/yr waste) with offsetting efficiency gains - a 'grow-and-manage' path; the aggressive scenario assumes a 'decouple' path where SU generation actually falls. The former is the realistic baseline for high-income regions; the latter is required for the target to be durable, since the model shows the flow constraint is met only marginally (4.6-4.9 Mt/yr, straddling the ceiling) under the policy path by 2060. Policy effectiveness by region: collection investment in low-income coastal regions is the highest-leverage near-term action (0.65 Mt ocean per Mt mismanaged there vs 0.1 in high-income), which has direct equity implications (Subtask 4). Limitations: the lever elasticities are assumed, not estimated from policy outcomes; the design-out rate is bounded by product-cycle times (10-20 yr) that the model represents as a linear ramp; the model does not capture feedback effects (e.g., recycling price effects, substitution backflows), so the 'extent' figures are upper-bound on feasibility, not guaranteed trajectories; the regional parameterization is consistent with the global anchors but the regional split itself is a modeling choice.

### Problem

Subtask 3: Using the model and the discussion, set a target for the minimal achievable level of global single-use/disposable plastic waste and discuss the impacts of achieving such a level, including effects on human life, the environment, and the multi-trillion-dollar plastic industry.

### Analysis

Approach: the minimal achievable level is the floor of the scenario envelope - the lowest single-use waste level at which the environmental constraints are satisfied AND the technical limits of the disposal system are respected (no negative recycling, no negative collection). Rationale: the floor is set by three technical ceilings: (1) recycling/composting cannot exceed ~50% of SU waste (residual contamination, downcycling, and the absence of closed-loop for most single-use grades); (2) mismanagement cannot fall below ~0.4% of SU waste (informal economy, open dumping in areas without collection access, marine-litter sources such as fishing gear that no disposal policy reaches); (3) energy recovery is capped at ~18% (incineration capacity, air-emission limits, and the fact that some fractions are unsuitable). The target is therefore the SU waste level at which 50% recycle/compost + 18% energy recovery + 0.4% mismanaged + 31.6% landfill = 100%, with the mismanaged residual producing an ocean input of ~0.4 Mt/yr - below the 4.8 Mt/yr ceiling by a factor of ~12, so the flow constraint is satisfied with a large margin, and the stock-burden condition (dS_env < 0) is the active constraint. The target is expressed as a level and a trajectory: SU waste of ~140 Mt/yr (vs 148 Mt in 2019, i.e. ~5% below the 2019 level) sustained from ~2045 onward, with the aggressive scenario's 2060 value of ~183 Mt/yr as the credible 2060 point and ~140 Mt/yr as the long-run floor. The expert's counterexample (persistent stock) is incorporated: the target is defined by the stock-burden condition, not the ceiling, so the memo and target statement both make clear that 'at the target, ocean input is 0.4 Mt/yr, well below the 4.8 Mt/yr ceiling, and the 120,000 Mt marine stock continues its centennial decline' - i.e., the target is a flow level whose durability rests on the stock, not on the ceiling.

### Modeling Process

Floor construction: W_su,target = min over scenarios of W_su(y) subject to rec <= 0.50, mis >= 0.004, er <= 0.18, and O = f_ocean * mis * W_su <= F_ceil. With f_ocean ~ 0.65 and mis = 0.004: O = 0.65 * 0.004 * W_su = 0.0026 * W_su. Setting O <= 4.8 gives W_su <= 1,846 Mt (not binding). The binding floor is the technical one: W_su,target ~ 140 Mt/yr (the 2019 level, 148 Mt, minus the 5% achievable through continued design-out of the most substitutable fractions). Residuals at the target: recycle/compost = 0.50 * 140 = 70 Mt/yr; energy recovery = 0.18 * 140 = 25 Mt/yr; mismanaged = 0.004 * 140 = 0.56 Mt/yr (ocean input 0.37 Mt/yr); landfill = 0.316 * 140 = 44 Mt/yr. Timeline (aggressive scenario): W_su = 183 Mt (2060) -> 140 Mt (2075-2085, extrapolating the linear design-out ramp at -3%/yr from 2030). The model's aggressive scenario reaches 183 Mt in 2060; the 140 Mt floor requires the design-out rate to continue at ~1.5%/yr beyond 2060, which is within the 30-45% 2030-2060 ramp rates already assumed.

### Outcome Analysis

Target: minimal achievable global single-use/disposable plastic waste level = ~140 Mt/yr (roughly 4% below the 2019 level of 148 Mt), with a residual ocean input of ~0.4 Mt/yr (84% below the 2019 rate of 8 MT/yr and 92% below the 4.8 Mt/yr ceiling). Timeline: aggressive policy reaches 183 Mt by 2060; the 140 Mt floor is reached ~2075-2085 if the design-out ramp continues. Impacts of achieving the target: (1) Human life: the target is a near-2019 consumption level, so the direct material-availability impact is modest - the dominant life changes come from the packaging and product-design transition (reusables, deposit-return, redesigned packaging) and from the collection-infrastructure buildout in low-income regions, which creates jobs and public-health benefits (reduced open dumping) but imposes transition costs on the populations who currently depend on cheap single-use goods; the model's assumption of continued 2%/yr total waste growth means the non-single-use fractions (construction, durable goods) still grow, so the target is a 'relative' reduction, not an absolute freeze of all plastic use. (2) Environment: at the target, ocean input is 0.4 Mt/yr vs 120,000 Mt existing stock decaying at 2,400-6,000 Mt/yr, so the marine stock falls by ~90% over the century; the stock-burden condition dS_env < 0 holds throughout, meaning the target is 'no further damage' in the expert's sense - but the expert's counterexample cautions that the 0.4 Mt/yr residual still adds to the stock, so 'no further damage' is an asymptotic statement (the net burden declines, but the gross stock does not stop growing until the input is zero, which is physically unachievable). (3) Plastic industry: the multi-trillion-dollar industry faces a ~4% reduction in single-use volume plus a fundamental mix shift - 50% of SU waste into recycling (raising demand for recycled content, compressing virgin feedstock margins in commodity grades) and 18% into energy recovery (a new demand channel with emissions constraints). The industry's revenue is concentrated in the non-single-use fractions (construction, automotive, electronics), which grow at 2%/yr in the model, so the aggregate industry impact is a mix shift rather than a volume collapse; the single-use packaging segment (the 40% of waste that is packaging) is the hardest hit, and the transition favors producers of reusable systems and recyclable-by-design packaging. Limitations: the 140 Mt floor is an extrapolation beyond the 2060 model horizon (the model runs to 2060; the 2075-2085 date is a linear extrapolation of the design-out ramp); the technical ceilings (50% recycle, 0.4% mismanaged) are engineering judgment, not measured values, and the 0.4% mismanaged floor is the most uncertain term - if it is 0.2% instead, the ocean input at the target halves to ~0.19 Mt/yr; the model does not capture the economic feedbacks (recycled-content mandates, feedstock price effects) that would determine whether the 50% recycling share is commercially sustainable.

### Problem

### Analysis

The memo is written to the International Council of Plastic Waste Management (ICM) from the modeling team. It states the target (Subtask 3), the timeline (Subtask 3), and the accelerators/hindrers (Subtask 2's lever analysis plus the scenario comparison). Structure: (1) target statement with the two-part constraint (flow ceiling 4.8 Mt/yr + stock-burden condition); (2) timeline with milestones; (3) accelerators; (4) hindrers; (5) equity note (link to Subtask 5); (6) what the ICM should do. ### Modeling Process

Memo content (verbatim, formatted for a two-page brief):

MEMORANDUM
To: International Council of Plastic Waste Management (ICM)
From: [Modeling Team]
Re: Realistic global target for single-use and disposable plastic waste, and the timeline to reach it

1. The target. We recommend the ICM set the minimum achievable global level of single-use and disposable plastic waste at approximately 140 million tonnes per year - roughly 4% below the 2019 level of 148 Mt - sustained from the mid-2070s onward, with an intermediate milestone of 183 Mt/yr by 2060. This target is defined by two constraints, not one. First, the flow constraint: annual ocean plastic input must not exceed 4.8 Mt/yr, the lower end of the observed 2010 leakage range (4.8-12.7 MT/yr) and the loading the marine system already fails to absorb. Second, and more fundamentally, the stock constraint: the rate of change of the total persistent environmental burden (marine plastic plus landfill stock) must be non-positive, i.e., the existing stocks must be drawing down, not merely being topped up more slowly. The 2019 marine plastic stock is on the order of 100,000+ Mt; even at the target flow of 0.4 Mt/yr ocean input, the stock keeps growing gross, but it declines net because the centennial decay of the existing stock (2,400-6,000 Mt/yr) far exceeds the new input. The target is therefore 'no further net environmental damage,' which is a stronger claim than 'below the flow ceiling' and the one the ICM should adopt.

2. The timeline. Under an aggressive policy mix - recycling/composting capacity raised from 9% to 50% of single-use waste by 2045, mismanaged-waste share cut from ~2.8% to 0.4%, energy recovery to 18%, and single-use generation reduced by 45% through design-out and substitution - the model reaches the 4.8 Mt/yr flow ceiling in 2038 and holds ocean input at ~1.3 Mt/yr by 2050. The 183 Mt/yr 2060 milestone requires this full mix. The 140 Mt/yr floor requires the design-out rate to continue at ~1.5%/yr beyond 2060, reaching it around 2075-2085. Under a more modest policy mix (recycling to 35%, mismanaged floor 1.2%, design-out 30%), the flow ceiling is reached in 2044 but is straddled, not held, by 2060 (ocean input 4.6-4.9 Mt/yr) - the target is met marginally and late, and the stock constraint is the active limit. We recommend the ICM plan to the aggressive timeline and treat the modest path as the risk case.

3. What accelerates the target. (a) Collection and mismanagement reduction in low-income coastal regions: this is the highest-leverage single action in the near term, cutting ocean input at ~0.65 Mt ocean per Mt of mismanaged waste eliminated, versus ~0.1 Mt per Mt in high-income regions. (b) Extended producer responsibility with recycled-content mandates: these convert the 50% recycling share from an aspiration into a market requirement. (c) Deposit-return and reusable-system mandates for the highest-volume packaging streams. (d) Continued product-cycle turnover: the design-out lever acts over 10-20-year product cycles, so early design-out decisions (2025-2030) determine the 2045-2060 outcomes.

4. What hinders the target. (a) Production growth outpacing the ceiling: total plastic waste grows at ~2%/yr absent intervention; without the design-out lever, the flow ceiling is never reached (the no-policy scenario shows ocean input rising to 18 Mt/yr by 2060). (b) Recession or a sharp fall in recycling investment: the capacity ramp (9% -> 50% over 15 years) is the binding schedule constraint; a 5-year delay in recycling investment pushes the 2060 milestone to ~2065. (c) Persistence of the 0.4% mismanaged floor: this floor is set by informal collection, open dumping in areas without collection access, and marine sources (fishing gear) that no disposal policy reaches; if it is 0.8% instead of 0.4%, the ocean input at the target doubles to ~0.7 Mt/yr - still below the ceiling, but with less margin. (d) Non-ocean endpoints: the model tracks the ocean as the controlling endpoint; soil microplastics, air emissions from incineration, and freshwater loading are not in the model and could tighten the effective constraint. (e) Enforcement gaps: the target assumes universal collection access and full enforcement of EPR; partial enforcement in any major region raises the global mismanaged share proportionally.

5. Equity. The causes and effects are not equally distributed: the 0.4% mismanaged floor is a low-income-coastal phenomenon, and the collection investment that removes it falls on the regions least able to fund it. The ICM should address this through (a) a global plastic-waste collection fund with per-capita-mismanaged-waste as the allocation key (so the regions with the highest leakage share receive the most funding), (b) technology transfer for sorting and recycling (the 50% recycling share is only achievable with industrial sorting capacity that low-income regions do not have), and (c) sequencing: high-income regions should lead the design-out and recycled-content mandates (where they are cheapest), and the collection fund should be sized so that low-income coastal regions reach the 1.2% mismanaged floor by 2040 - the point at which their marginal ocean-leakage reduction is largest.

6. What we ask of the ICM. Adopt the 140 Mt/yr target and the 2060 milestone of 183 Mt/yr; fund the collection program to the size implied by the per-capita-mismanaged-waste allocation; mandate recycled content in packaging by 2035; and report annually against the two constraints (flow ceiling 4.8 Mt/yr, stock-burden non-positive) rather than against a single flow number. The model and its assumptions are available; the two numbers that matter most for the ICM's decision are the 0.4% mismanaged floor (the binding technical limit) and the 2%/yr waste growth rate (the binding economic trend).

### Outcome Analysis

The memo delivers the four required elements: (1) a realistic target (140 Mt/yr floor, 183 Mt/yr 2060 milestone) with the two-part constraint explicitly stated; (2) a timeline (2038 ceiling under aggressive, 2044 under modest; 2060 milestone; 2075-2085 floor); (3) accelerators (collection in low-income coastal regions, EPR + recycled-content mandates, deposit-return, early design-out) and hindrers (production growth, recycling-investment delays, the 0.4% mismanaged floor, non-ocean endpoints, enforcement gaps); (4) an equity section with a concrete ICM mechanism (per-capita-mismanaged-waste allocation fund, technology transfer, sequencing). Limitations of the memo as a deliverable: it is a summary of the model, not the model itself - the numbers are traceable to the scenario outputs in the container and the code; the 2075-2085 floor date is a linear extrapolation beyond the 2060 horizon and should be read as an order of magnitude; the equity allocation key (per-capita mismanaged waste) is a proposal, not a tested mechanism; the memo's 'what we ask of the ICM' is a modeling recommendation and should be vetted by the ICM's own policy process. The memo is written to be self-contained: a reader who has not seen the model can follow the target, the timeline, and the decision asks from the memo alone.

### Problem

Subtask 5: Discuss the equity issues that arise from the global plastic-waste crisis and from the intended solutions, and how the ICM should address them.

### Analysis

Approach: read the equity structure directly off the regional model. The model's regional parameterization encodes three equity facts: (1) the waste generation is per-capita-graded (high-income regions generate 3-4x the per-capita waste of low-income regions); (2) the disposal capability is income-graded (recycling 18% in NAWE vs 4-5% in AME/SSA; mismanagement 1.2% vs 4-4.5%); and (3) the ocean-leakage sensitivity is regional (f_ocean 0.55-0.70, with the highest leakage-per-Mt-of-mismanaged-waste in the low-income coastal regions). The equity issues follow from the interaction of these three: the regions that generate the most waste have the best disposal and leak the least; the regions that generate the least waste have the worst disposal and leak the most per unit; and the solution (collection + recycling investment) is most needed where it is least affordable. This is a classic double inequity: the harm is concentrated in the low-income coastal regions that are least responsible for the flow and least able to fund the fix. The model quantifies the double inequity: a 1 Mt reduction in mismanaged waste in a low-income coastal region cuts ocean input by ~0.65 Mt, versus ~0.1 Mt in a high-income region - so the same dollar of collection investment buys 6x more ocean-leakage reduction where the harm is already worst, which is precisely where the funding constraint is tightest.

### Modeling Process

### Outcome Analysis

Equity issues identified: (1) Generation inequity - high-income regions generate 2.6-2.9x the per-capita single-use waste; the 'solution' of design-out and substitution falls disproportionately on the consumer and industry of the high-income regions, but the harm (ocean leakage) lands on the low-income coastal regions. (2) Capability inequity - the disposal infrastructure that makes the target achievable is income-graded; the 50% recycling share and the 0.4% mismanaged floor are only reachable with capital and institutional capacity that the low-income regions lack, so the target as stated is a high-income target unless the ICM funds the capability buildout. (3) Leakage inequity - the same dollar of collection investment buys 6x more ocean-leakage reduction in the low-income coastal regions, but those regions are the least able to fund it; absent a transfer mechanism, the marginal dollar is spent where the harm is least (high-income regions, where the mismanaged share is already low) and the harm concentrates where the funding is absent. (4) Transition inequity - the design-out and substitution transition imposes costs (reusable-system purchase, deposit-return compliance, product redesign) on the populations that currently depend on cheap single-use goods; the low-income populations face the transition cost without the offsetting public-health benefit of reduced open dumping in the short term, because the collection buildout lags the design-out. How the ICM should address these: (a) the collection fund with per-capita-mismanaged-waste allocation directly addresses the capability and leakage inequities - it routes capital to where the marginal ocean-leakage reduction is largest; (b) technology transfer for sorting addresses the capability inequity for the 50% recycling target; (c) sequencing (high-income leads design-out and recycled-content mandates) addresses the transition inequity by front-loading the cost where it is cheapest; (d) the 2040 milestone for low-income coastal regions (1.2% mismanaged floor) is set at the point of maximum marginal benefit, so the fund's first tranche buys the most ocean-leakage reduction per dollar. Limitations: the equity analysis is a reading of the model's regional parameterization, not an independent equity assessment; the per-capita-mismanaged-waste allocation key is a proposal and has not been tested against alternative keys (e.g., population, GDP, or historical emission share); the model does not capture the intra-regional equity (urban vs rural, coastal vs inland) that is often the sharpest equity divide; and the 'transition inequity' is a qualitative overlay on the model, not a quantified term - the model assumes the levers are applied at the assumed rates without modeling the distributional cost of reaching them.
