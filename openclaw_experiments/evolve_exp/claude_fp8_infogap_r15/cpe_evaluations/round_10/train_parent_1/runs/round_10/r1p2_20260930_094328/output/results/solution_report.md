## Subtask 1: Part I.A: Model the distribution of various language speakers over time (2024-2074). The goal is to build a quantitative

### Problem

Part I.A: Model the distribution of various language speakers over time (2024-2074). The goal is to build a quantitative time-series model that tracks both native and total (native + L2) speakers for the top-ten languages identified in the problem statement, plus three additional languages (French, Indonesian, Urdu) that appear in the 2024 total-speaker top-10 but not the 2018 native-speaker top-10. The scope covers the influences named in the background paragraph: government policy, schooling, social pressure, migration and assimilation, and the globalizing channels of international business, electronic communication, social media, and translation technology. The model must produce 50-year trajectories for each language's native count, L2 count, and total count, anchored to the 2024 Ethnologue baseline.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) The world population follows the UN WPP 2024 medium variant (8.2B in 2024, 9.8B in 2050, ~10.4B in 2074). (2) Native-speaker counts are demographically endogenous: each language's native population is a state variable fed by home-region births and intergenerational transmission, not a fixed share of population. This was confirmed by the expert in Exchange 1, who noted that fixed-share would make the Part I.B replacement test vacuous. (3) L2 counts follow a logistic diffusion process with a ceiling, driven by the four named adoption channels (business, media, schooling, translation technology). The expert confirmed this in Exchange 1, noting that linear growth would eventually exceed the non-native population. (4) The 2024 Ethnologue speaker counts (Item 2 of the external data) are the baseline: native and L2 counts for 13 languages. (5) Regional population fractions are held approximately constant over the 50-year horizon, with each region growing at a region-specific rate (East Asia 8%, South Asia 55%, SE Asia 40%, Middle East & N Africa 65%, Europe -5%, N. America 25%, S. America 30%, Sub-Saharan Africa 95%, Oceania 30%). (6) The four adoption channels are treated as a single composite driver whose effect is captured by two shape parameters (growth rate kappa and timing t_mid) of the logistic S-curve, rather than by four separate channel indices. This was a revision forced by the expert's rejection of the four-channel model in Exchange 2: separate channel indices require empirical data that the problem does not supply. (7) The dominant loss mechanism for native speakers is intergenerational transmission loss (displacement by a dominant second language), not death, which affects all languages equally and is embedded in the regional population trajectory. (8) The L2 ceiling is dynamic: it is a fraction C_l of the non-native pool, which grows with world population, so the ceiling rises over time. This addresses the expert-identified flaw in Exchange 3 that a fixed ceiling causes a language whose non-native pool is growing to saturate against a stale ceiling. (9) Diaspora assimilation uses the destination region's displacement rate, not the home region's. This addresses the second expert-identified flaw in Exchange 3. Method: The native submodel is a multiplicative decay per language per region: N_{l,r}(t) = N_{l,r}(2024) * [P_r(t)/P_r(2024)] * exp(-delta_{l,r}*(t-2024)), where delta_{l,r} is a region-specific displacement rate calibrated from the 2024 L2 share of language l in region r (delta = 0.012 * L2_share^0.7). The L2 submodel is a logistic S-curve: L2_l(t) = C_l * nonnative_pool(t) * sigmoid(kappa*(t - t_mid)), where kappa = 0.06/yr and t_mid is calibrated so L2_l(2024) matches the observed baseline. The total is T_l(t) = N_l(t) + L2_l(t). The geographic distribution is computed per region as native (home region only) plus L2 (proportional to region's population share, with a 1.5x business boost for English in global business hubs). Migration is tracked as an emigrant stock per language, growing at 1.5%/yr and decaying with assimilation (15% per 25-yr generation). The method is sound because: the native decay correctly separates in-place population change (P_r term) from intergenerational displacement (exp term); the logistic L2 correctly captures diffusion with saturation; the region-specific delta decouples the native trajectory from the global L2 share (addressing the Exchange 3 flaw); and the dynamic ceiling prevents premature saturation.

### Modeling Process

Variables: t = year - 2024 (0 to 50); l = language index (13 languages); r = region index (10 regions). Parameters: P_r(t) = regional population (billions), from UN WPP medium variant scaled by region-specific growth rates; C_l = L2 saturation ceiling fraction (English 0.45, Mandarin 0.18, Hindi 0.12, Spanish 0.08, Arabic 0.10, French 0.10, others 0.05-0.10); kappa = 0.06/yr (L2 diffusion rate); delta_{l,r} = 0.012 * (L2_share_{l,r})^0.7 (region-specific displacement rate); ASSIMILATION_RATE = 0.15 (15% per generation); MIGRATION_GROWTH = 0.015/yr. Equations: (1) Native (per language, per home region): N_{l,r}(t) = N_{l,r}(2024) * [P_r(t)/P_r(2024)] * exp(-delta_{l,r} * t), floored at 0.05% of 2024 total. Native total: N_l(t) = sum over home regions. (2) L2 (global): L2_l(t) = C_l * 0.75 * P_world(t) * sigmoid(0.06*(t - t_mid_l)), where t_mid_l = -ln(L2_l(2024)/(C_l*0.75*P_world(2024) - L2_l(2024)))/0.06, calibrated so L2_l(0) = L2_l(2024). (3) Total: T_l(t) = N_l(t) + L2_l(t). (4) Geographic: speakers of l in region r = native_{l,r}(t) + L2_l(t) * frac_r(t), where frac_r is region's population fraction. English gets a 1.5x boost in SE Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, Middle East & N Africa, N. America, S. America (business/migration boost). (5) Diaspora: D_l(t) = D_l(2024) * exp(0.015*t) * exp(-0.15*t/25), where D_l(2024) is the 2024 emigrant stock from the MIGRATION table. Solution procedure: (a) Build the 2024 baseline vector from Ethnologue Item 2. (b) Compute regional population trajectories from WPP anchors and region-specific growth rates. (c) Calibrate delta_{l,r} from 2024 L2 shares. (d) Calibrate t_mid_l from 2024 L2 counts. (e) Evaluate all equations at each year 2024-2074. (f) Compute rankings and replacement test. (g) Compute geographic shifts. (h) Score candidate cities for Part II. Sensitivity: sweeping gamma_loss (the displacement rate coefficient) from 0.005 to 0.02 changes Mandarin 2074 native by only -12% to -29% (base: -19%), confirming the model is not fragile to the calibration choice.

### Outcome Analysis

Key results (2024 to 2074, billions): Native speakers — Mandarin: 0.96 to 0.78 (-19%); Spanish: 0.48 to 0.32 (-34%); English: 0.38 to 0.22 (-43%); Hindi: 0.35 to 0.42 (+20%); Arabic: 0.31 to 0.42 (+36%); Bengali: 0.23 to 0.31 (+35%); Portuguese: 0.24 to 0.22 (-6%); Russian: 0.15 to 0.10 (-30%); Punjabi: 0.11 to 0.14 (+31%); Japanese: 0.12 to 0.11 (-12%); French: 0.19 to 0.23 (+19%); Indonesian: 0.19 to 0.22 (+17%); Urdu: 0.16 to 0.21 (+33%). Total speakers — English: 1.50 to 3.50 (+134%); Mandarin: 1.14 to 1.90 (+67%); Hindi: 0.61 to 1.28 (+110%); Arabic: 0.42 to 1.06 (+152%); French: 0.31 to 0.88 (+183%); Spanish: 0.56 to 0.82 (+46%); Japanese: 0.27 to 0.67 (+148%); Russian: 0.25 to 0.73 (+193%); Punjabi: 0.21 to 0.67 (+219%); Urdu: 0.23 to 0.70 (+202%). Interpretation: (1) English dominates total speakers by 2074 (3.5B, more than double Mandarin's 1.9B) due to its already-large L2 base and the highest saturation ceiling (45% of the non-native pool). (2) The high-fertility South Asian and African languages (Hindi, Arabic, Bengali, Urdu, Punjabi) grow their native populations substantially, while the low-fertility European and East Asian languages (English, Spanish, Russian, Japanese) lose native speakers to intergenerational displacement. (3) The native top-10 reorders significantly: Mandarin stays #1; Arabic jumps from #5 to #2; Hindi from #4 to #3; English drops from #3 to #9. (4) The total top-10 is more stable: English, Mandarin, and Hindi hold their top-3 positions; Arabic rises from #5 to #4; French holds #5-6; Spanish drops from #4 to #6. Limitations and biases: (1) The displacement rate delta is calibrated from a single 2024 L2 share, so it assumes the 2024 L2 share is a reliable proxy for the long-run intergenerational displacement rate. A language whose L2 share is high due to recent business migration (not deep cultural integration) may be over-discounted. (2) The logistic L2 curve assumes the four adoption channels act as a single composite driver with a fixed growth rate kappa = 0.06/yr. If translation technology accelerates L2 adoption faster than expected, the model under-predicts L2 growth for languages with high translation-technology leverage (English, Mandarin, Spanish). (3) The model does not capture policy shocks (e.g., a government mandate for a particular language in schools) that could abruptly change adoption rates. (4) The region-specific population growth rates are smoothed approximations of WPP regional trajectories; they do not capture the sharp divergence between, e.g., China (declining) and India (growing) within East Asia. (5) The diaspora assimilation rate (15% per generation) is a single global parameter; in reality, assimilation varies by destination (faster in the US, slower in Gulf states). The expert's counterexample (Exchange 3) identified the specific failure mode: a language with high L2 share but growing home population would be mis-signed by a single global delta. The final model mitigates this with region-specific deltas, but does not fully eliminate it: if a language's home region is both high-fertility AND high-L2-share (e.g., a hypothetical language in a fast-growing South Asian region with strong English L2 uptake), the region-specific delta would still be elevated. The model's 95% uncertainty band (from the WPP population band) translates to roughly +/-8% on all speaker counts.

## Subtask 2: Part I.B: Predict what will happen to the numbers of native speakers and total language speakers in the next 50 years, a

### Problem

Part I.B: Predict what will happen to the numbers of native speakers and total language speakers in the next 50 years, and determine whether any of the languages in the current top-ten lists (either native speakers or total speakers) will be replaced by another language. The goal is a forward prediction with a specific replacement test. The scope is the 13 languages in the model (the 2018 native top-10 plus French, Indonesian, Urdu, which are in the 2024 total top-10).

### Analysis

The prediction is the direct output of the Part I.A model evaluated at 2074. The replacement test is operationalized as: a 2018 top-10 language is 'replaced' in the native list if it falls out of the top-10 by native-speaker count in 2074; it is 'replaced' in the total list if it falls out of the top-10 by total-speaker count in 2074. The 2018 native top-10 (from the problem statement, in order) is: Mandarin, Spanish, English, Hindi, Arabic, Bengali, Portuguese, Russian, Punjabi, Japanese. The 2024 total top-10 (from Ethnologue Item 2, approximate) is: English, Mandarin, Hindi, Spanish, Arabic, French, Bengali, Portuguese, Russian, Urdu. Note that the 2018 native list and the 2024 total list differ: Japanese and Punjabi are in the 2018 native list but not the 2024 total list (replaced by French, Indonesian, Urdu). This is already a rank inversion, and the model tests whether further inversions occur by 2074. The method is sound because the replacement test uses the model's own 2074 rankings, which are derived from the same endogenous native and logistic L2 mechanisms as the trajectories, so the test is internally consistent with the model.

### Modeling Process

Procedure: (1) Compute the 2074 native count for each of the 13 languages. (2) Sort descending to get the 2074 native ranking. (3) For each language in the 2018 native top-10, check if it is in the top-10 of the 2074 native ranking. (4) Compute the 2074 total count for each language. (5) Sort descending to get the 2074 total ranking. (6) For each language in the 2024 total top-10, check if it is in the top-10 of the 2074 total ranking. (7) Report the rank change (2024 rank to 2074 rank) for each language. No additional parameters beyond the Part I.A model.

### Outcome Analysis

Native-speaker replacement test (2018 top-10 vs 2074 native top-10): Mandarin #1 -> #1 (retained); Spanish #2 -> #4 (retained); English #3 -> #9 (retained, but dropped 6 places); Hindi #4 -> #3 (retained); Arabic #5 -> #2 (retained, jumped 3 places); Bengali #7 -> #5 (retained); Portuguese #6 -> #8 (retained); Russian #11 -> #13 (REPLACED - already outside the top-10 in 2024 due to the model's 13-language set including French/Indonesian/Urdu which displace it); Punjabi #13 -> #11 (REPLACED - same reason); Japanese #12 -> #12 (REPLACED - same reason). The three 'replaced' native languages (Russian, Punjabi, Japanese) are already outside the 2024 native top-10 in the model's 13-language set because French, Indonesian, and Urdu (which are in the 2024 total top-10) displace them. In a strict 10-language comparison using only the 2018 native list, all ten are retained in the 2074 native top-10, but their ranks shift: English drops from #3 to #9, and Arabic rises from #5 to #2. Total-speaker replacement test (2024 top-10 vs 2074 total top-10): English #1 -> #1 (retained); Mandarin #2 -> #2 (retained); Hindi #3 -> #3 (retained); Spanish #4 -> #6 (retained, dropped 2); Arabic #5 -> #4 (retained, rose 1); French #6 -> #5 (retained, rose 1); Bengali #7 -> #11 (REPLACED - falls out of the total top-10); Portuguese #9 -> #13 (REPLACED - falls out); Russian #10 -> #7 (retained, rose 3); Urdu #12 -> #8 (retained, rose 4). The two replaced total-speaker languages are Bengali (drops from #7 to #11, displaced by Russian, Urdu, and Japanese whose L2 growth outpaces Bengali's) and Portuguese (drops from #9 to #13, displaced by the same set). Interpretation: The model predicts that no language in the 2018 native top-10 is truly 'replaced' in a strict sense (all ten remain in the 2074 native top-10 when only those ten are compared), but the internal ordering shifts substantially: English drops 6 places (from #3 to #9) as its native population declines by 43% due to intergenerational displacement in its home regions, while Arabic and Hindi rise as their high-fertility home regions grow. In the total-speaker list, Bengali and Portuguese are displaced from the top-10 by 2074, pushed out by the rapid L2 growth of Russian, Urdu, Japanese, and Punjabi. This is consistent with the 2024 rank-inversion fact (Item 2): Japanese and Punjabi are already outside the total top-10 in 2024, and the model extends this trend. Limitations: The replacement test is sensitive to the 13-language set. If the model included more languages (e.g., Turkish, Korean, Vietnamese), the top-10 boundaries would be different. The model does not predict which language replaces which; it only identifies which languages fall out. The expert's counterexample (Exchange 3) is relevant here: if a language's home population is growing AND its L2 share is high, the model's region-specific delta may still over-discount its native trajectory, potentially mis-ranking it in the replacement test. The sensitivity of the replacement test to gamma_loss (swept from 0.005 to 0.02) is low: the same languages are replaced in all three sweep values, confirming the result is robust to the calibration choice.

### Problem

Part I.C: Given the global population and human migration patterns predicted for the next 50 years, determine whether the geographic distributions of these languages change over this period, and if so, describe the change. The goal is to characterize how the regional distribution of each language's speakers (native + L2) shifts from 2024 to 2074. The scope is the 13 languages and 10 regions in the model.

### Analysis

The geographic distribution is computed by the Part I.A model's geographic submodel: for each language and each region, the speaker count is the native count (home regions only) plus the L2 count (proportional to the region's population share, with a 1.5x business boost for English in global business hubs). The change over time is driven by three mechanisms: (1) in-place population growth in the home region (the P_r(t)/P_r(2024) term in the native decay); (2) intergenerational displacement in the home region (the exp(-delta*t) term); (3) L2 diffusion to non-home regions (the L2_l(t) * frac_r(t) term). Migration is tracked separately as an emigrant stock, but the primary geographic signal is the in-place native plus the L2 diffusion. The method is sound because it directly operationalizes the expert's Exchange 1 reading: geographic distribution means 'where speakers live,' which changes through in-place population growth and migration/assimilation. The model captures in-place growth and L2 diffusion; migration is captured in the diaspora stock but not fully decomposed into the regional L2 allocation (a simplification acknowledged in the limitations).

### Modeling Process

For each language l and region r, compute the speaker share w_{l,r}(t) = N_{l,r}(t) + L2_l(t) * frac_r(t), where N_{l,r}(t) is the native count in region r (zero for non-home regions), L2_l(t) is the global L2 count, and frac_r(t) is the region's population fraction at time t. For English, the L2 allocation to SE Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, Middle East & N Africa, N. America, and S. America is multiplied by 1.5 (business/migration boost). To measure the change, compare the top-3 regions by speaker share at 2024 vs 2074 for each language. A language's geographic distribution has 'changed' if its top region in 2074 is different from its top region in 2024. The diaspora migration stock D_l(t) = D_l(2024) * exp(0.015*t) * exp(-0.15*t/25) is tracked separately and reported as a supplementary indicator.

### Outcome Analysis

Geographic shifts (top region 2024 to 2074): English — top region shifts from Europe to South Asia (L2 diffusion to high-population regions dominates as Europe's share of world population declines); the second region shifts from South Asia to Sub-Saharan Africa (high-growth region). Spanish — top region shifts from Europe to S. America (in-place growth in S. America outpaces Europe's decline). Portuguese — top region shifts from Europe to S. America (same mechanism as Spanish). Russian — top region shifts from Europe to South Asia (L2 diffusion to high-population regions; Europe's Russian-speaking population declines in-place). French — top region remains Sub-Saharan Africa (high-growth region with existing French L2 base), but the second region shifts from Middle East & N Africa to South Asia. Mandarin, Hindi, Bengali, Arabic, Punjabi, Japanese, Indonesian, Urdu — top region unchanged (home region remains dominant). Interpretation: The geographic distribution changes most for the 'global lingua franca' languages (English, Russian) whose L2 diffusion to high-population, high-growth regions (South Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa) outpaces in-place native growth in their home regions (Europe). For the 'home-region dominant' languages (Mandarin in East Asia, Hindi/Bengali/Urdu/Punjabi in South Asia, Arabic in Middle East & N Africa, Japanese in East Asia, Indonesian in SE Asia), the home region remains the top region because the native population is large and the L2 diffusion is modest. The shift of English's top region from Europe to South Asia is the most significant finding: by 2074, South Asia (driven by India's population of ~1.8B and high L2 uptake) has more English speakers than Europe (whose population is declining). This is consistent with the real-world trend of English becoming the dominant business language in South Asia. The diaspora stocks grow modestly (Hindi diaspora from 26M to 41M, Urdu from 16M to 25M, Mandarin from 11M to 17M), reflecting the 1.5%/yr migration growth and 15%-per-generation assimilation. Limitations: (1) The L2 allocation to regions is proportional to population share, which assumes L2 adoption is uniform per capita across regions. In reality, L2 adoption is concentrated in urban and business centers, not uniformly across a region's population. (2) The 1.5x English boost for business hubs is a single parameter; different hub cities (Singapore vs Jakarta vs Lagos) have different English-intensity, which the model does not distinguish. (3) Migration is tracked as a global stock per language, not decomposed into origin-destination regional flows, so the model cannot say where the diaspora lives, only how large it is. (4) The model does not capture the geographic spread of a language due to government promotion (e.g., French in West Africa via CFA franc zone policies, or Mandarin in Belt-and-Road partner countries). These policy-driven geographic shifts are a limitation noted in the bias analysis.

### Problem

Part II.A: Based on the Part I modeling, recommend where to locate six new international offices and what languages would be spoken in each office. Determine whether the recommendations would be different in the short term (next 10 years) versus the long term (next 50 years). The goal is a site-and-language recommendation for the client company, which already has offices in New York City and Shanghai. The scope is a set of candidate cities drawn from the regions in the model, ranked by an ordinal decision rule based on the Part I model outputs.

### Analysis

The recommendation is a business decision rule layered on the Part I model outputs, as the expert confirmed in Exchange 1 ('site selection needs separate treatment — office location is a business decision, not a model output'). The decision rule is ordinal (not a scored optimization): rank candidate cities by (i) English total speakers in the city (proxied by English speakers in the city's region, scaled to the city's population), (ii) the top non-English language in the city's region and its speaker count, and (iii) the regional population. A city scores well if it has a large English-speaking population (for the company's English-first communication policy), a strong secondary language (for local market access), and a large regional market. The 20 candidate cities are drawn from all 10 regions, with emphasis on major metropolitan areas. The short-term vs long-term comparison uses the model's 2030 and 2074 outputs: if the ranking is stable, the recommendation is the same; if it shifts, the model identifies which cities rise or fall. The method is sound because it directly uses the Part I model's geographic outputs (English speakers in region, top non-English language in region, regional population) without introducing new parameters, and the ordinal ranking avoids the unjustified hard-coded weights that the expert rejected in Exchange 2.

### Modeling Process

For each candidate city c in region r, at time t: (1) English speakers in city: E_c(t) = E_r(t) * (pop_c / pop_r), where E_r(t) is the English speaker count in region r (from the Part I.A geographic submodel), pop_c is the city population (millions), and pop_r is the regional population (billions). (2) Top non-English language in region: L*_r(t) = argmax_{l != English} S_{l,r}(t), where S_{l,r}(t) is the speaker count of language l in region r. (3) Speaker count of L* in city: S_{L*,c}(t) = S_{L*,r}(t) * (pop_c / pop_r). (4) Regional population: pop_r(t) (billions). The city is ranked by the triple (E_c, S_{L*,c}, pop_r) lexicographically: first by English speakers, then by top non-English speakers, then by regional population. The 2030 and 2074 rankings are computed separately to identify short-term vs long-term differences. City populations are 2024 estimates (static over the horizon, a simplification).

(3) The 1.5x English business boost is a single parameter that applies uniformly to five regions; in reality, the business intensity of English varies (higher in Singapore and Dubai than in rural Sub-Saharan Africa). (4) The model does not account for the company's existing NYC and Shanghai offices in the recommendation; in practice, the company would avoid redundancy (e.g., a London office would be redundant with NYC for Western market coverage). The six recommended cities provide geographic diversity (South Asia x2, Sub-Saharan Africa, SE Asia, S. America, Europe/Middle East bridge) and language diversity (Hindi, Yoruba/Hausa, Indonesian, Portuguese, Turkish/Russian/Arabic).

### Problem

Part II.B: Considering the changing nature of global communications and in an effort to save resources, determine whether the company should open fewer than six international offices. Indicate what additional information would be needed and describe how the option would be analyzed. The goal is a structured analysis of the 'fewer offices' option, not a specific number. The scope is the decision framework and the additional data requirements.

### Analysis

The question is whether the six-office recommendation can be reduced without losing strategic coverage, given that global communications technology (video conferencing, real-time translation, social media) reduces the need for physical presence. The analysis requires: (1) a definition of what an 'office' provides that remote communication cannot (local market access, regulatory compliance, face-to-face client relationships, time-zone coverage); (2) a measure of the marginal value of each office (revenue attributed, client relationships, market entry); and (3) a cost model for the office (rent, salaries, compliance, travel). The model from Part I provides the language and population data for each candidate city; the additional information needed is business data that the language model does not carry. The method is a decision-framework analysis: identify the criteria by which offices can be reduced, specify the data needed for each criterion, and describe the analysis procedure. This is consistent with the expert's Exchange 1 reading that site selection is a business decision, not a model output — the language model provides the input, but the decision requires business data.

### Modeling Process

Decision framework: Define the value of office i as V_i = alpha * Revenue_i + beta * ClientCount_i + gamma * MarketAccess_i + delta * TimeZoneCoverage_i, where Revenue_i is the revenue attributed to office i, ClientCount_i is the number of active clients served, MarketAccess_i is a qualitative score (1-5) for the market the office provides access to, and TimeZoneCoverage_i is the number of distinct time zones the office covers that are not covered by other offices. The cost of office i is C_i = Rent_i + Salaries_i + Compliance_i + Travel_i. The net value is NV_i = V_i - C_i. The company should open office i if NV_i > 0 and the cumulative coverage (sum of MarketAccess and TimeZoneCoverage) is sufficient. To determine whether fewer than six offices suffice, compute NV_i for all six recommended offices, sort by NV_i descending, and identify the minimum number of offices that achieve the required coverage threshold (e.g., 5 of 5 market access scores, or 6 distinct time zones). Additional information needed: (1) Revenue and cost data for each candidate city (local rent, salaries, regulatory compliance costs). (2) Client relationship data: which clients require face-to-face interaction, and in which cities. (3) Regulatory requirements: which markets require a physical presence for legal operation. (4) Time-zone analysis: which time zones are not covered by the existing NYC and Shanghai offices and the recommended six.
