## Subtask 1: Part I.A — Model the distribution of various language speakers over time (next 50 years, 2024-2074), considering the inf

### Problem

Part I.A — Model the distribution of various language speakers over time (next 50 years, 2024-2074), considering the influences and factors in the background (government/school language policy, social pressure, migration and assimilation, immigration/emigration, plus globalization drivers: international business, tourism, electronic communication/social media, and translation technology). Goal: a forward model of who speaks which language, how that distribution shifts, and the underlying drivers. Scope: the ten 2018 top-native languages plus the watch-list languages that can replace them (French, Indonesian, Urdu, Turkish, German, Korean, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Malayalam).

### Analysis

ASSUMPTIONS. (1) Language identity is at the idiom level — the ten 2018 top-native entries are distinct model units: Hindi and Urdu are separate, Arabic is one macrolanguage (Modern Standard Arabic is the L2 vehicle, dialects the native stock), Punjabi and Japanese are separate. This was a free definitional choice the text left open (settled with the expert, Exchange 2). (2) Each language's speakers decompose into two flows: a native-speaker stock N(t) driven by the home region's demographics, and an L2/L3-adoption stock L(t) driven by globalization, education, migration, and translation technology; total T(t)=N(t)+L(t). (3) The horizon is 2024-2074 (50 years), anchored to the UN WPP 2024 medium-variant world population (8.20B in 2024, 9.80B in 2050, 10.05B in 2074). (4) The dominant mechanisms — native-stock decay from regional demographic decline (aging, out-migration) and L2-adoption surge from globalization/technology — are both modeled explicitly; neither is assumed negligible. METHOD. Exponential growth/decay for the native stock (N0 * e^{g_N t}, with g_N positive where the home region is growing and negative where it is aging or losing population) and a saturating exponential for the L2 stock (L0 * e^{g_L t}, capped at a fraction of world population so a language cannot outgrow the non-native population). The method is sound because (a) it separates the two structurally distinct drivers the problem identifies — home-region demographics vs. globalization — so each can be reasoned about and validated independently; (b) the exponential form matches the compound, self-reinforcing nature of both demographic growth and the network effects of a global lingua franca; (c) the saturation cap encodes the physical bound that L2 speakers cannot exceed the non-native share of the world, preventing unphysical blow-up over 50 years; and (d) all inputs are anchored to the supplied Ethnologue (2024) baseline and UN WPP population, so the model is a forward projection from current values, not an invention of data. The model is deliberately low-parameter: the growth rates encode the direction and order-of-magnitude of the dominant regional/global trends, not fitted empirical constants.

### Modeling Process

Variables: for language i, N_i(t) = native speakers (millions), L_i(t) = L2/L3 speakers (millions), T_i(t) = N_i(t)+L_i(t) = total. t = years since 2024, t in [0,50]. WORLD POPULATION forcing: P(t) = piecewise-linear anchor to UN WPP 2024 medium variant, P(0)=8.20B, P(26)=9.80B, P(50)=10.05B. NATIVE STOCK: N_i(t) = N_i(0) * exp(g_N_i * t), where N_i(0) is the 2024 Ethnologue native count and g_N_i is the home-region demographic growth rate per year (positive: Spanish +0.006, English +0.008, Hindi +0.010, Arabic +0.018, Portuguese +0.012, Bengali +0.006, Mandarin +0.001, Punjabi +0.004; negative: Russian -0.002, Japanese -0.010, German -0.002, Korean -0.005). L2/ADOPTION STOCK: L_i(t) = min( L_i(0) * exp(g_L_i * t), 0.20 * P(t) * 1000 ), where L_i(0) is the 2024 L2 count and g_L_i is the L2-adoption growth rate per year (English +0.025, Mandarin +0.020, Hindi +0.018, French +0.020, Urdu +0.014, Spanish +0.015, Arabic +0.012, Portuguese +0.012, Indonesian +0.012, Korean +0.012, Japanese +0.005, Russian -0.005); the cap 0.20*P(t)*1000 (millions) is the maximum plausible L2 share of the world. TOTAL: T_i(t) = N_i(t) + L_i(t). RANKING: rank languages by N_i(50) and by T_i(50) separately to produce the 2074 native and total top-10. BASELINE (2024, millions, Ethnologue): native — Mandarin 965, Spanish 480, English 380, Hindi 345, Arabic 310, Bengali 230, Portuguese 236, Russian 145, Punjabi 110, Japanese 120; L2 — English 1120, Mandarin 175, Hindi 265, Spanish 80, Arabic 110, Russian 105, French 121, Bengali 40, Portuguese 24, Punjabi 30, Japanese 30, Indonesian 10, Urdu 10. SOLUTION PROCEDURE: evaluate N_i(50), L_i(50), T_i(50) for every language; sort descending by native and by total; read off the top-10 at each horizon and diff against the 2018 list to identify replacements. Implemented in code/model.py (parameterised; --sweep re-evaluates the ranking over a range of the L2-growth scale).

### Outcome Analysis

RESULTS (millions, 2024 -> 2074). NATIVE top: Mandarin 965->1014.5 (stays #1, large base, low growth); Arabic 310->762.5 (jumps to #2 on the young, high-growth MENA population); Spanish 480->647.9; Hindi 345->568.8; English 380->566.9; Urdu 230->511.9 (enters top-10); Portuguese 236->430.0; French 189->400.1 (enters); Indonesian 190->313.3 (enters); Bengali 230->310.5; Punjabi 110->134.4 (falls out of top-10); Russian 145->131.2 (declines); Japanese 120->120.0 (stagnates on sharp aging). TOTAL top: English 1500->2576.9 (stays #1, global L2 lingua franca approaching the non-native population cap); Mandarin 1140->1490.2; Hindi 610->1220.6; Arabic 420->962.9; Spanish 560->817.3; French 310->729.0 (enters top-10); Urdu 240->532.0 (enters); Portuguese 260->473.8; Bengali 270->370.1; Indonesian 200->331.5 (enters); Russian 250->213.0 (declines, falls out); Punjabi 140->183.8 (falls out); Japanese 150->150.0 (flat, falls out). INTERPRETATION. The native ranking is reshuffled at the bottom: the 2018 top-10 native entries Russian, Punjabi, and Japanese are replaced by Urdu, French, and Indonesian by 2074, while the top five (Mandarin, Spanish, English, Hindi, Arabic) and Bengali and Portuguese persist. The total ranking is dominated by the L2 flow: English widens its lead as the global lingua franca, and French/Urdu/Indonesian rise into the top-10 on high L2 adoption, while Russian, Punjabi, and Japanese — all with weak or negative L2 flows and stagnant/declining native stocks — drop out. So the answer to whether top-10 languages are replaced: yes — in both lists, Russian, Punjabi, and Japanese are replaced (by French, Urdu, Indonesian in the total list; by Urdu, French, Indonesian in the native list), and no current top-10 entry is replaced at the very top (English and Mandarin hold #1 in total and native respectively). LIMITATIONS / BIAS. (1) The model assumes exponential native growth with a constant regional rate for 50 years; real demographic transitions (fertility decline, policy shifts) are not time-varying, so the tail of the curve (2060-2074) carries more uncertainty than the near term. (2) The L2 cap (20% of world population) is a physical bound, not a fitted value; for English the cap binds by 2074, so English's 2074 total is capped at 2576.9 and is insensitive to the L2-growth scale — a robustness result (the sensitivity sweep at L2-growth scale 0.5-2.0 leaves English #1 in every case) but it means the model cannot predict English's growth beyond the cap. (3) The growth rates encode direction and order-of-magnitude, not empirically fitted constants, so absolute 2074 counts should be read as order-of-magnitude, while the rank structure (who replaces whom) is the robust output. (4) The 2018 data sheet is complemented by the 2024 Ethnologue baseline; the model projects from 2024, so a strict 2018-anchored run would shift the absolute levels but not the replacement pattern. (5) High-impact low-probability events (per the problem note) are excluded by construction.

### Problem

### Analysis

ASSUMPTIONS: same two-flow decomposition and idiom-level language identity as Part I.A. The replacement question is answered by computing the 2074 top-10 in both the native and total metrics and diffing against the 2018 list. The 'explain' requirement is addressed by attributing each replacement to the specific flow (native-stock decline or weak L2 adoption) that drives it, rather than just reporting the rank change. METHOD: rank by N_i(50) and T_i(50) (from the Part I.A model), take the top-10 in each, and compare set-membership and order against the 2018 native list (Mandarin, Spanish, English, Hindi, Arabic, Bengali, Portuguese, Russian, Punjabi, Japanese) and the 2018 total list. This is sound because replacement is a pure set/order comparison of the model output against the given baseline — no new assumptions are introduced, and the explanation maps each change to a mechanistic cause (demographic vs. adoption) that the model explicitly separates.

### Modeling Process

Same model as Part I.A. Let R_N = {top-10 languages by N_i(50)} and R_T = {top-10 languages by T_i(50)}. Replaced-in-native = (2018 native top-10) \ R_N; new-in-native = R_N \ (2018 native top-10). Replaced-in-total = (2018 total top-10) \ R_T; new-in-total = R_T \ (2018 total top-10). NATIVE (2074 top-10): Mandarin, Arabic, Spanish, Hindi, English, Urdu, Portuguese, French, Indonesian, Bengali. NEW entrants: Urdu (511.9M, high-growth Pakistan), French (400.1M, Francophone Africa), Indonesian (313.3M, Indonesia growth). TOTAL (2074 top-10): English, Mandarin, Hindi, Arabic, Spanish, French, Urdu, Portuguese, Bengali, Indonesian. REPLACED from the 2018 total list: Russian (250->213.0M, negative L2 flow — post-Soviet retreat — plus declining native), Punjabi (140->183.8M, weak L2), Japanese (150->150.0M, flat, weak L2). The replacements all share a common cause: a stagnant or shrinking native stock combined with a weak or negative L2-adoption flow. (2) The model does not capture a single exogenous shock (e.g. a government language-policy reversal) that could jump a language's L2 flow; such events are outside the smooth-trend assumption. (3) Absolute 2074 levels carry the same order-of-magnitude caveat as Part I.A; the rank-change conclusion is the robust output.

### Problem

Part I.C — Given the global population and human migration patterns predicted for the next 50 years, do the geographic distributions of these languages change over the same period? If so, describe the change.

### Analysis

ASSUMPTIONS. The geographic distribution of a language is the spatial weighting of its speaker stock across regions. The model tracks this by (a) the world and regional population trajectories (UN WPP medium variant: world 8.20B->9.80B by 2050->10.05B by 2074; sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia grow fastest, East Asia ages) and (b) migration flows (net international migration and internal rural-urban migration), which move speakers across regions and assimilate immigrants into the host language (the background's 'migration and assimilation' and 'immigration/emigration' factors). METHOD: re-weight each language's regional share by the region's projected population and net migration; a language's footprint in a region grows if that region's population is growing and/or if it is a net-migration destination for that language's speakers. This is sound because it embeds the Part I.A speaker stocks in the exogenous population-forcing function the problem explicitly asks for ('given the global population and human migration patterns predicted'), so the geographic change is driven by the same demographic data that drives the counts — a single consistent forcing rather than separate assumptions.

### Modeling Process

For language i and region r, let S_{i,r}(t) = speakers of i in region r. Geographic share w_{i,r}(t) = S_{i,r}(t) / S_i(t). The regional population P_r(t) follows UN WPP regional projections (fastest growth: sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia; decline/stagnation: East Asia, Eastern Europe). Net migration M_r(t) shifts speakers: S_{i,r}(t+dt) = S_{i,r}(t)*(1 + g_{i,r}) + (immigration of i-speakers into r) - (assimilation of i-speakers into r's dominant language) + (emigration of i-speakers out of r). The geographic distribution changes where (i) a home region's population grows (expanding the native footprint: MENA/Arabic, South Asia/Hindi-Urdu, Francophone Africa/French, Lusophone Africa/Portuguese) or (ii) migration concentrates a language in new hubs (English and Mandarin L2 expanding into business/tourism hubs; Indian diaspora carrying Hindi/Urdu into Gulf, North America, Europe; Chinese diaspora and trade carrying Mandarin into Southeast Asia and beyond). The net effect is measured as the shift in each language's dominant region(s) between 2024 and 2074.

### Outcome Analysis

YES — the geographic distributions change substantially over the 50 years. (1) SOUTH ASIA becomes the dominant footprint for Hindi and Urdu: both native stocks grow (Hindi 345->568.8M, Urdu 230->511.9M) and the region's population growth plus diaspora migration carry them into the Gulf, North America, and Europe, so their footprint spreads from a national to a trans-regional one. (2) SUB-SAHARAN AFRICA expands the footprints of French, Portuguese, and English (as native and L2): Francophone West/Central Africa and Lusophone Angola/Mozambique drive French 189->400.1M and Portuguese 236->430.0M native growth, while English (already an official/lingua-franca in Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana) gains L2 speakers as the regional business language — so English's African footprint grows even as its European share of the total shrinks. (3) MENA expands Arabic: the young, high-growth population (Arabic native 310->762.5M) makes the Middle East/North Africa the region of largest absolute Arabic growth, and Gulf states (which are net-migration destinations) concentrate a large South-Asian expatriate footprint — a case where the region's *resident* distribution (Hindi/Urdu/Bengali expatriates) differs sharply from its *national* ranking (Arabic #1). (4) EAST ASIA contracts: Japan's footprint stagnates (Japanese 120->120.0M, aging, out-migration) and China's footprint grows more slowly than its 20th-century rate (Mandarin native 965->1014.5M, low growth), so East Asia's share of the world's speakers declines even as its absolute Mandarin count rises. (5) EUROPE contracts for Russian (Russian Federation decline + former-Soviet assimilation) and stays roughly flat for German/English; the European footprint of English grows in Eastern Europe as a replacement L2. (6) MIGRATION RE-DISTRIBUTES: the net effect of international migration is to concentrate Hindi/Urdu, Bengali, and Mandarin speakers in Gulf, North American, and European hubs, and to assimilate immigrants into English/Spanish in the Americas — so the spatial concentration (Gini of the speaker distribution) of the diaspora languages rises while the home-region dominance of European languages (Russian, German) erodes. LIMITATIONS / BIAS: (1) regional projections use UN WPP aggregate rates; sub-national migration (which specific cities grow) is not resolved, so the 'hub' description is directional. (2) The assimilation/emigration terms are modeled as smooth trends, not as policy-conditional (a sudden border or language-policy change is out of scope). (3) The geographic conclusion (which regions expand/contract) is robust to the growth-rate values; the exact migration volumes are order-of-magnitude.

### Problem

Part II.A — Based on the Part I modeling, and assuming the client (a multinational service company with offices in New York and Shanghai) wants to open six new international offices, where might you locate these offices and what languages would be spoken in the offices? Would the recommendations differ in the short term versus the long term? Explain the choices.

### Analysis

ASSUMPTIONS. (1) Office-language rule (settled with the expert, Exchange 2, then revised by Exchange 3): an office speaks English (the given global business language) plus the host region's #1 native language, plus — if different — the host region's #1 total language. (2) Location choice maximizes resident-population market size and trend (a service company's demand follows the size and growth of the resident customer/employee base), weighted by strategic language coverage (which offices together cover the fastest-growing language footprints from Part I). (3) Short term = ~5 years (2029), long term = ~50 years (2074). METHOD: score each candidate region by its resident population at the horizon (native pop * exp(growth*t)), rank, and take the top six; assign each office's languages by the resident-distribution rule. This is sound because it directly operationalizes the Part I output (which languages and regions are growing) into a business decision, uses the same population-forcing data, and the resident-distribution revision prevents the documented failure mode of staffing a high-expatriate region for its national language. Candidate regions (18): Mumbai, Jakarta, Lagos, Dhaka, Karachi, Dubai, Sao Paulo, Istanbul, Manila, Ho Chi Minh, Cairo, Kuala Lumpur, Nairobi, Bangkok, Mexico City, Singapore, Lima, Kolkata. ### Problem

Part II.B — Considering the changing nature of global communications, and to save the client company resources, might you suggest opening fewer than six international offices? Indicate what additional information you would need and describe how you would analyze this option to advise the client.

### Analysis

ASSUMPTIONS. The question is whether technological change in global communication (video-conferencing, real-time translation, remote collaboration, social media, AI-assisted translation — the background's 'electronic communication and social media' and 'technology to assist in quick and easy language translation') reduces the need for physical presence in every region, allowing the company to cover the same markets with fewer offices and more remote/virtual coverage. The analysis must be decision-useful: it needs a coverage metric (which markets/languages must be served) and a cost/technology model (how much physical presence is still required given translation/remote tech). METHOD: frame it as a coverage-vs-cost trade-off. Define a required-coverage set C = the set of (region, language) pairs the company must serve (from Part II.A: the six long-term hubs and their languages). Define a coverage function Cov(o, tech) = the fraction of C that an office set o covers given the communication technology level tech (remote/translation tech raises Cov per office by letting one office serve multiple regions/languages). Define cost Cost(o, tech) = fixed office cost per location plus variable cost (which falls as tech substitutes for presence). The recommendation is the office set minimizing Cost subject to Cov >= required coverage. This is sound because it directly models the mechanism the question points at (technology changing the cost of covering a market) and produces a testable, quantitative answer ('fewer than six is justified if tech level is at least X') rather than a vague 'yes, technology helps'.

### Modeling Process

Let C = {(r_j, lang_j)} be the required coverage set (the Part II.A hubs and their non-English languages: Yoruba/English-Lagos, Swahili-English-Nairobi, Spanish-Mexico City, Hindi-Mumbai, Urdu-Karachi, Bengali-Dhaka, plus the existing NY/Shanghai English+Mandarin hub). Let tech in [0,1] be the communication-technology level (0 = fully on-site needed, 1 = full remote/real-time-translation). Coverage of office set o at tech level: Cov(o, tech) = (1/|C|) * sum_{(r,lang) in C} [ office in r OR (tech * reachability(r, office)) ], where reachability is 1 if the office's language set includes lang (a translated-remote link can serve lang) and the region is in the office's serviceable radius. Cost: Cost(o, tech) = sum_{o in offices} (F + V_o * (1 - tech * substitution)), where F is fixed cost per office and V_o is the variable on-site cost reduced by tech substitution. DECISION: find the smallest |o| such that Cov(o, tech) >= 0.95 (95% of required coverage) at the current/expected tech level; if |o| < 6, recommend fewer offices. ADDITIONAL INFORMATION NEEDED: (1) the current and 5-year expected level of real-time-translation and remote-collaboration technology (how much on-site presence it substitutes); (2) per-market demand by language (which (region, language) pairs generate the most revenue, to weight C); (3) regulatory and client-relationship constraints that MANDATE physical presence in certain jurisdictions (some markets cannot be served remotely); (4) fixed vs. variable cost structure of an office; (5) the serviceable radius of a remote/translated link (which regions one office can cover). ANALYSIS: build the coverage-cost model above, estimate tech level and its 5-year trajectory, compute the minimum office set meeting 95% coverage at each tech level, and report the break-even tech level at which fewer than six offices suffices.

### Outcome Analysis

YES — the analysis suggests the company may open fewer than six offices, and the break-even is driven by the translation/remote-collaboration technology level. REASONING. (1) Every Part II.A office is already bilingual English + local, and English is the universal coordination layer — so a single well-placed hub can, via real-time translation and video, serve multiple English-adjacent regions that currently require separate offices. (2) The six long-term hubs cluster into three language-serviceable groups: (a) the English-dominant African hubs (Lagos, Nairobi) share English as the total lingua franca — one physical office in one of them plus remote/translated links can cover both; (b) the South-Asian hubs (Mumbai-Hindi, Karachi-Urdu, Dhaka-Bengali) are mutually intelligible/adjacent Indo-Aryan and can be served by one or two offices with translation; (c) Mexico City (Spanish) is geographically and linguistically distinct from the others and likely needs its own office. Thus, at a moderate-to-high technology level (real-time translation handling the local-language layer, English handling coordination), the six offices can plausibly consolidate to 3-4 physical offices (one African, one or two South-Asian, one Latin-American) plus virtual coverage, saving the fixed cost of 2-3 offices. (3) The recommendation is conditional: fewer than six is justified IF the expected 5-year technology level reaches the break-even where Cov(3-4 offices, tech) >= 0.95. If technology is low (translation not yet reliable for the local-language layer), the full six are needed because each local language requires on-site staffing. ANSWER TO THE CLIENT: open the full six only if real-time translation/remote collaboration is not yet at a level where one bilingual English hub can serve adjacent same-language-region markets; otherwise open 3-4 anchor offices (Lagos or Nairobi for Africa; Mumbai for South Asia, possibly + Karachi or Dhaka if Hindi and Urdu/Bengali demand diverge; Mexico City for Latin America) and cover the rest by remote/translated links, re-evaluating annually as translation technology improves. ADDITIONAL INFORMATION (as specified): current and expected translation/remote-collaboration technology level; per-market revenue by language; regulatory mandates for physical presence; office fixed/variable cost; remote-link serviceable radius. LIMITATIONS / BIAS: (1) the coverage-cost model is a trade-off frame, not a fitted cost function — the actual break-even tech level requires the company's real cost and revenue data, which are not in the problem. (2) it assumes translation technology substitutes uniformly across language pairs; in practice, translation quality varies by language (e.g. English-Spanish pairs are easier than English-Yoruba), so the consolidation benefit is larger for the high-resource language pairs. (3) it under-weights client-relationship and trust effects that may require physical presence regardless of technology — a qualitative factor the model flags but cannot quantify. (4) the 'fewer than six' conclusion is a conditional recommendation (depends on tech level), not an unconditional one, which is the honest output of the coverage-cost analysis.
