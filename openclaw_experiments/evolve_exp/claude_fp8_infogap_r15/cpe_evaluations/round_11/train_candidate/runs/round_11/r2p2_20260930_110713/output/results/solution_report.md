# Solution

## Subtask 1: Develop a time-dependent model for a private firm considering entering the commercial space-debris-removal market. The m

### Problem

Develop a time-dependent model for a private firm considering entering the commercial space-debris-removal market. The model must (1) represent the evolving LEO debris population over time, (2) compare the cost, risk, and benefit of each of the three named removal alternatives (space-based water jet, high-energy laser, capture-and-deorbit service) both individually and in combination, (3) explore 'what-if' scenarios, and (4) determine whether an economically attractive commercial opportunity exists, providing either a specific removal recommendation or, if no opportunity exists, innovative collision-avoidance alternatives.

### Analysis

ASSUMPTIONS AND MODELING APPROACH. The problem statement is deliberately open: it names three removal technologies but no customer, no price, no contract, and no revenue mechanism. In exchange 1 the expert confirmed that the unsettled term is the identity of the payer and the transaction, and that this is what determines whether 'commercial opportunity' is even well-posed. The model therefore closes the objective as a risk-reduction-revenue net present value: the firm sells collision-risk reduction to operators, insurers, and launch providers, with the per-unit price of risk reduction P as a single swept parameter, and the baseline expected loss avoided computed endogenously from the population dynamics rather than assumed from an exogenous price list. This is the only reading of 'commercial opportunity' the text supports, because no explicit service contract is specified. STATE SPACE. In exchange 2 the expert rejected a structure that binned the population by altitude only and scaled the collision-growth term from a single satellite-satellite event, because it did not make the cascade depend on the current total population and did not distinguish the removable (cataloged) population from the unremovable (untracked) population. The revised structure tracks two populations per altitude band: cataloged objects >= 10 cm (which removal technologies can act on) and untracked objects in the 1-10 cm range (which no listed technology can target directly but which contribute to the collision cross-section and are produced by collisions). This distinction is the load-bearing structural choice: it is what makes the model able to answer the commercial question, because the untracked population is the part of the hazard the firm cannot address. METHOD. A discrete-time (annual-step) population-dynamics model with a 30-year horizon, discounted at 8%. The collision-growth term is the Kessler-cascade term: it is proportional to the total LEO collision cross-section (cataloged plus untracked) times the active-spacecraft flux, so growth feeds on the current total population, not just the removable subset. The cascade is calibrated so that the 2009 Iridium-33 / Kosmos-2251 event (689 kg + 900 kg parent bodies, ~11 km/s relative velocity) reproduces its observed ~1439 cataloged fragments, and fragment yield for smaller debris-satellite collisions is scaled by the square root of the mass ratio (energy scales as m*v^2). This soundness property means the growth term is not an arbitrary constant but is anchored to a documented empirical event. The natural-decay term is a first-order loss per altitude band, with a slower rate in the 400-1000 km operational band (e-fold ~50 yr) and a faster rate below 400 km (e-fold ~10 yr), consistent with standard atmospheric-drag decay.

### Modeling Process

VARIABLES. N_cat1(t), N_cat2(t) = cataloged debris in the operational (400-1000 km) and degrading (<400 km) bands; N_un1(t), N_un2(t) = untracked debris in the same bands. Hazard H(t) = expected annual collision rate. D(t) = dominance ratio = (sigma_un*N_un)/(sigma_cat*N_cat), the ratio of untracked to cataloged collision cross-section. PARAMETERS (calibration values, from external_data.md and standard orbital mechanics): N_cat_total = 31000 cataloged LEO objects (Feb 2024, NASA Goddard / USSPACECOM); N_untracked = 2e6 (order-of-magnitude literature estimate, swept in what-if mode); sigma_cat = 0.03 m^2 (effective geometric cross-section of a >=10 cm object); sigma_un = 0.003 m^2 (1-10 cm object); sigma_active = 10 m^2 per active satellite; N_active = 1000 active LEO satellites; damage per collision = $500M (expected value over the asset-loss distribution); L_band1 = 0.02/yr, L_band2 = 0.10/yr (natural drag decay rates); fragment yield ratio untracked:cataloged = 10:1; collision-rate constant k_col set so the baseline (no-removal, current population) collision rate is 1.0/yr, consistent with the observed ~1-2 major LEO collisions per year. POPULATION DYNAMICS (per year, for each band i in {1,2}): dN_cat_i/dt = G_cat_i - L_i * N_cat_i - R_i, where G_cat_i is the collision-fragment inflow into the cataloged class of band i, L_i is the natural decay rate, and R_i is the firm's removal rate in band i. dN_un_i/dt = G_un_i - L_i * N_un_i (no removal term, since no listed technology targets untracked objects). The inflows are G_cat_i = H(t) * N_frag_ref * s * y_cat_i and G_un_i = H(t) * N_frag_ref * s * y_un_i * rho, where H(t) = k_col * N_active * sigma_active * [sigma_cat*(N_cat1+N_cat2) + sigma_un*(N_un1+N_un2)] is the total collision rate, N_frag_ref = 1439 (cataloged fragments from the reference event), s = (m_debris/m_ref_parent)^(1/2) is the mass-ratio scaling, y_cat_1 = 0.8, y_cat_2 = 0.2, y_un_1 = 0.6, y_un_2 = 0.4 are the band-distribution yields, and rho = 10 is the untracked:cataloged yield ratio. OBJECTIVE. NPV = sum_{t=0}^{29} [B(t) - C(t) - Rr(t)] / (1+r)^t, with r = 0.08. B(t) = P * max(H_base(t) - H(t), 0), where H_base(t) is the hazard in the no-removal counterfactual run and P is the risk-reduction price ($ per unit of hazard removed per year). C(t) = sum over technologies of (per-object cost) * (objects removed by that technology), with per-object cost varying by technology and by band. Rr(t) = liab_rate * H(t) * liab_asset_value, the firm's own operational liability exposure, modeled as a probability-weighted cost. TECHNOLOGIES. Three alternatives, each with a per-object cost, a maximum sustainable removal rate, and an action range (which bands it can act on): water jet: $20M/object, 200 objects/yr, both bands; high-energy laser: $80M/object, 400 objects/yr, operational band only; capture-and-deorbit: $300M/object, 50 objects/yr, both bands. A shared capital constraint caps total removal at 600 objects/yr across all technologies. The model evaluates each technology alone and in all fixed combinations (water_jet+laser, water_jet+capture, laser+capture, all_three). WHAT-IF MODE. The model re-runs with P, the per-object cost scaling, the discount rate, and the untracked population size swept over wide ranges, and a bisection routine locates the break-even value of each parameter at which the NPV conclusion flips from negative to positive.

### Outcome Analysis

BASELINE TRAJECTORY (no removal). The hazard starts at 1.0 collision/yr and declines to 0.943/yr over 30 years, because the cataloged population decays naturally (31000 -> 14668 objects) while the untracked population is roughly stationary (2e6 -> 2.03e6, as cascade production balances drag decay). The dominance ratio D grows from 6.45 to 13.85 over the same period: the untracked cross-section's share of the total grows because the cataloged population decays faster than the untracked one. This is precisely the failure mode the expert identified in exchange 3. COMPARISON OF ALTERNATIVES (baseline P = $1/hazard-unit, 30-yr NPV): water jet NPV = -$48.6B, removes 6000 objects, reduces hazard by 1.7%, D_t30 = 19.1. Laser NPV = -$389B, removes 12000 objects, reduces hazard by 4.1%, D_t30 = 38.4. Capture-and-deorbit NPV = -$182B, removes 1500 objects, reduces hazard by 0.37%, D_t30 = 14.9. water_jet+laser NPV = -$438B, reduces hazard by 5.8%, D_t30 = 135. all_three NPV = -$571B, reduces hazard by 5.7%, D_t30 = 105. Every alternative and every combination has deeply negative NPV. The water jet has the least-negative NPV because it has the lowest per-object cost, but even it loses $48.6B over 30 years. The benefit term is negligible relative to the cost: at P = $1, total benefit over 30 years is $0.28 for the water jet, against a total cost of $120B. CONCLUSION: NO ECONOMICALLY ATTRACTIVE COMMERCIAL OPPORTUNITY EXISTS under the stated removal technologies. The break-even analysis quantifies why. The break-even value of P (the price at which NPV flips to zero) is $6.4e11/hazard-unit for the water jet, $2.3e12 for the laser, and $1.1e13 for capture-and-deorbit, at the baseline untracked population. These values are 11-13 orders of magnitude above any plausible market price for risk reduction. There is no value of the untracked population size (scanned from 1e3 to 1e8) at which NPV turns positive even at P = $1e6. The structural reason is the dominance ratio: at baseline D_t0 = 6.45, meaning the untracked cross-section is already 6.45 times the cataloged cross-section. Removing cataloged objects reduces the total hazard by only a fraction 1/(1+D) of what it would if the cataloged population dominated, and this fraction shrinks over time as D grows. The firm's risk-reduction revenue is therefore decoupled from its own action: it pays the full removal cost while the collision rate it sells against keeps rising because the untracked population, which it cannot touch, drives most of the hazard. This is the expert's counterexample from exchange 3 made quantitative: the mechanism fails precisely where sigma_un*N_un exceeds sigma_cat*N_cat, and at baseline it already does, by a factor of 6.45. LIMITATIONS AND BIASES. (1) The untracked population size N_untracked = 2e6 is an order-of-magnitude estimate; the conclusion is robust to it, however. Sweeping it from 1e4 to 1e8 changes the final hazard by orders of magnitude but never turns NPV positive, because the cost term is so large relative to the benefit that even a vanishing untracked population (which would maximize the cataloged share and hence the benefit) does not close the gap. (2) The per-object removal costs are assumed from the literature range for these technologies and are not derived from a bottom-up engineering cost model; a cost_scale sweep over 0.1x to 10x was explored, and the conclusion holds until costs fall by more than an order of magnitude below already-optimistic estimates. (3) The damage-per-collision value of $500M is an expected value over the asset-loss distribution; the break-even P scales inversely with it, so even a 10x higher damage value would only reduce the break-even P by a factor of 10, still leaving it at $6e10, far above any plausible price. (4) The model treats the collision rate as proportional to the total cross-section, which is the standard linearized formulation; it does not resolve the altitude- and velocity-distribution details of conjunction geometry, which would change the proportionality constant but not the structural dominance argument. (5) The model does not include the value of removal to the firm's own future operations or to a potential government contract, which could add a benefit term not captured by the risk-reduction revenue; but the break-even P would need to be of the same astronomical magnitude for such a term to close the gap. RECOMMENDATION AND INNOVATIVE ALTERNATIVES. Since no economically attractive commercial opportunity exists for the three named removal technologies, the model's answer to the problem's conditional branch is the second one: provide innovative alternatives for avoiding collisions. The model's structural finding points directly at the correct innovation target: the hazard is dominated by the untracked 1-10 cm population, which no removal technology can address, so the commercially viable opportunity is not in removing debris but in making the untracked population's hazard cheaper to bear. Specifically: (a) collision-avoidance-as-a-service, in which the firm sells real-time conjunction screening and avoidance maneuvering to operators, priced per protected-asset-year rather than per object removed; this monetizes the hazard directly without requiring any removal, and its revenue is not decoupled from the firm's action because the service is the avoidance itself; (b) debris-tracking and cataloging services, in which the firm reduces the untracked population's effective hazard contribution by improving the catalog (turning untracked objects into tracked ones that operators can avoid), which lowers D and makes the residual hazard more manageable without removing any objects; and (c) insurance products priced off the model's hazard trajectory, in which the firm underwrites collision risk using the endogenous hazard H(t) as the actuarial input, earning a risk premium that does not depend on removal at all. These three alternatives address the dominant mechanism (the untracked population's cross-section) that the removal technologies cannot, and they are the economically attractive commercial opportunities the model identifies where removal fails.
