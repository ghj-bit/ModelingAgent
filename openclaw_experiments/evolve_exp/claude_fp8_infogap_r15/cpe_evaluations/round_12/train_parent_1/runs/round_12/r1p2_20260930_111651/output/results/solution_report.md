# Solution

## Subtask 1: Develop an emergency evacuation model for the Louvre that allows museum leaders to explore a range of options to evacuat

### Problem

Develop an emergency evacuation model for the Louvre that allows museum leaders to explore a range of options to evacuate visitors as quickly and safely as possible while also allowing emergency personnel to enter as fast as possible. Identify potential bottlenecks that limit movement toward the exits. The model must be adaptable to a broad set of considerations and threat types, where each threat can alter or remove segments of routes to safety. Scope: a parameterized, reproducible egress model over the Louvre's five floors (two underground, ~72,735 m^2, wings up to 480 m) and its four main public entrances (Pyramid, Passage Richelieu, Carrousel du Louvre, Portes Des Lions) plus staff-known auxiliary emergency/service exits.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) Objective: T is the completion time of the last occupant, the standard reading of 'all occupants leave as quickly and safely as possible'; partial objectives (95%) would be an added assumption not in the text (settled by expert, Exchange 1). (2) Two-way flow: the domain admits concurrent opposing egress and emergency-entry on shared links, so the model carries a two-way flow penalty rather than assuming egress and entry do not interact (settled by expert, Exchange 1; phased separation is a convenience the statement does not authorize). (3) Threat degradation: a threat may remove any graph element — nodes (floors/wings), links (corridors/stairwells), and exits (per the problem's security discussion of exits) — and T is re-solved on the degraded graph (settled by expert, Exchange 1). (4) Population: 20,000 occupants (a plausible peak for a large day at the Louvre, which sees 8.1 M visitors/year; sensitivity-swept 10,000-30,000). (5) Population classes and fractions (stated assumption, sensitivity-swept): tourist 55%, family 25%, ambulant-disabled 12%, wheelchair 3%, staff 5%. (6) Class speeds (SFPE Handbook / Fruin): tourist 1.34 m/s (SD 0.25), family 1.15 m/s, ambulant-disabled 0.94 m/s (SD 0.30), wheelchair 0.50 m/s (midpoint of 0.36-0.69), staff 1.40 m/s. (7) Doorway specific-flow calibration: 1.3 persons/s per m of effective width, saturating at ~0.6 m (NIST / Song et al.; Jo & Koshimizu 2014). (8) Free-flow speed 1.34 m/s design mean, swept 0.85-1.85 m/s (SFPE). Method: a network-flow-with-queue (capacity-allocation) model rather than a macroscopic continuity PDE. A PDE would capture mid-corridor density waves but is over-parameterized for the data available and would not expose the per-exit capacity limits that drive the policy; a capacity-allocation model directly exposes exit and corridor capacities, which is what the 'identify bottlenecks' and 'exit-sizing' deliverables need. The method is sound because egress in a large museum is throughput-limited by doors and corridors (the specific-flow calibration), not by mid-corridor jam dynamics, and the allocation form makes the bottleneck set a direct output. The model is class-resolved (T = max over classes of each class's completion time), which addresses the expert's Exchange 3 counterexample (c): a mean-speed aggregate would mis-estimate the tail, and the safety deliverable is about the slowest people leaving, not the average person.

### Modeling Process

Graph G = (V, E). Nodes V are six floor hubs (U2, U1, G, 1, 2, 3 — the two underground levels U2/U1, ground G, and upper 1/2/3) plus a shared core node. Links E are floor-to-core corridors (effective width 8 m, lengths 150-300 m) and floor-to-exit links. Exits: pyramid (3.0 m), passage_richelieu (2.5 m), carrousel (2.5 m), portes_lions (2.5 m), and auxiliary exits aux_east/aux_west/aux_north (1.2 m each). Floor areas (m^2): U2 12000, U1 14000, G 16000, 1 14000, 2 10000, 3 6735 (sum 72,735). Exit capacity: for effective width w, C_exit(w) = 1.3w for w <= 0.6 m; C_exit(w) = 1.3*0.6*(1 + 0.5*ln(w/0.6)) for w > 0.6 m (sub-linear saturation). Corridor capacity: C_corridor = min(C_exit(8 m), v*8 m) where v is free speed; at v = 1.34 m/s this is ~10.7 persons/s, below the exit pool so exits bind. Per floor f, egress splits into two channels: direct_f <= C_direct[f] (f's own exits) and relief_f <= C_corridor[f] (f's corridor to the core). Relief is routed to other floors' exits by area/capacity share R[f][g]; the total through each receiving exit g is capped by C_direct[g], so global exit capacities are never exceeded (no double-counting). This is the bottleneck-migration mechanism: a floor whose exits are removed migrates its flow to other floors' exits. Per-class discharge rate: floor f discharges class c at rate C_f * (v_c / 1.34) * (1 - beta*f_opp), where v_c is the class's mean speed and f_opp is the opposing-flow fraction (two-way penalty, beta = 0.5). The two-way penalty models concurrent emergency entry: f_opp = min(1, (emergency_entry * area_f/total_area) / (C_direct[f] + C_corridor[f])). Solution procedure: time-step the per-class, per-floor queues (dt = 1 s, max 3600 s); each step, each floor's queue discharges into direct and relief channels up to the channel caps and the class's effective rate; T = max over classes of the time at which that class's total queue reaches zero. Bottlenecks = floors with the most saturation (queue wanting to discharge faster than the floor's capacity) at the completion time. Threat degradation: a threat is a set of removed graph elements (node, link, or exit); re-solve T on the degraded graph and report delta-T and the new bottleneck set. Constants are command-line configurable: python evac_model.py --sweep SPEED=0.85,1.34,1.85 | --sweep POP=10000,30000 | --sweep EMERG=0,10 | --threat remove_exit:pyramid.

### Outcome Analysis

Base case (20,000 occupants, no threat): T = 638 s = 10.6 min. Per-class completion times: staff 55 s (0.9 min), wheelchair 93 s (1.6 min), ambulant-disabled 198 s (3.3 min), family 338 s (5.6 min), tourist 638 s (10.6 min). The tail (last occupant) is the tourist class at the base population; the slowest class (wheelchair) completes early because it is a small fraction, but its per-capita travel time is the longest — the class-resolved T makes the tail explicit. Base bottlenecks: floors U2, U1, 2 (the underground levels and the top floor, which have the fewest/ smallest direct exits). Threat battery (20,000 occupants, base T = 638 s): remove pyramid exit: T = 677 s (+39 s, +0.7 min), bottlenecks migrate to {1, U2, G}; remove passage_richelieu: T = 662 s (+24 s); remove carrousel: T = 745 s (+107 s); remove portes_lions: T = 844 s (+206 s, +3.4 min), bottlenecks {2, U2, G}; remove top floor (node 2): T = 740 s (+102 s); remove ground corridor to core (link G|core): T = 638 s (+0 s, G still drains via its direct exits). Compound threat (pyramid + portes_lions removed): T = 851 s (+213 s), bottlenecks migrate to {G, 2, 1} — demonstrating bottleneck migration: removing two exits shifts the binding constraint to different floors. Sensitivity: free-flow speed 0.85 -> 1.85 m/s gives T = 1007 s -> 462 s (roughly inversely proportional to speed, as expected for a throughput-limited system); population 10,000 -> 30,000 gives T = 319 s -> 958 s (roughly proportional to population, confirming the system is capacity-limited, not congestion-limited, in this range); concurrent emergency entry 0 -> 10 persons/s gives T = 638 s -> 900 s (the two-way penalty costs up to +262 s at high entry rates, confirming the expert's Exchange 1 ruling that opposing flows must be modeled concurrently). Model limitations and biases. (1) Class aggregation hiding the last occupant — the expert's Exchange 3 counterexample: if classes were aggregated into a mean speed, the tail would be mis-estimated; the model avoids this by class-resolving T, but the class fractions are a stated assumption, so the tail's class (and hence T) is only as good as the fraction assumption. (2) Graph abstraction vs. real geometry: the five-floor graph lumps up-to-480-m wings into single corridor links with one capacity; a mid-corridor bottleneck (gallery junction, narrow passage) is not represented, so the model can be right about total T and wrong about where the crowd jams — the 'where to intervene' output is limited by link granularity. (3) Exit-level contention is approximated: relief routed through a receiving exit is capped by that exit's capacity, but the contention is allocated by area/capacity share, not solved as a full transport problem; a more granular model would route relief by shortest residual path. (4) Two-way penalty is linear (1 - beta*f_opp); real two-way flow interactions are non-linear and can be more severe at high opposing fractions, so the model may under-estimate T at high emergency-entry rates. (5) No stochasticity: class speeds use means, not the SFPE SDs; a Monte-Carlo over the speed distributions would widen the T estimate. Policy and procedural recommendations (mechanically derived from the model outputs, per the expert's Exchange 1 ruling that the policy deliverable must be derived, not free text). (i) Exit allocation: the model's per-scenario bottleneck set identifies which exits to keep open under each threat; e.g., under a portes_lions closure, pre-position the relief flow through the pyramid and carrousel (the compound-threat bottleneck set {G, 2, 1} shows where the load lands). (ii) Bottleneck mitigation: the saturated floors (U2, U1, 2 in the base case) map to physical interventions — widen the auxiliary exits on the underground levels, add a staff-known emergency exit on the top floor, or pre-position staff to meter flow. (iii) Crowd management: the class-wise delay penalty triggers procedural rules — the wheelchair class (0.50 m/s, ~2.7x slower than tourist) and ambulant-disabled class (0.94 m/s) each trigger an escort-assignment rule and a priority-corridor rule so the slowest people are not left to self-organize at the tail; the model's per-class completion times show the wheelchair class finishes at 1.6 min only because it is a small fraction, so the escort must be staged at notification, not at congestion. (iv) Technology: the Affluences-style real-time entrance-waiting app generalizes to a real-time egress-waiting display at each floor, showing the model's per-exit residual capacity to guide visitor routing (the relief routing R[f][g] is the allocation the app would publish). (v) Emergency entry: the two-way penalty shows concurrent entry costs up to +262 s at 10 persons/s; the recommendation is to time emergency entry to arrive after the first ~6 min (when the fastest classes have cleared the main corridors), or to use dedicated entry routes that do not share links with the dominant egress flow — the model's two-way term quantifies the cost of not doing so. Adaptation to other large crowded structures (the one deliverable the expert allowed as a qualitative extension): the transferable parameters are the specific-flow calibration (1.3 p/s per m, saturating at 0.6 m — a property of doors, not buildings), the free-flow speed distribution (a property of people, not buildings), the two-way penalty, and the class speed distributions; the building-specific parameters are the floor areas, exit widths, corridor lengths, and the floor-to-exit adjacency, which must be re-measured for each structure. The model form (capacity allocation with class-resolved T and node/link/exit degradation) is the reusable skeleton; only the graph and the width/area constants change.
