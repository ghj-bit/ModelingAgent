## Subtask 1: Develop a macroscopic emergency-evacuation model for the Louvre that (a) computes time-to-empty for a heterogeneous crow

### Problem

Develop a macroscopic emergency-evacuation model for the Louvre that (a) computes time-to-empty for a heterogeneous crowd as a function of total occupancy N, (b) identifies the binding bottleneck at each exit, and (c) reports a Pareto frontier trading off evacuation speed (T_empty) against emergency-responder arrival time (T_responder). The model must treat total occupancy N as a swept planning parameter (not derived from the 8.1 M annual visitor figure) and must cover all occupants (visitors plus staff).

### Analysis

Assumptions grounded by the expert consultation: (1) N is a planning parameter swept over {3000, 8000, 12000} occupants, representing low, mid, and peak-day conditions, including staff; (2) the objective is a two-objective trade-off between T_empty and T_responder, reported as a Pareto frontier, not a single scalar; (3) the four main public entrances (Pyramid, Passage Richelieu, Carrousel du Louvre, Portes Des Lions) are always available; service/secret exits form a contingent reserve closed by default and released only after a security-release decision; (4) a threat is a structural edge/node deletion set, and adaptability means re-optimization stays fast and feasible (or is explicitly flagged infeasible). The modeling approach is a three-layer macroscopic network model: a floor-stratified cell network (geometry), LWR / triangular fundamental-diagram flow dynamics with explicit door-bottleneck capacity conversion (flow), and a decision layer that assigns each floor to its time-weighted nearest available exit and reserves a protected responder corridor (decision). This is sound because it captures the dominant loss mechanism — the capacity-to-flow conversion at each door — which idealized 'everyone walks at free speed' models ignore, and it produces a non-degenerate Pareto frontier by making the responder corridor a genuine capacity trade-off rather than a hard constraint.

### Modeling Process

Network: 5 floor hubs (F-2, F-1, F0, F1, F2) each connected to 4 wing-segment nodes by wing edges (length 120 m, width 8 m). Inter-floor stair edges connect adjacent floor hubs (length 15 m, width 2.4 m, 4 banks per level). Exits: 4 public entrances on the ground floor (Pyramid 6 m, Passage Richelieu 4 m, Carrousel du Louvre 4 m, Portes Des Lions 4 m) and 4 reserve exits on various floors (Service B2 1.8 m, Service B1 2.0 m, Service 1st 2.0 m, VIP roof 2.2 m). Each door has capacity C = alpha * w persons/s with alpha = 1.3 (NIST). Flow: each edge carries a density governed by a triangular fundamental diagram with v_f = 1.34 m/s (healthy adults), v_f = 0.94 m/s (disabled), rho_jam = 4 persons/m^2; outflow = min(v * rho * dx, C). Routing: Dijkstra with edge time = length / v_route (v_route = 1.0 m/s nominal half-density speed); each floor hub is assigned to its time-weighted nearest open exit. Decision layer: (1) exit assignment per floor; (2) T_empty = max over exits of (max travel time of floors assigned to that exit + total load at that exit / door capacity); (3) responder corridor: a protected path of width w_c (m) reserved on the stairs between the ground floor and the incident floor. The corridor takes fraction w_c/2.4 of stair width, reducing egress capacity by that fraction and giving responders a faster path: responder speed = v_f * (0.5 + 0.5 * w_c/2.4). T_responder = (number of stairs * 15 m) / responder_speed + 10 s approach. Pareto frontier: sweep w_c in {0.0, 0.6, 1.2, 1.8, 2.4} and reserve release in {0,1,2,3}, collect (T_empty, T_responder) pairs, and return the non-dominated set. Threat: a deletion set of edges/nodes; re-run the assignment and report the new T_empty and the delta, or flag INFEASIBLE if a floor is disconnected.

### Outcome Analysis

Pareto frontier (N=8000, incident floor 1): (803.8 s, 32.4 s), (813.8 s, 27.9 s), (833.8 s, 21.2 s). The trade-off is clear: a wider responder corridor (w_c from 0 to 2.4 m) reduces T_responder from 32.4 s to 21.2 s but increases T_empty from 803.8 s to 833.8 s (the reserved stair capacity slows egress). The knee of the frontier is at w_c = 0.6 m (T_empty = 813.8 s, T_responder = 27.9 s), which is the recommended operating point: it buys a 4.5 s responder speed-up for only a 10 s evacuation cost. Per-N results (w_c = 1.2 m, reserve release 0..3): N=3000 gives T_empty 410.8-626.5 s; N=8000 gives 833.8-1395.8 s; N=12000 gives 1175.6-2011.2 s. T_empty scales roughly linearly with N, confirming the door-capacity-limited regime. Bottleneck identification: at N=3000 the binding bottleneck is the Pyramid (main entrance, 6 m, highest single-door capacity); at N=8000 and N=12000 the bottleneck shifts to the Service B2 (secret) exit when reserve release >= 2, because the B2 reserve exit (1.8 m) becomes the discharge path for the underground floors and its narrow width caps the flow. This is a critical policy finding: at high occupancy, opening the narrow B2 reserve exit does not help and can worsen the bottleneck because it draws load away from the wider public exits. Threat adaptability (N=8000): removing the Pyramid exit increases T_empty by 42.5% (1205.6 s to 1718.5 s) and shifts the bottleneck to Passage Richelieu; removing one stair has no effect (redundant 4-bank design); isolating the B2 floor (removing all B2 stairs and the B2 reserve exit) makes the model INFEASIBLE — the B2 floor is disconnected from every exit, so the adaptability metric is undefined rather than merely large. This is the Exchange-3 counterexample made explicit in the model: the model reports infeasibility as a first-class state, not a large T_empty. Limitations and biases: (1) the network is a representative abstraction, not the actual Louvre geometry; wing lengths (120 m) and corridor widths (8 m) are scaled to the 480 m wing and 72,735 m^2 area figures but do not capture the real floor plan; (2) the uniform floor-occupancy assumption ignores that visitors cluster on certain floors and in certain wings; (3) the triangular fundamental diagram uses a single v_f per population class and does not model the speed-density feedback of a heterogeneous crowd (disabled and able-bodied mixing); (4) the responder corridor penalty is applied to all stair crossings, not just the specific stair the corridor occupies, which overestimates the egress cost of a narrow corridor; (5) the model does not capture crowd psychology, panic, or route-preference changes under threat, which the expert explicitly excluded from the adaptability definition. The infeasibility state (disconnected floor) is the model's most important structural limitation: it means the adaptability metric is undefined for that threat, and a real evacuation plan must have a contingency for the disconnected subpopulation (e.g., pre-positioned responders, vertical lifelines, or a second egress path per floor).

## Subtask 2: Identify potential bottlenecks that may limit movement towards the exits, and determine how the model's bottleneck ident

### Problem

Identify potential bottlenecks that may limit movement towards the exits, and determine how the model's bottleneck identification changes with occupancy N and reserve-exit release.

### Analysis

Bottlenecks are identified by computing, for each open exit, the completion time = max travel time of floors assigned to that exit + total load / door capacity, and taking the exit with the maximum completion time. The bottleneck is the exit whose discharge (not travel) time is longest. This is sound because in a capacity-limited regime, the exit with the smallest door capacity relative to its assigned load will empty last, and it is this exit that limits the whole building's evacuation. The analysis sweeps N over {3000, 8000, 12000} and reserve release over {0,1,2,3} to see how the bottleneck shifts.

### Modeling Process

For each exit e with door width w_e and assigned load L_e (sum of occupants of floors routed to e), the discharge time is L_e / (alpha * w_e) with alpha = 1.3 persons/s/m. The completion time is max_travel_e + L_e / (alpha * w_e). The bottleneck is argmax over e of completion time. The discharge time (not travel) is the binding term at high N because travel times are similar across exits (all floors route to the nearest exit) while discharge times diverge as 1/w_e.

### Outcome Analysis

At N=3000, the bottleneck is the Pyramid (main, 6 m) at all reserve-release levels: its discharge time is longest because it carries the largest load (all 5 floors' ground-level occupants route to the nearest public exit, and the Pyramid is the widest and thus attracts the most). At N=8000 and N=12000 with reserve release >= 2, the bottleneck shifts to the Service B2 (secret, 1.8 m) exit: the B2 reserve exit, when opened, becomes the nearest exit for the underground floors and its narrow 1.8 m width caps the flow to about 2.34 persons/s, which is slower than the public exits. This is a counterintuitive finding: opening a reserve exit can worsen the bottleneck by drawing load to a narrow door. Policy implication: reserve exits should be released selectively, and only if their width is comparable to the public exits they are supplementing. At N=3000 the reserve exits are too small to matter; at high N they become the bottleneck if opened. The 4-bank stair design is robust: removing one stair has zero effect on T_empty (the remaining 3 banks carry the load), confirming that the stairs are not the binding bottleneck in the base case. The bottleneck identification assumes each floor routes to a single exit (its nearest). In reality, floors near multiple exits may split their load, which would smooth the bottleneck but also change which exit is binding. The model's all-or-nothing assignment is a simplification that makes the bottleneck more pronounced than it would be under optimal load-splitting.

## Subtask 3: Model the two-objective trade-off between fast egress (minimize T_empty) and fast emergency-responder entry (minimize T_

### Problem

Model the two-objective trade-off between fast egress (minimize T_empty) and fast emergency-responder entry (minimize T_responder), and report the Pareto frontier that lets museum leaders explore the range of options.

### Analysis

Per the expert's Exchange-1 ruling, the two objectives are reported as a Pareto frontier, not as a hard constraint. The decision variable that creates the trade-off is the width of the protected responder corridor (w_c): a wider corridor gives responders a faster, less-congested path (lower T_responder) but reserves more stair capacity for responders, reducing egress capacity (higher T_empty). The Pareto frontier is the set of (T_empty, T_responder) pairs that are not dominated by any other pair in the sweep. This is sound because it makes the trade-off explicit and lets the museum leaders choose the operating point based on their risk tolerance.

### Modeling Process

The responder corridor occupies width w_c of the 2.4 m stair width between the ground floor and the incident floor. The fraction of stair width reserved is w_c / 2.4. Responder speed = v_f * (0.5 + 0.5 * w_c/2.4), interpolating from half free-speed (w_c=0, shared with evacuees) to full free-speed (w_c=2.4, dedicated corridor). T_responder = (abs(incident_floor) * 15 m) / responder_speed + 10 s. The egress penalty: each stair crossing on the reserved stair is slowed by a factor 1 / (1 - w_c/2.4) (for w_c < 2.4) or is fully blocked (w_c = 2.4, +100% delay). T_empty is recomputed with the slowed stair travel times. The Pareto frontier is the non-dominated set over w_c in {0.0, 0.6, 1.2, 1.8, 2.4} and reserve release in {0,1,2,3}.

### Outcome Analysis

The Pareto frontier at N=8000, incident floor 1 has three points: (803.8 s, 32.4 s) at w_c=0 (no corridor, responders share egress space), (813.8 s, 27.9 s) at w_c=0.6 m, and (833.8 s, 21.2 s) at w_c=2.4 m (full dedicated corridor). The frontier is nearly flat in T_empty (803.8 to 833.8 s, a 3.7% increase) while T_responder drops from 32.4 s to 21.2 s (a 34.6% decrease). This means the cost of protecting the responder corridor is small relative to the benefit: a full 2.4 m dedicated corridor buys 11.2 s of responder speed-up for only 30 s of extra evacuation time. The recommended operating point is w_c = 2.4 m (full dedicated corridor) because the evacuation cost is small and the responder benefit is large. However, at high N (12000), the absolute T_empty cost is larger (1175.6 s to 2011.2 s across reserve releases), so the museum may prefer w_c = 1.2 m (T_empty = 1410.8 s, T_responder = 24.9 s) as a compromise. The Pareto frontier also reveals that the reserve-exit release decision interacts with the corridor width: at w_c=0, releasing 3 reserve exits worsens T_empty (1365.8 s) because the narrow reserve exits become bottlenecks, while at w_c=2.4, releasing 2 reserve exits is optimal (833.8 s). The corridor penalty is applied to all stair crossings between the ground floor and the incident floor, not just the specific stair the corridor occupies. In a real building with multiple stair banks, only one stair is reserved, so the egress penalty is smaller than modeled. This overestimates the cost of the corridor and makes the Pareto frontier more conservative (favoring narrower corridors) than it should be. The responder speed interpolation (0.5 to 1.0 * v_f) is a heuristic; in reality, responders in a narrow corridor with evacuees may be slower than half free-speed due to push-back.

### Problem

Model the adaptability of the evacuation plan to threats that remove route segments (exits, stairs, wings), and quantify how much each threat degrades T_empty or makes the plan infeasible.

### Analysis

Per the expert's Exchange-1 ruling, a threat is a structural edge/node deletion set, and adaptability means re-optimization stays fast and feasible. The model applies a catalog of representative threats (remove one stair, remove the Pyramid exit, remove the Pyramid wing, isolate the B2 floor) and reports the new T_empty and the delta relative to the no-threat baseline. Per the Exchange-3 counterexample, if a threat disconnects a floor from every exit, the model reports INFEASIBLE rather than a large T_empty, because the adaptability metric (T_empty delta) is undefined for a disconnected subpopulation. This is the model's most important structural limitation, made explicit.

### Modeling Process

A threat is a triple (removed_stairs, removed_wings, removed_exits). The model applies the deletions to the network, re-runs the Dijkstra routing and the assignment, and computes the new T_empty. The delta = (T_empty_threat - T_empty_base) / T_empty_base. If any floor hub has no reachable exit (Dijkstra returns inf), the result is INFEASIBLE with a note identifying the disconnected floor. The threat catalog: (1) no_threat (baseline); (2) one_stair (remove one of the 4 stair banks between F0 and F1); (3) pyramid_removed (remove the Pyramid exit); (4) pyramid_wing_removed (remove the Pyramid wing edge and the Pyramid exit); (5) isolate_B2 (remove all 4 stair edges between B2 and B1 and the B2 reserve exit).

### Outcome Analysis

At N=8000: (1) no_threat: T_empty = 1205.6 s (baseline). (2) one_stair: T_empty = 1205.6 s, delta = 0.0 — the 4-bank stair design is fully redundant; removing one stair has zero effect. (3) pyramid_removed: T_empty = 1718.5 s, delta = +42.5% — losing the widest public exit is the most damaging single-exit removal; the bottleneck shifts to Passage Richelieu. (4) pyramid_wing_removed: T_empty = 1718.5 s, delta = +42.5% — same as removing the exit alone, because the wing edge is not the binding constraint. (5) isolate_B2: INFEASIBLE, 'floor -2 disconnected' — removing all B2 stairs and the B2 reserve exit disconnects the B2 floor from every exit. The model correctly flags this as infeasible rather than reporting a large T_empty. This is the Exchange-3 counterexample: the model's adaptability claim ('re-optimization stays fast and feasible') has a boundary condition where it fails — the re-optimization is not merely slower but impossible, and the model must represent the unreachable subpopulation explicitly. Policy implication: every underground floor must have at least two independent egress paths (e.g., two separate stair banks to different exits), so that no single threat can disconnect it. The B2 floor in the current design has only one reserve exit and shares its stairs with B1; a threat that removes both the stairs and the reserve exit isolates B2. The model's INFEASIBLE state is the basis for the redundancy recommendation. The threat catalog is small and does not cover correlated threats (e.g., a fire that removes an exit and its adjacent wing simultaneously) or partial degradations (e.g., a stair that is smoke-filled and thus slower but not removed). The model treats threats as binary deletions; a more realistic model would have continuous degradation parameters. The infeasibility detection is based on graph connectivity; in reality, a floor may be 'connected' in the graph but have a path that is too slow or too dangerous (smoke, fire) to be usable. The model does not capture this.

### Problem

Discuss how the model can be adapted and implemented for other large, crowded structures (stadiums, malls, airports, transit hubs).

### Analysis

The model's three-layer structure is general: the geometry layer (floor-stratified cell network) can be re-parameterized for any building by changing the floor count, wing layout, and exit set; the flow layer (LWR / fundamental diagram) is building-agnostic and uses the same speed-density relation; the decision layer (time-weighted nearest-exit assignment, responder corridor, Pareto frontier, threat re-optimization) is also building-agnostic. The key parameters that change per building are: floor count and area (determines N and wing lengths), exit widths and locations (determines capacities), and the responder entry points (determines the corridor paths). The model's adaptability (threat re-optimization with an INFEASIBLE state) is a general feature that any evacuation plan needs.

### Modeling Process

To adapt the model to a new building: (1) build the network: create floor hubs and wing nodes for the new building's layout, with edge lengths and widths from the floor plan; (2) set the exits: list the public exits and reserve exits with their widths; (3) set the occupancy range: sweep N over the building's typical, peak, and extreme occupancy; (4) set the responder entry points: identify the nearest perimeter entrances for each floor; (5) run the same simulation, Pareto frontier, and threat catalog. The code is parameterized so that only the network construction and the N sweep need to change.

### Outcome Analysis

The model generalizes to other large crowded structures with the following caveats: (1) Stadiums: the floor-stratified single-hub-per-floor assumption is a poor fit for a stadium, which is a single large bowl with multiple tiers. The model would need to be extended to a ring-based or sector-based network. The fundamental diagram and door-capacity model still apply, but the routing would be radial, not floor-stratified. (2) Malls: a mall is typically 2-3 floors with open plan and many small exits. The model applies directly; the key change is the exit set (many narrow exits rather than a few wide ones) and the occupancy distribution (shoppers cluster in food courts and anchor stores, not uniformly). (3) Airports: an airport is a single large floor with many gates and a security checkpoint that acts as a bottleneck. The model applies with the checkpoint as an additional bottleneck edge; the responder corridor is less relevant (responders enter from the tarmac or service entrances). (4) Transit hubs: a transit hub has multiple levels and high peak-hour occupancy. The model applies directly; the key challenge is the time-varying occupancy (trains arrive in bursts), which the model's steady-state assumption does not capture. General implementation: the model's code is structured so that the network construction is a separate function (build_louvre_network); replacing it with build_stadium_network, build_mall_network, etc., and adjusting the N sweep and exit set, is sufficient. The flow, routing, decision, and threat layers are unchanged. The INFEASIBLE state and the Pareto frontier are general features that any building's evacuation plan needs. The model assumes a static network (no time-varying topology) and a steady-state occupancy (no bursts or waves). Buildings with highly time-varying occupancy (transit hubs, stadiums with entry/exit waves) need a time-dependent extension. The model also assumes all occupants are on the same floor at the start of the evacuation; in a stadium, occupants are in the stands, not on the floor, and the 'floor' abstraction needs to be reinterpreted as 'tier' or 'section'. The fundamental diagram parameters (v_f, rho_jam) are the same for all buildings, but the door capacity constant alpha = 1.3 persons/s/m may need to be recalibrated for different door types (automatic vs. manual, sliding vs. hinged).
