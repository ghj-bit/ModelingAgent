## Subtask 1: Task 1 — Ecological framework, assumptions, and the energy-expenditure / caloric-intake model for the three dragons (the

### Problem

Task 1 — Ecological framework, assumptions, and the energy-expenditure / caloric-intake model for the three dragons (the problem's first two minimum requirements: 'What is the ecological impact and requirements of the dragons? What are the energy expenditures of the dragons, and what are their caloric intake requirements?'). Scope: a single governing energy-balance mechanism, anchored on the given biology (hatchling ~10 kg, ~30-40 kg after a year, continued growth), parameterized per life stage and climate, with every assumption tied to a physical constraint.

### Analysis

Assumptions, each mapped to a physical constraint: (A1) dragons are warm-blooded endotherms with avian-like flight, so maintenance energy follows Kleiber's allometric law BMR = 3.4 * M^0.75 W (M in kg; equivalently 70 * M^0.75 kcal/day), the standard allometric scaling for endotherms (data item 1); the exponent is treated as a sensitivity parameter (0.67-1.0) because the 0.73 vs 0.75 debate shifts predicted rates by up to ~26% for a 100 kg animal, so the exponent, not the 0.75 value, is the structural quantity. (A2) field metabolic rate = BMR x ACT with ACT = 2.0, a field-to-basal multiplier in the range of free-living mammals, covering flight and foraging activity; ACT is swept 1.5-2.5. (A3) the diet is large-vertebrate prey (livestock-sized), consistent with the 'breath fire, resist tremendous trauma' apex-predator framing; prey metabolizable energy is 2000 kcal/kg live mass (cattle-based benchmark, data item 2, scaled to a carnivore). (A4) assimilation: the two trophic transfers (net primary productivity -> herbivore prey -> dragon) each proceed at E1 = E2 = 10% (within the stated 5-20% band); the product, not the sum, governs area. (A5) growth energy: a fixed fraction EF_G = 0.40 of surplus energy (intake minus maintenance) is partitioned to mass accretion at 3800 kcal/kg, so growth is an output of the energy balance, not a parallel model. (A6) the governing constraint, declared per the expert consultation: area is the equilibrium energy-closure outcome (prey-carrying-capacity area that closes the dragon's annual energy deficit over a full annual cycle), and 'assistance' is a single parameter alpha in {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} splitting the prey deficit between community-assisted production (alpha) and wild forage (1-alpha). The problem asks both 'how much area is required' (closure reading) and 'how large a community ... for varying levels of assistance' (design variable); the two-stage structure (Stage A closure -> Stage B assistance parameterization) reconciles both. Method soundness: a mass -> energy -> biomass -> area chain is the standard trophic-cascade closure used in predator carrying-capacity models; it is closed (no free parameters per climate), monotone in M, and every constant is either given, anchored to the supplied data, or flagged as an assumption to sweep.

### Modeling Process

Stage A (equilibrium closure). Per dragon of mass M in climate c: (1) BMR(M) = 3.4 * M^BETA [W], BETA = 0.75; (2) E_ann(M, c) = BMR(M) * ACT * THERM(c) * 365 * 86400 / 1000 * 0.239006 [kcal/yr], with THERM(c) the climate thermal multiplier (1.00 arid, 1.00 temperate, 1.35 arctic); (3) prey requirement P(M, c) = E_ann / PREY_KCAL [kg prey/yr], PREY_KCAL = 2000 kcal/kg. Stage B (assistance). With alpha: wild-forage share P_wild = (1-alpha) * P, assisted share P_ass = alpha * P. (4) Required wild area A(alpha, M, c) = P_wild / (CAP(c) * E1 * E2) [km^2], where CAP(c) is the herbivore standing-biomass carrying capacity per km^2 (arid 40, temperate 250, arctic 25 kg/km^2) and E1*E2 = 0.01 the two trophic transfers; alpha = 0 gives the pure-forage area the problem's 'area required' asks for. (5) Required community N(alpha, M, c) = P_ass / (CAPITA(c) * HUM_EFF) [people], CAPITA(c) = net meat surplus per assisting person per year (arid 400, temperate 800, arctic 300 kg/yr), HUM_EFF = 0.20 agricultural-surplus efficiency. Life stages: hatchling M = 10 kg, yearling M = 35 kg, adult M = 2000 kg (the 2 t adult is the stated upper extrapolation of 'grow throughout their life'; flagged as an assumption). Growth dynamics: dM/dt = EF_G * (intake - maintenance) / 3800 [kg/day], run per climate until M = 40 kg, giving juvenile growth pace as an output. For the three dragons: multiply per-dragon requirements by 3 (areas sum at the same alpha; populations sum).

### Outcome Analysis

Per-dragon results (base run, BETA = 0.75, ACT = 2.0): annual energy — hatchling 2.88e5 kcal (arid/temperate), 3.89e5 (arctic); yearling 7.38e5, 9.96e5 (arctic); adult 1.53e7, 2.07e7 (arctic). Prey requirement — adult: 7664 kg/yr temperate/arid, 10347 kg/yr arctic (about two medium cattle per day for one adult dragon). Pure-forage area (alpha = 0), one dragon — hatchling 0.58 / 360 / 778 km^2 (temperate/arid/arctic); yearling 148 / 922 / 1991 km^2; adult 3066 / 19161 / 41387 km^2. For the three dragons at alpha = 0 the pure-forage area is ~9198 km^2 (temperate, adults), ~57482 km^2 (arid), ~124160 km^2 (arctic) — roughly a small county to a small state. Community at full assistance (alpha = 1), three adult dragons: 144 (temperate), 288 (arid), 516 (arctic) people whose net agricultural surplus closes the deficit; at alpha = 0.5 the community halves and the wild area halves as well, tracing the area-community trade-off. Climate matters through two independent channels: THERM(c) raises demand in the arctic (therm 1.35 gives +35% adult energy) and CAP(c) lowers supply (arctic 25 vs temperate 250 kg/km^2 is a 10x area penalty), so the temperate:arid:arctic area ratio for one adult is ~1 : 6.3 : 13.5 — moving a dragon from arid to arctic multiplies the required area ~7x at identical mass. Limitations: the 2 t adult mass is extrapolated, not given; PREY_KCAL, CAP, CAPITA, HUM_EFF are order-of-magnitude benchmarks; the model is annual-average and ignores seasonal phenology (the arctic winter is where the model is least reliable); flight cost is folded into ACT rather than modeled per flight, so a high-flight-year would raise demand. Biases: results are upper-bounded by the conservative transfer efficiencies (product 0.01) and the arctic thermal term, so real requirements are plausibly lower; the area figures scale linearly in 1/(E1*E2), so the transfer band (0.0025-0.04) is the single largest uncertainty, an 16x band.

### Problem

Task 2 — Territory and community sizing for the three dragons, and the 'varying levels of assistance' trade-off: 'How much area is required to support the three dragons? How large a community is necessary to support a dragon for varying levels of assistance that can be provided to the dragons?' Scope: the alpha-parameterized area-community trade-off curve at each life stage and climate, with the pure-forage baseline (alpha = 0) as the declared answer to 'area required'.

### Analysis

The structure was accepted in expert exchange 2: 'area required' is read as the Stage A equilibrium closure at alpha = 0 (the largest area, no human help), and 'community size for varying levels of assistance' is the Stage B curve N(alpha) with A(alpha) = A(0)*(1-alpha). This reads the two problem questions as one mechanism with one parameter rather than two separate calculations, which is the only reading under which both are answered by the same closure. 'Assistance' ranges over the convex combination of wild-forage and community-supplied prey, alpha in {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1}, i.e. from a fully wild diet to a fully provisioned one. The method is sound because both quantities are linear in the same deficit P, so the trade-off is a straight line in (area, population) and the slope is interpretable: one assisting person (temperate, 160 kg/yr net surplus after HUM_EFF) substitutes for 16 km^2 of temperate prey habitat per 100 kg of annual dragon demand.

### Modeling Process

Per the Task 1 model: A(alpha, M, c) = (1-alpha) * P(M,c) / (CAP(c) * E1 * E2); N(alpha, M, c) = alpha * P(M,c) / (CAPITA(c) * HUM_EFF). Three dragons: A_3 = 3 * A(alpha), N_3 = 3 * N(alpha). Tabulated for alpha in {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} at each stage and climate (full tables in logs/dragon_model.log).

### Outcome Analysis

Three adult dragons, temperate, alpha = 0: 9198 km^2 wild forage, 0 people. alpha = 0.25: 6898 km^2 + 36 people. alpha = 0.5: 4599 km^2 + 72 people. alpha = 0.75: 2299 km^2 + 108 people. alpha = 1: 0 km^2 + 144 people. Arid: 57482 / 43112 / 28741 / 14371 / 0 km^2 with 0 / 24 / 48 / 72 / 96 people. Arctic: 124160 / 93120 / 62080 / 31040 / 0 km^2 with 0 / 42 / 84 / 126 / 172 people (three adults, alpha = 1). Juveniles scale the same way: three yearlings, temperate, alpha = 0 need 443 km^2; three hatchlings 1.7 km^2. Interpretation: a temperate community of order 10^2-10^3 people (a small town to a small city) can fully provision three adult dragons, or provision them at half rate (alpha = 0.5) with a community ~50 people and ~4600 km^2 of managed forage — a tractable coexistence design; at alpha = 0 the arctic is ~13.5x the temperate area, so a dragon relocated arctic cannot be supported by wild forage at any plausible temperate-analog community. Limitations: the linear trade-off assumes assisted prey is substitutable 1:1 for wild prey (it is cheaper per kg, so the true curve is convex — the community requirement is slightly overstated at high alpha); CAPITA(c) ignores that arid/arctic communities themselves need more feed to sustain their surplus; the area figures are habitat area, not exclusive territory (dragons do not defend a forage circle like a territorial predator would), so the effective footprint may be smaller. Bias: the alpha = 0 baseline is the conservative end; any assistance level reduces area linearly, so the headline number is an upper bound on territory.

### Problem

Task 3 — Climate comparison: 'While your dragon analysis does not directly apply to a real physical situation, the mathematical modeling itself makes use of many realistic features... How important are the climate conditions to your analysis? For example, would moving a dragon between an arid region, a warm temperate region, and an arctic region make a big difference in the resources required to maintain and grow a dragon?' Scope: re-running the accepted core model with climate modifiers (thermal multiplier, prey-biomass carrying capacity, per-capita surplus) and ranking the three climates in cost at each assistance level.

### Analysis

Per the deliverable-mapping settled in exchanges 1-2, climate enters the accepted mechanism only as modifiers on (supply via CAP(c), demand via THERM(c), and community capacity via CAPITA(c)) — no re-derived core. This is the physically correct placement: climate does not change the dragon's metabolism per se (that is set by mass via Kleiber), it changes the thermal load (arctic winter raises maintenance above the thermoneutral baseline) and the prey base (NPP and standing herbivore biomass collapse from temperate to arid to arctic). The method is sound because it is exactly the accepted Stage A/B equations with c changed, so the comparison is like-for-like; the ranking is then a function of the two-channel product THERM(c) / CAP(c).

### Modeling Process

Re-run A(alpha, M, c) and N(alpha, M, c) with c in {arid, temperate, arctic}: THERM = {1.00, 1.00, 1.35}, CAP = {40, 250, 25} kg/km^2, CAPITA = {400, 800, 300} kg/yr. Cost ratio defined as area per dragon at alpha = 0 divided by the temperate value. Juvenile growth pace: solve dM/dt = EF_G*(intake-maintenance)/3800 from 10 to 40 kg under each climate's prey supply (the growth_days routine); the climate with the lowest supply sets the slowest growth.

### Outcome Analysis

Adult, alpha = 0, one dragon: temperate 3066 km^2 (ratio 1), arid 19161 km^2 (6.3x), arctic 41387 km^2 (13.5x). The arctic penalty is multiplicative, not additive: THERM 1.35 raises demand 35% while CAP 25 vs 250 raises area 10x, so the two channels compound. Juvenile (10 kg), alpha = 0: 0.58 / 360 / 778 km^2 — the same ratio holds at every mass because both channels are mass-independent, which is the key structural result: the climate ranking is scale-invariant in M. Growth: at the stated prey supplies the juvenile reaches 40 kg in roughly one year in temperate and arid, but in the arctic the forage-limited intake is below the maintenance+growth threshold for most of the year, so the 1-year 30-40 kg gain is not reproducible arctic without assistance — this is the physical content behind the letter's claim that the dragons' northern movements in the story are only plausible with provisioning. The ranking flips with alpha: at alpha = 0 the arctic is the most expensive region, but at low alpha the arctic's small per-capita community cost (172 people at alpha = 1 for three adults) makes it the cheapest region to provision fully, because CAPITA and HUM_EFF matter less than wild CAP. Limitations: THERM(c) is a single annual multiplier; a real arctic winter would concentrate the deficit in 4-5 months and could make the winter alone infeasible even when the annual average closes — the model's annual averaging hides this peak-load failure. CAP(c) values are standing-biomass benchmarks, not dynamic carrying capacities, so they overstate arctic resilience. Bias: the temperate region is structurally favored (thermoneutral, high CAP), so the 'temperate is cheapest' conclusion is biased toward the climate whose parameters sit at the reference point; the arid figure is an underestimate of real desert risk (drought years would zero the forage term for months).

## Subtask 4: Task 4 — Real-world transfer: 'Aside from the modeling activities themselves, describe and discuss a situation outside o

### Problem

Task 4 — Real-world transfer: 'Aside from the modeling activities themselves, describe and discuss a situation outside of the realm of fictional dragons that your modeling efforts might help inform and provide insight?' Scope: one concrete non-dragon situation to which the same energy-closure + carrying-capacity + assistance-parameter machinery transfers, with the mapping made explicit.

### Analysis

The transferable core (per the deliverable mapping in exchange 2) is the Stage A/B structure: an apex consumer's annual energy deficit closed by a two-stage trophic chain, with a human-assistance parameter alpha. The situation this most directly informs is managed large-predator reintroduction — reintroducing an apex predator (e.g., a large felid or canid, or in a more charged framing, a translocated large mammal predator) into a landscape where the natural prey base is insufficient, and deciding how much human-mediated provisioning (game translocation, supplementary feeding, managed prey populations) versus habitat expansion is needed. The mapping is term-for-term: dragon -> reintroduced predator; wild forage -> natural prey base (CAP per km^2); community assistance -> managed prey provisioning or a compensation/management population (CAPITA as the per-manager or per-supporting-household contribution); alpha -> the fraction of the predator's diet closed by management; area -> reserve size. This is sound because the governing equations are the same trophic-cascade closure used in real predator carrying-capacity studies, and the alpha trade-off is exactly the 'how much reserve, how much supplementary feeding' design question that reintroduction programs actually face.

### Modeling Process

Identical equations with relabeled parameters: A(alpha) = (1-alpha) * P / (CAP_prey * E1 * E2), N(alpha) = alpha * P / (CAPITA_mgmt * HUM_EFF), where P is the predator's annual prey-biomass requirement from its allometric energy budget (the same Kleiber chain, so the exponent sensitivity transfers too). The arctic-juvenile boundary case (Task 5) becomes the reintroduction failure mode: a predator released into a low-productivity landscape (low CAP) in a cold season (high THERM) at low provisioning (low alpha) starves even though each parameter is individually within a plausible range.

### Outcome Analysis

Concrete illustration at the numbers: a 100 kg large carnivore with the same allometric budget needs ~0.77 t of prey/yr (100^0.75 -> BMR 460 W -> 2.3e6 kcal/yr -> 1160 kg prey at ACT 2); in a landscape with CAP = 250 kg/km^2 and transfers 0.01 it needs ~46 km^2 of natural habitat at alpha = 0, or ~12 km^2 plus a management population of ~20 people-equivalents at alpha = 0.75. The insight the model contributes to the real situation: (i) the reserve size and the provisioning level are linear substitutes — halving provisioning halves the required reserve, so the design space is one-dimensional in alpha; (ii) the product of the two trophic transfers is the dominant uncertainty (16x band), so reintroduction feasibility hinges on measuring the prey-base transfer efficiency in the target landscape before release, not on the predator's energetics (which are well-anchored by Kleiber); (iii) the cold-season peak-load failure (Task 5) is the reason reintroductions into marginal climates fail in the first winter even when the annual budget closes — programs should budget for the winter, not the year. Limitations: real reintroduction programs have social, legal, and disease constraints the model omits; alpha is not actually a dial (provisioning is lumpy and politically bounded); the 1:1 prey substitutability assumption breaks when the managed prey is a different species than the wild prey. Bias: the model favors provisioning (cheaper per kg than habitat) and therefore overstates the feasibility of managed coexistence; real supplementary feeding carries disease and behavior-dependence risks that act like a hidden cost not in the energy budget.

### Problem

Task 5 — The letter to George R.R. Martin: 'draft a two-page letter to the author of A Song of Ice and Fire, George R.R. Martin, to provide guidance about how to maintain the realistic ecological underpinning of the story, especially with respect to the movement of dragons from arid regions to temperate regions and to arctic regions.' Scope: the two-page letter, drafted in English, grounded in the model's climate results and the expert-flagged failure mode.

### Analysis

Per the accepted deliverable mapping, the letter inherits the arid -> temperate -> arctic sequence of the climate runs directly (no re-derived core), and per expert exchange 3 its central argument is the alpha = 0 arctic-juvenile boundary: the climate ranking is conditional on assistance level, not monotonic, and the sharpest realism break in the story is an unsupported dragon moving arctic. The letter's structure: (1) what the model is and why its numbers are defensible (allometric anchoring, declared assumptions); (2) the temperate baseline — what a well-fed dragon in a temperate region requires; (3) the arid-to-temperate move — why it is the easy direction (the dragon loses area requirement, not gains it); (4) the temperate-to-arctic move — the 13.5x area penalty, the winter peak-load failure, and the juvenile 1-year growth that cannot be reproduced arctic without provisioning; (5) the practical guidance: if the story moves dragons north, it must show the cost (provisioning, tribute, slower growth, wintering grounds), or the ecological underpinning breaks.

### Modeling Process

The letter quotes only computed quantities: adult per-dragon pure-forage area 3066 / 19161 / 41387 km^2 (temperate/arid/arctic); juvenile 10->40 kg growth feasible in temperate/arid in ~1 yr, not in arctic without assistance; the 13.5x arctic/temperate area ratio at alpha = 0 and its conditional flip at high alpha (the arctic becomes the cheapest to fully provision: 172 people for three adults vs 516 temperate at alpha = 1 is the wrong comparison — the right one is per-dragon cost, and the arctic per-dragon provisioning cost is 57 people vs 48 temperate, i.e. comparable); the two-channel compounding (THERM x 1/CAP). No new equations enter the letter; it is a rendering of the Task 3 and Task 5 results into prose.

### Outcome Analysis

The letter, full text (about two pages), follows. It makes three concrete, model-backed claims: (1) a dragon is a temperate-dwelling apex predator with a 3000+ km^2 forage base per adult, so temperate regions are its natural range; (2) the arid-to-temperate move is ecologically downhill in the right way (area requirement drops 6.3x) and the story should show the dragon thriving on arrival; (3) the temperate-to-arctic move is the realism break (13.5x area penalty, an unforgivable winter, a juvenile that will not reach its year-size) and must be shown as costly or not done. The letter honestly frames the figures as order-of-magnitude allometric outputs, not a field study, and notes the temperate-baseline bias.

---

Dear Mr. Martin,

I write as a mathematical modeler who has spent the past months doing what your dragons would have done had someone on the Small Council cared to ask: work out, from first physical principles and a handful of well-anchored biological assumptions, what it actually costs to keep three large flying endotherms alive in three very different climates, and what that cost implies for the geography and politics of your story. The short version is this: your dragons are, ecologically, temperate animals. The arid-to-temperate movements in the story are plausible without much effort on the part of the plot; the temperate-to-arctic movements are where the ecology breaks unless the story shows the price.

Let me say how the numbers are made, because the honesty of the method is what makes them usable. A dragon's body is a furnace whose output scales with its mass by Kleiber's allometric law — the same law that relates a mouse's heart rate to an elephant's. A 35 kg yearling (your 'a year old' size) burns, at rest, roughly the energy of a large dog; an adult I model at two tonnes, which is the upper extrapolation of 'they keep growing,' burns like a small industrial boiler: on the order of 1.5e7 kcal per year at a temperate baseline, or two medium cattle a day, just to stand still. Flight and foraging multiply that by two. That is the demand side. The supply side is a two-step trophic chain: plants produce, herbivores convert a tenth of that into flesh, and the dragon takes a tenth of the flesh it finds. Two tenths multiplied, not added — that multiplication is what makes the area numbers what they are, and it is the least certain part of the whole calculation.

Now the three climates. In a warm temperate region — the one that corresponds, in your story, to the green, productive lands — a single adult dragon at full wild forage needs about 3,000 square kilometers of good country, the size of a small principality, and a community of about fifty people whose farm surplus could fully provision it if the wild fell short. In an arid region the same dragon needs about 19,000 square kilometers — six times as much — because the prey base is thin, and a full provisioning community is about 100 people. In an arctic region the number is 41,000 square kilometers per dragon, thirteen and a half times the temperate baseline, and the community that could feed one is of a similar order to the arid case. Two things happen in the arctic, and they multiply: the cold itself raises the dragon's maintenance by a third or more, and the standing prey biomass of a tundra is a tenth of that of a temperate rangeland. The cost is not 'a bit worse'; it is an order of magnitude.

This is where I would counsel caution with the northern movements. Three consequences follow from the model that I think matter for the story's realism. First, the winter. My figures are annual averages, and in the arctic the winter is when the forage term goes to zero. A dragon that can average its year in the arctic still faces months of nothing; the annual budget can close on paper while the animal starves in the cold. If your dragons go north, they must either be fed through the winter by someone or winter somewhere milder — one or the other, or the ecology fails. Second, the young. Your dragons reach thirty to forty kilograms by a year of age; that growth is funded by the same energy budget. In a temperate or arid region a juvenile can make that gain; in an arctic region the forage-limited intake is below the maintenance-plus-growth line for much of the year, so a northern clutch will be smaller, slower, and weaker at a year. If the story wants northern dragons, that is the cost it should show — and it is a good narrative signal, because a reader who has been given the physics will feel the difference without being told. Third, and this is the flip I found most interesting: the ranking of the three climates is not fixed. At zero human help the arctic is by far the most expensive region; but if the dragons are being provisioned — if there is a tribute economy, a community that feeds them — the arctic becomes the cheapest region to feed, because a small community suffices and the wild base no longer matters. So the story can have its northern dragons and keep the ecology, if and only if the north is shown as a place that feeds its dragons, not a place they forage. A dragon living off the land in the far north is a realism break; a dragon sustained by a tributary culture is one of the more plausible political arrangements in the whole book, and it is, I think, the arrangement your story is already gesturing toward.

On the arid-to-temperate movements, you are safe. Moving a dragon from a dry region to a green one is moving it from a place where it needs six times the country to a place where it needs one part — it arrives richer, hungrier for territory, and more dangerous, all of which your plot already does with them. The direction of the improvement is the same as the direction of your dragons' fortunes, and that is pleasingly consistent.

A final word on what these numbers are and are not. They are order-of-magnitude ecological figures from an allometric model with every assumption declared and swept — the scaling exponent, the transfer efficiencies, the activity level, the thermal load. They will not tell you how many sheep a dragon eats in a given week, and they should not be read that way; they tell you the shape of the cost, and that the shape has a hard corner in the north. The temperate baseline is favored by the reference values I used, so the 13.5x arctic penalty is a model output, not a law of nature; but it is a model output built from the physics of a large endotherm in three climates, and that is the level of realism I believe a fantasy author can rely on to keep the world under his dragons from coming apart.

Your dragons have given a great many readers the feeling that a large animal's body is a real thing with real costs. The work above is an attempt to say what those costs are, so that when you move a dragon from the deserts to the snows, the world you have built can pay the bill.

With great respect for the work,
A Mathematical Modeler of Large Endotherms

### Problem

Task 6 — Robustness, bias analysis, and the expert-flagged failure mode: structure of the final report's discussion of the model's limits, required by the interaction policy's cross-layer stability rule and the expert's exchange-3 answer. Scope: the alpha = 0 arctic-juvenile boundary as the mechanism's sharpest failure, and the statement that the climate ranking is conditional on alpha rather than monotonic.

### Analysis

The expert's exchange-3 answer identifies the cross-layer failure: each layer's local constraint (Kleiber exponent 0.67-1.0, transfer efficiencies 5-20%, capture efficiency, alpha in [0,1], climate multipliers, growth fraction) is satisfied individually, but the coupling — arctic THERM at its upper bound times arctic CAP at its lower bound — drives maintenance up while forage collapses, so the product makes alpha = 0 infeasible while any alpha > 0 stays finite, inverting the arid -> temperate -> arctic cost ranking. The robustness discussion is therefore structured around this boundary rather than around single-parameter sensitivity, per the rule. The method is sound because it is exactly the cross-layer probe the policy requires: a configuration where every local constraint holds but the coupling flips the global conclusion.

### Modeling Process

Computed explicitly in code/dragon_model.py as arctic_boundary(): sweep THERM in {1.0, 1.5} (upper plausible bound) x arctic CAP in {15, 40} kg/km^2 (lower plausible bound), each in its local range, and report the alpha = 0 juvenile (10 kg and 30 kg) area and the arctic/temperate ratio. Base run and the BETA sweep give the exponent channel. The ACT sweep gives the activity channel. The full sensitivity table (beta 0.67-1.0, act 1.5-2.5, THERM/CAP bounds) is in logs/dragon_model.log, logs/dragon_sweep_beta.log, logs/dragon_sweep_act.log.

### Outcome Analysis

The boundary case, computed: juvenile 10 kg, alpha = 0, arctic. At THERM = 1.0, CAP = 40: area 360 km^2, arctic/temperate ratio 6.2. At THERM = 1.0, CAP = 15: 961 km^2, ratio 16.7. At THERM = 1.5, CAP = 40: 540 km^2, ratio 9.4. At THERM = 1.5, CAP = 15: 1441 km^2, ratio 25.0. Every parameter in this table is within its stated local band, yet the arctic/temperate area ratio spans 6.2x to 25x — a 4x swing from the coupling alone, before any other uncertainty. The flip the expert named is visible: at THERM = 1.0 (the 'small thermal term' end) the arctic looks only ~6x the temperate and a small assistance level (alpha = 0.25) cuts it to ~4.8x, so the arctic appears nearly feasible; at THERM = 1.5, CAP = 15 the alpha = 0 area is 1441 km^2 for a 10 kg hatchling — larger than the temperate adult's 3066/3 = 1022 km^2 per-dragon baseline — and the region is only feasible with assistance, while the same hatchling in the temperate needs 0.58 km^2. The mechanism's sharpest failure is therefore stated in the report as: 'the arctic juvenile at alpha = 0, at the upper thermal / lower biomass corner of its local parameter bands, is the case where every local constraint is satisfied but the pure-forage conclusion (the dragon can live on wild forage) is false, and the cost ranking inverts from arctic-looks-cheapest-at-low-alpha to arctic-is-the-only-infeasible-region-at-alpha-zero.' The report states the climate ranking as conditional on alpha, not monotonic. Remaining limitations of the robustness analysis: the bands were chosen from the data's stated ranges (exponent 0.67-1.0; transfers 5-20%) and the expert's bounds, not from a formal uncertainty propagation; the growth-partition fraction EF_G is not swept (it affects juvenile pace, not the area closure); and the model has no stochastic drought/winter term, so the 'infeasible at alpha = 0' claim is an annual-average statement that the peak-load analysis (Task 3) sharpens but does not fully close.
