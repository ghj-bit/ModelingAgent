## Subtask 1: Subtask 1 — Energy expenditures and caloric intake requirements of the dragons. Goal: compute, for a dragon of mass M, t

### Problem

Subtask 1 — Energy expenditures and caloric intake requirements of the dragons. Goal: compute, for a dragon of mass M, the daily metabolizable-energy demand and the corresponding gross caloric (prey-mass) intake, across the adult mass range and across the Kleiber-exponent, activity, and assimilation-uncertainty ranges. Scope: deliverable (2) of the problem; the primary output is the GROSS diet basis (per expert Exchange 1, Q1b), with metabolizable energy derived.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (i) Dragons are endothermic flying predators, so basal metabolic rate follows Kleiber's law BMR = 3.4·M^α W with M in kg (central α = 0.75, swept over {0.67, 0.75, 1.0} because the exponent choice shifts predicted metabolic rate by up to ~26% for a 100 kg animal — the supplied data flag α as a sensitivity parameter, not a fixed truth). (ii) A flying predator's field metabolic rate is a multiple of BMR; we use an activity surcharge k_activity = 2.5 (swept 2–3), an assumption tied to the flight capability the problem names. (iii) Growth carries an enthalpy cost dH_form = 20,000 kJ per kg of new tissue (an assumption, order of the energy in protein/fat/bone synthesis), applied to an annual mass gain dM = 20 kg/yr. (iv) Diet is large-vertebrate prey; assimilation efficiency η_assim = 0.20 (swept 0.15–0.30) is the fraction of consumed prey mass retained as metabolizable energy — the supplied cattle data give the metabolizable-energy density of feed, and a carnivore's assimilation of that must be an assumed, sensitivity-tested efficiency. Method and why it is sound: Kleiber's law is the standard allometric bridge from mass to metabolic rate and is the only defensible way to extrapolate from the observed 10 kg hatchling / 30–40 kg juvenile sizes to the adult range the text says is unbounded ('grow throughout their life'). The gross-diet basis is primary because, per the expert, area is set by biomass removed from the ecosystem, which is gross intake; metabolizable energy is the intermediate that the assimilation efficiency converts to prey mass. All uncertain constants are swept rather than fixed, so the result is a defensible range, not a false-precision point.

### Modeling Process

Let M be dragon mass (kg), α the Kleiber exponent, k_activity the activity surcharge, k_region the climate maintenance multiplier, dH_form the enthalpy of growth (kJ/kg), dM the annual mass gain (kg/yr), η_assim the assimilation efficiency. (1) BMR(M) = 3.4·M^α [W]. (2) Maintenance metabolizable demand: E_maint = BMR·86.4·k_activity·k_region [kJ/day] (86.4 = kJ/day per W). (3) Growth demand: E_growth = dM·dH_form/365 [kJ/day]. (4) Total metabolizable demand: E_met = E_maint + E_growth [kJ/day]. (5) Gross metabolizable-equivalent intake: E_gross_met = E_met/η_assim [kJ/day]. (6) Gross prey live-mass intake: F_gross = E_gross_met / e_prey [kg/day], where e_prey = 13 kJ/kg is the metabolizable energy per kg of live prey mass (from a 250 kg prey carrying 3250 kJ metabolizable, per the supplied cattle energy density). (7) Gross caloric intake in food units: F_gross_kcal = E_gross_met/4.184 [kcal/day]. (8) Prey count: N_prey = F_gross / 250 [prey/day]. Solution procedure: evaluate (1)–(8) for M ∈ {100, 500, 1000, 5000, 10000} kg, α ∈ {0.67, 0.75, 1.0}, k_activity ∈ {2, 2.5, 3}, η_assim ∈ {0.15, 0.20, 0.30}, k_region per region. Code: code/dragon_model.py (--mode base / mass_sweep / alpha_sweep / eta_sweep).

### Outcome Analysis

Results (temperate, central parameters, three dragons not yet aggregated): at M = 100 kg, E_met ≈ 5,813 kcal/day and F_gross ≈ 9,354 kg/day (~37 prey/day); at M = 1,000 kg, E_met ≈ 31,475 kcal/day and F_gross ≈ 50,651 kg/day (~203 prey/day); at M = 10,000 kg, E_met ≈ 175,788 kcal/day and F_gross ≈ 282,883 kg/day (~1,132 prey/day). Sensitivity: the Kleiber exponent is the dominant single-parameter uncertainty — at M = 1,000 kg, α = 0.67 gives 18,223 kcal/day vs α = 1.0 giving 175,788 kcal/day (a ~9.6× spread), so the caloric requirement is set by the exponent choice to within an order of magnitude; η_assim at M = 1,000 kg moves F_gross from 67,535 kg/day (η=0.15) to 33,767 kg/day (η=0.30), a factor of 2. The model's limitations: (i) Kleiber's law is fitted to mammals and birds; a dragon's true exponent is unknown, which is why it is swept, not fixed. (ii) The activity surcharge lumps flight, foraging, and fire-breathing into one multiplier; fire-breathing in particular is an unmodeled energetic cost that would raise E_met. (iii) The enthalpy of growth is a single constant; real growth partitions energy into maintenance of new tissue and structural (bone/scale) deposition that is less efficient. Bias: the gross-diet basis systematically over-states the *energy* the dragon needs (it includes the 1/η_assim loss) but correctly states the *biomass* it must remove, which is what the downstream area model requires.

## Subtask 2: Subtask 2 — Ecological impact and requirements of the dragons. Goal: characterize what the dragons require of their envi

### Problem

Subtask 2 — Ecological impact and requirements of the dragons. Goal: characterize what the dragons require of their environment (prey biomass, habitat) and what impact they have (predation pressure on the local prey community). Scope: deliverable (1); reported as the same core chain as Subtask 1 plus the implied predation pressure, not a separate model (per expert Exchange 1, Q1c).

### Analysis

Assumptions. The dragons are apex predators on large-vertebrate prey; their ecological requirement is a sustained supply of gross prey biomass at the rate F_gross (Subtask 1), and their impact is the removal of that biomass from the prey population. We treat the prey community as a standing crop replaced by habitat net primary production at a yield B_car (tonnes of prey biomass per km² per year), so the requirement is a minimum habitat area and the impact is a predation rate on that standing crop. The three dragons are assumed to forage over the same region (no territorial separation), so their draws add. Method and why it is sound: expressing impact as a fraction of the habitat's annual prey-biomass turnover is the standard way to state predation pressure on a carrying-capacity-limited community; it makes the impact a *rate relative to renewal*, which is the quantity that determines whether the prey population is depleted. The supplied cattle carrying-capacity data inform the order of B_car (a temperate grassland sustains on the order of 1 t of herbivore biomass per km² per year), which we sweep rather than fix.

### Modeling Process

Let B_car be the habitat prey-biomass yield (t/km²/yr), f_forage the climate foraging multiplier, and B_car_scale the regional NPP fraction (temperate 1.0, arctic 0.4, arid 0.3). Effective yield: B_eff = B_car·1000·B_car_scale/f_forage [kg/km²/yr]. Annual gross prey draw of three dragons: D_annual = F_gross·3·365 [kg/yr]. Predation pressure (fraction of annual prey-biomass turnover removed): p = D_annual / (B_eff·A), where A is the foraged area. Minimum foraged area to sustain the draw: A_min = D_annual / B_eff [km²]. At A = A_min, p = 1 (the dragons remove the habitat's entire annual prey turnover); at A > A_min, p < 1. Code: code/dragon_model.py (area_km2, run_case).

### Outcome Analysis

Results (central B_car = 1 t/km²/yr, M = 1,000 kg, temperate): the three dragons remove D_annual ≈ 55.5 million kg/yr of prey biomass and require A_min ≈ 55,463 km² of temperate habitat — at that area they consume 100% of the annual prey turnover, i.e. they are at the carrying-capacity limit of the region's prey community. At M = 10,000 kg the requirement is A_min ≈ 309,757 km² (temperate) — an area larger than many European countries — and the predation pressure at A_min is still 100% of turnover. Impact: at or near A_min the dragons are a *regulating* predator on the prey community (they set the prey population to its carrying capacity); any reduction in habitat below A_min drives p > 1 and local prey depletion. Limitations: (i) B_car is the weakest-anchored constant in the model — real prey-biomass yield varies by an order of magnitude across biomes, so A_min inherits that full uncertainty. (ii) The model assumes the prey population is at carrying capacity and renewed at B_car; if the prey are themselves K-selected with slow turnover, the effective renewal is lower and A_min larger. (iii) No prey-side dynamics (refuge, migration, density dependence) are modeled; the impact is a steady-state turnover fraction, not a trajectory. Bias: stating impact as a turnover fraction at A_min flatters the dragons (it is the best case); realistic foraging is less efficient than 100% of turnover, so the true impact at a given area is a lower fraction but the true required area is larger.

### Problem

Subtask 3 — Area required to support the three dragons. Goal: compute the minimum habitat area that sustains the three dragons' gross prey-biomass demand, across the adult mass range and the three climate regions. Scope: deliverable (3); the area model consumes gross prey mass (per expert Exchange 1, Q1b), not metabolizable energy.

### Analysis

Assumptions. The habitat is a single biome of uniform prey-biomass yield B_car, with climate entering through the foraging multiplier f_forage (arid 1.5 — foraging is less efficient in sparse, dispersed prey) and the regional NPP fraction B_car_scale (arctic 0.4, temperate 1.0, arid 0.3). The dragons forage over the minimum area that sustains their draw; we do not model seasonal movement or multiple habitats. Method and why it is sound: area = annual demand / annual yield is the carrying-capacity relation; it is the direct inverse of the predation-pressure relation in Subtask 2 and is the standard way to convert a biomass requirement into a spatial requirement. Climate enters as two *distinct* multipliers (energy via k_region, area via f_forage and B_car_scale) per the expert's Exchange 1 ruling that climate is not a single all-purpose factor.

### Modeling Process

A_min(M, region) = [F_gross(M)·3·365] / [B_car·1000·B_car_scale(region)/f_forage(region)] [km²], with F_gross from Subtask 1. Evaluated for M ∈ {100, 500, 1000, 5000, 10000} kg and region ∈ {arctic, temperate, arid}, B_car swept over {0.3, 1.0, 3.0} t/km²/yr. Code: code/dragon_model.py (--mode mass_sweep / region_sweep).

### Outcome Analysis

Results (central B_car = 1 t/km²/yr): M = 1,000 kg requires 10,242 km² (M=100), 33,166 km² (M=500), 55,463 km² (M=1,000), 184,370 km² (M=5,000), 309,757 km² (M=10,000) in temperate climate. Region effect at M = 1,000 kg: arctic 179,908 km², temperate 55,463 km², arid 236,064 km². The area requirement scales super-linearly with mass (M^0.75 through metabolism times the activity and assimilation terms) and is 4.3× larger in the arid region than temperate at equal mass, driven by the 0.3 NPP fraction and the 1.5 foraging multiplier. Limitations: (i) A_min is a *minimum*; realistic foraging efficiency below 100% of turnover multiplies it. (ii) The uniform-biome assumption ignores that a real range spans gradients; the true required area is at least as large as the minimum over the best sub-region. (iii) The arctic penalty is dominated by the NPP fraction (0.4), which is the least certain of the climate parameters. Bias: the temperate reference is the most data-anchored (cattle-grassland yield); the arid and arctic values are extrapolations by NPP fraction and foraging multiplier, so the cross-region *ranking* is more robust than the cross-region *ratios*.

## Subtask 4: Subtask 4 — Community size necessary to support a dragon for varying levels of assistance. Goal: determine, for each ass

### Problem

Subtask 4 — Community size necessary to support a dragon for varying levels of assistance. Goal: determine, for each assistance level a (the human-provided fraction of the dragons' gross caloric demand), the minimum human community size whose food surplus can supply a·F_gross, and the joint feasibility of the community and dragons sharing one habitat. Scope: deliverable (4); a separate assistance-parameterized sub-model (per expert Exchange 1, Q1c), not a trivial slice of the area model.

### Analysis

Assumptions. A human can divert a per-capita food surplus s = 200 kg/person/yr to the dragons (an assumption of order a pre-industrial surplus after the community's own subsistence). The community farms the *same* habitat the dragons forage, so the community's farmed draw and the dragons' wild draw are additive on the shared carrying capacity. The assistance level a ∈ [0,1] is the fraction of the dragons' gross demand supplied by the community; (1−a) is drawn wild. Method and why it is sound: community size = (farmed draw)/(per-capita surplus) is a direct division; the joint constraint is the F×C shared-resource coupling the expert identified in Exchange 3 — the total draw F_gross (wild + farmed) must fit the habitat capacity, and because the total is independent of a, the joint-feasibility condition is a *step* (all a feasible iff capacity ≥ total demand, else none), not a slope in a. This is the correction to the naive two-layer certification that would sum a wild area and a community area independently and thereby over-count the habitat by up to 2× at intermediate a.

### Modeling Process

Total annual gross demand of three dragons: D = F_gross·3·365 [kg/yr]. Wild draw: D_wild = D·(1−a). Farmed draw: D_farmed = D·a. Community size: Pop(a) = ceil(D_farmed / s) = ceil(D·a/s). Habitat capacity over area A: C = B_eff·A. Joint feasibility: D ≤ C (total draw, independent of a). At the minimum area A_min (Subtask 3), C = D by construction, so joint feasibility holds for all a and a_max_joint = 1; if the available habitat A < A_min, then C < D and a_max_joint = 0 (no positive assistance is jointly feasible). The 'kink' in community size is at a = 0 (Pop jumps from 0 to D/s as a becomes positive). Code: code/dragon_model.py (community_and_joint, --mode a_sweep).

### Outcome Analysis

Results (M = 1,000 kg, temperate, at A_min): D ≈ 55.46 million kg/yr. Community size Pop(a): a=0 → 0; a=0.25 → 69,329; a=0.5 → 138,658; a=0.75 → 207,986; a=1.0 → 277,315. At A_min the joint constraint is active (C = D) and feasible for all a; at 3×A_min the joint constraint is slack (C = 3D) and all a remain feasible with large spare capacity. The F×C correction matters precisely here: a naive model that certified the wild draw (area A_min) and the community draw (area A_min·a) *separately* and required their sum would declare the joint state infeasible for a > 0 at A_min, which is wrong — the correct constraint is the *total* draw D against the *single* capacity C, and D ≤ C holds. Limitations: (i) s = 200 kg/person/yr is the least-anchored constant in the sub-model; a community with a smaller surplus (subsistence-level) would need a population an order of magnitude larger. (ii) The community's own subsistence is not modeled — we assume it is met outside the dragon habitat; if it is met inside, the capacity C is reduced by the community's own draw and a_max_joint drops below 1. (iii) The step-like a_max_joint means the sub-model has no gradual 'partial assistance' regime at a fixed area — assistance is all-or-nothing at the capacity limit, which is a structural artifact of the shared-resource coupling, not a biological claim. Bias: reporting community size at A_min is the most favorable (smallest-population) case; any habitat loss below A_min makes the joint state infeasible for all a > 0, a discontinuity the linear Pop(a) curve does not show.

## Subtask 5: Subtask 5 — Climate sensitivity: how much moving a dragon between arid, warm-temperate, and arctic regions changes the r

### Problem

Subtask 5 — Climate sensitivity: how much moving a dragon between arid, warm-temperate, and arctic regions changes the resources required to maintain and grow it. Goal: quantify the resource (energy and area) change across the three regions and state whether the difference is large. Scope: deliverable (5); climate enters through two distinct multipliers (k_region on maintenance energy, f_forage and B_car_scale on area) per expert Exchange 1.

### Analysis

Assumptions. The three regions differ in (i) thermoregulatory cost: an arctic dragon must burn more to stay warm (k_region = 1.3), a warm-temperate dragon is the reference (1.0), an arid dragon has a lower metabolic thermoregulatory cost (0.85) but a foraging penalty; (ii) prey-biomass yield: arctic 0.4×, temperate 1.0×, arid 0.3× of the temperate reference (NPP fractions); (iii) foraging efficiency: arid 1.5× (prey are sparse and dispersed), others 1.0. The dragon's mass and diet are held fixed across the move; only the regional multipliers change. Method and why it is sound: separating the energy-side and area-side climate effects lets us see that climate can *flip* the resource ranking — a region that is cheap in energy can be expensive in area — which a single 'climate factor' would hide. The conclusion-flip between the energy ranking and the area ranking is demonstrated in logs/conclusion_flip.log and is the key robustness finding for the letter.

### Modeling Process

For each region r ∈ {arctic, temperate, arid}: E_met(r) = [3.4·M^α·86.4·k_activity·k_region(r) + dM·dH_form/365]; A_min(r) = [F_gross(M)·3·365]/[B_car·1000·B_car_scale(r)/f_forage(r)], with F_gross(M) = E_met(r)/η_assim/13. The resource change of moving from region r1 to r2 is the ratio E_met(r2)/E_met(r1) for energy and A_min(r2)/A_min(r1) for area. Code: code/dragon_model.py (--mode region_sweep, --sweep M_kg=...).

### Outcome Analysis

Results (M = 1,000 kg, central parameters): Energy (metabolizable kcal/day): arctic 40,839, temperate 31,475, arid 26,793 — moving temperate→arctic raises energy demand by 30%, temperate→arid lowers it by 15%. Area (km²): arctic 179,908, temperate 55,463, arid 236,064 — moving temperate→arctic raises area by 3.2×, temperate→arid raises it by 4.3×. The answer to the problem's question is YES, climate makes a big difference, but *differently in the two resources*: in energy the arctic is the most demanding (thermoregulatory surcharge) and the arid the least; in area the arid is the most demanding (low NPP + foraging penalty) and the temperate the least. The two rankings DISAGREE (energy: arctic > temperate > arid; area: arid > arctic > temperate), so there is no single 'most demanding region' — the conclusion depends on which resource you weight, and at M = 10,000 kg the arid area requirement (1,316,813 km²) exceeds the arctic (1,006,364 km²) by a wide margin while the arctic still demands 53% more energy than the arid. This flip is the cross-layer (energy × foraging) failure mode the expert's counterexample pointed toward: each layer is stable in its own range, but the coupling produces a ranking reversal that a single-region or single-metric analysis cannot see. Limitations: the k_region and B_car_scale values are assumptions of order, not measurements; the flip is robust to moderate perturbations (it requires the arid NPP fraction to exceed the arctic's, which is the opposite of the assumed 0.3 vs 0.4) but the *magnitudes* of the regional ratios are not. Bias: the model holds the dragon's mass fixed across the move; in reality a dragon in a low-yield region would grow slower (less food), so the arid dragon would be *smaller* and its area demand lower than modeled — the model therefore over-states the arid penalty by ignoring growth-rate feedback.

### Problem

Subtask 6 — A real-world situation the dragon modeling informs. Goal: identify and discuss a non-fictional situation to which the same mass→metabolism→food→area→community mechanism and its shared-resource coupling transfer. Scope: the problem's 'outside the realm of fictional dragons' requirement; a discussion, not a new model.

### Analysis

The transferable structure is: a large endothermic consumer with allometric metabolic demand, foraging on a carrying-capacity-limited biomass, optionally supplemented by a human-managed supply, over a habitat of region-specific yield. The two structural findings — (i) the gross-diet (biomass-removal) basis is what sets the area, and (ii) the F×C shared-resource coupling where a wild draw and a managed draw on the same habitat must be certified jointly, not separately — are the insights that carry over. The most direct real-world analogue is the management of a large reintroduced apex predator (e.g. wolves, lynx, or bears) in a landscape shared with human agriculture: the predator's allometric prey demand sets a minimum habitat/forage area, the local livestock or game community is the 'prey biomass', and a human 'assistance' (compensation, managed culling, or supplemental feeding) draws on the same landscape's productive capacity. The shared-resource coupling is exactly the tension in such landscapes: the predator's wild take and the human-managed take (harvest, forage removal for livestock) are both certified against the same carrying capacity, and a plan that certifies each separately over-counts the landscape by up to 2× at intermediate assistance — the same double-count the dragon model's F×C correction removes. A second, more quantitative analogue is the siting of a large free-ranging livestock operation (e.g. an extensive cattle ranch) next to a protected area: the ranch's feed draw and the wildlife's forage draw on the same rangeland yield must be summed against one carrying capacity, and the conclusion-flip (a region cheap in one resource but expensive in another) mirrors the trade-off between a high-forage/low-carrying region and a low-forage/high-carrying region in ranch siting. Method and why it is sound: the transfer is at the *structural* level (the mechanism and its coupling), not the parameter level — the dragon's specific constants do not apply, but the allometric bridge, the gross-biomass area relation, and the joint-capacity constraint are the same equations with different constants, which is precisely what makes the modeling 'use many realistic features' as the problem requires.

### Modeling Process

No new equations; the transfer reuses Subtasks 1–4 with the dragon constants replaced by the real species'/operation's constants: BMR = 3.4·M^α W for the predator or herd; F_gross = E_met/η_assim/e_prey for the biomass demand; A_min = D_annual/B_eff for the minimum forage area; Pop(a) = D·a/s for the human-management size; and the joint constraint D_wild + D_managed ≤ B_eff·A for the shared landscape. The two structural findings carry over verbatim: the area is set by gross biomass removal (not metabolizable energy), and the wild + managed draws must be certified jointly against one carrying capacity.

### Outcome Analysis

What the dragon modeling contributes: (1) a defensible, sensitivity-honest allometric pipeline from consumer mass to habitat area, with the exponent, activity, and assimilation uncertainties carried as ranges rather than hidden in a single number — the same discipline a real predator-reintroduction or ranch-siting analysis needs. (2) the gross-diet basis: the area requirement is set by biomass removed from the ecosystem, a framing that real wildlife-management plans sometimes get wrong by reasoning in energy rather than in the prey/livestock biomass actually taken. (3) the F×C shared-resource correction: the most transferable single finding, because the error it fixes — certifying a wild take and a managed take separately and summing the areas — is a real, recurring mistake in landscape-scale coexistence planning, and it over-counts the landscape's capacity by up to 2× at intermediate assistance. (4) the conclusion-flip: the demonstration that 'most demanding' is resource-relative, so a siting or management recommendation that rests on a single metric (energy or area) can be reversed by the other — a caution for any real decision that trades off forage yield against thermoregulatory or carrying-cost. Limitations of the transfer: the dragon's fire-breathing, flight, and trauma-resistance have no real analogue, so the *magnitude* of the energy surcharge (k_activity) does not transfer; and the dragon's assumed uniform diet and steady growth are idealizations that real predators and ranches (seasonal, density-dependent) only approximately satisfy. The structural lessons, not the numbers, are what inform the real situation.
