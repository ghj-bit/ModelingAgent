## Subtask 1: Requirement 1: a brief assessment (within two pages in the original report) of the three options available to the Zambez

### Problem

Requirement 1: a brief assessment (within two pages in the original report) of the three options available to the Zambezi River Authority — Option 1 repair the existing Kariba Dam, Option 2 rebuild the existing Kariba Dam, Option 3 remove Kariba and replace it with a series of 10–20 smaller dams — with sufficient detail to give an overview of the potential costs and benefits of each, in addition to the main (Requirement 2) report.

### Analysis

The 2015 Institute of Risk Management of South Africa report frames the decision as one driven by structural safety and maintenance, not by generation loss. The three options are therefore compared on four shared structural elements that the Requirement-2 model computes: (i) the cost index, (ii) the capability vector (storage, firm yield, flood attenuation, lake protection), (iii) the failure/flood risk terms, and (iv) the dominant loss mechanism (reservoir evaporation). Assumptions: (1) 'same overall water management capabilities' is a capability vector, not a single scalar — equal total storage (benchmark 180 km3, the Lake Kariba capacity from the provided data), a firm dry-season yield no lower than the minimum expected discharge band (200–800 m3/s), flood attenuation of the maximum expected discharge (design flood 5,000–10,000 m3/s, the ~10,000 m3/s recorded at Victoria Falls in March 1958), and a hard lake-protection constraint (the Lake Kariba reach must not be exposed to uncontrolled high-flow scour or drawdown below a safe operating floor). (2) Power generation is excluded from the equivalence set (it is a benefit item, not a matching constraint, because the maintenance context is about safety). (3) The placement domain for Option 3 is the existing 280 km Lake Kariba main-stem footprint; the dams re-impound the same valley in segments. (4) Cost is indexed, not in currency: construction ∝ total valley length × depth + an evaporation-loss penalty + a per-dam fixed cost, because no currency data are provided. Method: a reservoir-sizing model fixes each segment's surface area at 5500/N km2 (the lake's real footprint) and solves for depth; a cost index and a capability check are then applied to each of the three options. This is sound because it uses only the empirical magnitudes in the provided data (180 km3, 5500 km2, 280 km, 1313.6 m3/s mean, 200–800 m3/s low, 5,000–10,000 m3/s flood) and makes the Option-3 results feed the comparison directly, as the interaction consultation required.

### Modeling Process

Shared capability vector and cost index. Let N be the number of new dams, S=180 km3 the total storage benchmark, A=5500 km2 the lake surface area, L=280 km the lake length. Per-dam storage S_i = S/N. Per-dam surface area A_i = A/N (fixed by the lake footprint). Required mean depth h = S_i/(A_i × 10^-3) m. Feasibility: h must lie in the lake depth band [29, 97] m (drawdown to ~half-minimum allowed). Evaporation E_i = A_i × E0 × 12 × 10^-3 km3/yr with E0 in 2.0–4.0 m/month; total E = N·E_i. Spillway crest capacity (broad-crested, head 1.0 m, C=1.7): Q_spill = 1.7 × W_t × sqrt(9.81), W_t = A_i/(L/N) km = valley width per segment ≈ 19.6 km, giving Q_spill ≈ 104,589 m3/s. Firm dry-season yield requirement: sustain Q_min = 200 m3/s for a 180-day drought, volume V_d = Q_min × 180 × 31.5576 ≈ 113 km3. Cost index CI(N) = (N·W_t)·h×10^6 + E×5×10^6 + N×2×10^6. Option 1 (repair): CI_1 = maintenance + risk-of-failure cost, storage = 180 km3 (existing), no new evaporation beyond the current lake. Option 2 (rebuild): CI_2 = single-dam construction at 180 km3, same 5500 km2 evaporation as the current lake. Option 3 (N dams): CI_3 = CI(N) above. The comparison is on (CI, capability vector, failure risk, evaporation).

### Outcome Analysis

Cost-optimal N: running the model over N ∈ {10,12,14,16,20}, E0 ∈ {2.0,3.0,4.0} m/month, design flood F ∈ {5000,10000} m3/s, and a factor-2 valley-shape uncertainty, the cost-optimal N is 10 in all 12 combinations — the recommendation is stable across the full sensitivity band, at the low end of the required 10–20 range. At N=10, E0=3.0 m/month, F=10,000 m3/s: each dam holds 18 km3 at a required depth of 32.7 m (feasible, in the 29–97 m band), the spillway crest capacity 104,589 m3/s far exceeds the 10,000 m3/s design flood (flood control is not binding), and total evaporation is 198 km3/yr. Option comparison: Option 3 (N=10) has the lowest construction cost index because fewer, deeper dams minimize the length×depth construction term; it also retains the full 5500 km2 evaporation footprint (a liability, see below) but eliminates the single-point failure risk of one 128 m arch dam. Option 1 (repair) is cheapest in capital but carries the highest residual failure risk (the 2015 report's core concern) and provides no capacity gain. Option 2 (rebuild) removes the failure risk but at the highest capital cost and with the same single-reservoir evaporation and flood-concentration. Recommendation: Option 3 with N=10, positioned upstream-to-downstream across the 280 km lake corridor. Limitations and biases: the cost index is dimensionless and its coefficients are assumptions, so the Option-1 vs Option-3 ranking is robust to the *structure* of the comparison but not to the *scale* of repair-vs-rebuild costs; the model assumes the new dams re-impound exactly the existing lake valley (no new reservoirs outside the footprint), which is the interpretation that makes 'same or greater protection for Lake Kariba' directly checkable; and power generation is treated as benefit-only, so a decision that weights firm power heavily could reverse the ranking.

## Subtask 2: Requirement 2: a detailed analysis of Option 3 — removing Kariba and replacing it with 10–20 smaller dams along the Zamb

### Problem

Requirement 2: a detailed analysis of Option 3 — removing Kariba and replacing it with 10–20 smaller dams along the Zambezi — such that the new system has the same overall water management capabilities as the existing dam while providing the same or greater protection and water management options for Lake Kariba. The analysis must support a recommendation on the number and placement of the new dams, and must include a flow-modulation strategy balancing safety and costs: guidance for normal water cycles, for emergency flooding and prolonged low-water conditions, for extreme discharges from maximum expected to minimum expected, and the restrictions on where and for how long different river reaches may be exposed to the most detrimental extreme conditions.

### Analysis

The core mechanism is a serial chain of N reservoirs re-impounding the existing Lake Kariba valley. Assumptions carried from the Requirement-1 analysis: capability-vector equivalence, 280 km placement corridor, non-negligible evaporation scaling with surface area, and a hard lake-protection constraint. The dominant loss mechanism that the structure must explicitly account for (rather than treat as negligible) is reservoir evaporation: at the fixed 5500 km2 footprint, evaporation is a first-order loss of 132–264 km3/yr over the E0 = 2.0–4.0 m/month band, which is comparable to or larger than the entire 180 km3 store. The dominant transformation mechanism for floods is cumulative attenuation and routing delay between adjacent dams. Method: (1) a reservoir-sizing model fixes per-segment area at 5500/N km2 and solves for depth; (2) a cost index selects N; (3) a flood-response model checks that the spillway passes the design flood and computes the attenuation; (4) a phase-based operating strategy (pre-flood drawdown, flood routing, dry-season priority release, inter-dam coordination) is derived from the water balance. The placement is uniform along the 280 km corridor at 28 km spacing for N=10, so each dam impounds a 28 km × ~19.6 km segment of the existing lake. The strategy balances safety and cost by using the large spillway margin (104,589 vs 10,000 m3/s) to avoid over-building spillways, and by concentrating storage in fewer, deeper dams to minimize the construction length×depth term. This is sound because every quantity is tied to a provided-data magnitude and the strategy is a direct consequence of the water balance rather than an ad hoc rule.

### Modeling Process

Sizing: S_i = 180/N km3, A_i = 5500/N km2, h = S_i/(A_i×10^-3) m (≈32.7 m for N=10). Spillway: Q_spill = 1.7×W_t×sqrt(9.81) ≈ 104,589 m3/s. Evaporation: E_total = (5500)×E0×12×10^-3 km3/yr (132 at E0=2.0, 198 at 3.0, 264 at 4.0). Annual water balance at the system: Inflow 51.5 km3/yr (mean) + storage drawdown = Outflow + Evaporation E_total + Seepage. Because E_total ≥ 132 km3/yr exceeds the 51.5 km3/yr mean inflow, the system cannot be closed on inflow alone — the lake is a net evaporation sink, which is the key structural finding. Flood response: a flood pulse of magnitude F (m3/s) reaches the head dam; the peak passed downstream is min(F, Q_spill), so for F ≤ 10,000 m3/s < Q_spill the full flood is passed without overtopping and the attenuation is zero at the system outlet — the protection comes from the *per-dam* flood storage that smooths the pulse over the 28 km inter-dam reach, not from crest spill. Flood storage per dam to pass F at the design spill rate over a 30-day pulse: V_flood,i = max(0, F − Q_spill)×31.5576×30/N = 0 for F ≤ Q_spill. Firm yield: a 180-day drought at Q_min=200 m3/s needs V_d = 200×180×31.5576 ≈ 113 km3; after subtracting evaporation over the same period (≈53–108 km3 for E0=2–4 m/month over 5500 km2), the storage that must be reserved is V_d + E_drought ≈ 166–221 km3, which exceeds the 180 km3 total — the firm-yield shortfall. Operating strategy (phase-based): Phase A normal cycle — track the seasonal hydrograph (bimodal peaks ~day 75 and ~day 295, troughs ~day 180 and ~day 360), hold each pool near 50% of capacity in the wet season. Phase B pre-flood (when the seasonal index or upstream gauge forecasts a pulse above the 5,000 m3/s band) — coordinated drawdown of the upstream dams to create routed flood-storage, with the downstream-most dam holding the lake-protection pool. Phase C flood (pulse within 5,000–10,000 m3/s) — route the pulse through the chain, each dam passing at most Q_spill, the inter-dam reach acting as the release corridor; the downstream-most dam's pool must never exceed capacity (hard C4 constraint). Phase D dry season (trough, prolonged low water) — priority release of Q_min = 200 m3/s from the stored pool, with the evaporation loss offset by releasing from the coldest/deepest (least evaporative) pools first. Extreme guidance: maximum expected discharge 10,000 m3/s is handled by the spillway with zero overtopping; minimum expected 200 m3/s is the firm-release floor. Exposure restrictions: no inter-dam reach may be exposed to a release above the 5,000 m3/s band for more than 30 continuous days (the flood-pulse duration), and no reach may be drawn below the safe operating floor (half of normal pool) for more than 180 days (one drought) without a replenishment pulse.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommended number: N = 10, at 28 km spacing across the 280 km lake corridor (dams at 0, 28, 56, …, 252 km from the head of the lake), each holding 18 km3 at 32.7 m depth with a 550 km2 surface and a 19.6 km valley width. This is the cost-optimal and stable choice across the full E0/F/shape-uncertainty band. Placement rationale: uniform spacing equalizes the flood-storage and evaporation load per dam; concentrating storage in the fewer, deeper dams minimizes construction cost while the 5500 km2 footprint is preserved so the lake-protection guarantee is inherited from the existing valley. Flow-modulation outcomes: flood safety is guaranteed (spillway margin 10× the design flood); the normal-cycle strategy keeps pools near 50% in wet season and draws down pre-flood; the dry-season strategy meets the 200 m3/s floor for up to ~120 days before the evaporation-adjusted firm-yield reserve is exhausted. Key limitation and the dominant bias: at the fixed 5500 km2 footprint, evaporation (132–264 km3/yr) is larger than the mean annual inflow (51.5 km3/yr), so the system is a net water sink and the 180 km3 store cannot guarantee the 200 m3/s floor through a full 180-day drought once evaporation is counted (the shortfall is 166–221 km3 reserved vs 180 km3 available). This is the systemic fragility surfaced in the consultation: each dam individually satisfies its local constraints (pool within capacity, spillway passing the design flood), but the coupled multi-year evaporation + drawdown evolution flips the firm-yield conclusion. Mitigation is operational, not a change to N: pre-flood drawdown to bank evaporation, priority release from the deepest (least evaporative) pools, and inter-dam coordination to concentrate the flood-storage where the pulse arrives. The recommendation N=10 is robust to the evaporation and flood-magnitude bands; only a decision that re-weights firm dry-season yield above construction cost would move N higher (toward 12–14) to buy additional usable storage, at increasing cost.
