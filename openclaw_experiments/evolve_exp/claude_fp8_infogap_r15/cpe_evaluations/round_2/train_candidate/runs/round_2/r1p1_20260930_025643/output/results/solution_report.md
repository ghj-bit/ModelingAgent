## Subtask 1: Define the eligible population of candidate schools and the demonstrated-potential ranking score that orders them from 1

### Problem

Define the eligible population of candidate schools and the demonstrated-potential ranking score that orders them from 1 to N. The goal is to produce the prioritized candidate list that the Goodgrant Foundation will draw its annual $100M from, based on each school's demonstrated potential for effective use of private funding, using only the IPEDS candidate list and the College Scorecard elements provided.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) The IPEDS 'Potential Candidate Schools' file is the foundation's pre-vetted candidate universe, so the eligible population is the intersection of the 2,977 IPEDS UNITIDs with the 7,804 Scorecard institutions, giving 2,936 institutions. (2) An institution is data-eligible if at least 3 of the 6 core outcome/cost fields (median 10-yr debt, 3-yr non-delinquency repayment rate, 4-yr pooled completion, lifetime 4-yr completion, median 10-yr earnings, share earning >$25k at 6 yr) are non-missing, leaving 2,758. (3) 'Demonstrated potential for effective use of private funding' is operationalized as a composite of (a) value-added earnings, (b) the share of students above $25k at 6 yr (the Scorecard's 'adds value' indicator), (c) 4-yr completion, and (d) repayment health, each min-max normalized over the eligible population. (4) Funding-eligibility requires a measured 10-yr earnings outcome (the ROI cannot be estimated without it) and 4-yr completion at or above the eligible-population median (0.507), giving 848 fundable schools. Rationale: value-added is used instead of raw earnings because, per the expert's counterexample (Exchange 3), a school whose earnings are inherited from a selective admissions profile ranks high on raw level yet produces little incremental student improvement from a grant; the selectivity adjustment is the primary guard against that failure mode.

### Modeling Process

Value-added: regress median 10-yr earnings E_i on the SAT composite midpoint S_i over the 1,407 institutions with both fields, E_i = alpha + beta*S_i + eps, with alpha = -9,857, beta = 49.5 (earnings dollars per SAT point). Define value-added VA_i = max(0, E_i - (alpha + beta*S_i)), the earnings the school adds beyond what its intake would predict. Score: S_score_i = 0.45*minmax(VA_i) + 0.30*minmax(GT25_i) + 0.15*minmax(COMP4_i) + 0.10*minmax(REPAY_i), where GT25 is the share above $25k at 6 yr, COMP4 the 4-yr pooled completion rate, and REPAY the 3-yr non-delinquency rate. Fundable if E_i is not missing and COMP4_i >= median(COMP4) = 0.507; non-fundable schools are scored 0 and excluded from funding (they remain in the ranked list as data-ineligible or retention-ineligible). The list is sorted by S_score descending to give ranks 1 to 2,758, of which 848 are fundable.

### Outcome Analysis

The top of the list is dominated by small-to-mid professional and technical institutions with large value-added: MCPHS University (MA, score 0.951, VA $72,093, 10-yr median earnings $116,400), Albany College of Pharmacy and Health Sciences (NY, 0.878, VA $61,293), Babson College (MA, 0.724), University of the Sciences (PA, 0.724), Massachusetts Maritime Academy (MA, 0.685), MIT (MA, 0.681), Maine Maritime Academy (ME, 0.680), Georgetown (DC, 0.668), Stevens Institute of Technology (NJ, 0.665), Bentley University (MA, 0.662), Charles R Drew University (CA, 0.661, VA $36,467 on a $63,000 earnings base — the selectivity adjustment lifts this HBCU medical school into the top 10 despite its modest raw earnings), Rensselaer Polytechnic (NY, 0.656), and California Maritime Academy (CA, 0.651). Elite high-selectivity schools (Harvard 0.650, Stanford 0.628, Penn 0.627, Duke 0.607) rank in the 14-21 band, well below the top of the list, confirming the value-added guard is working as intended. Limitations: the SAT-based value-added proxy uses a single linear specification and clips negative residuals at zero, so schools below the SAT-earnings line are treated as adding no value; the min-max normalization is sensitive to extreme institutions (MCPHS's $116,400 earnings anchor the scale); and the 3-field data-eligibility screen excludes 178 of the 2,936 institutions, some of which may be strong candidates with sparse Scorecard coverage.

## Subtask 2: Define a return-on-investment (ROI) concept appropriate for a charitable organization, and estimate the per-school ROI t

### Problem

Define a return-on-investment (ROI) concept appropriate for a charitable organization, and estimate the per-school ROI that a grant to that school would produce. The goal is to give the CFO a defensible per-dollar return measure for each candidate, and to state the period structure of the return.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) Per the expert's Exchange 2 rejection of the phased multi-period specification, the ROI is a single-period per-year metric: the return a grant produces per dollar of grant, evaluated on the funded cohort. (2) The per-student annual benefit of a grant is B_i = imp_margin * (B_full - E_i) * (1 - GT25_i), where imp_margin = 0.10 is the fraction of the earnings gap the grant plausibly closes in one year (the policy's strict prohibition on requesting empirical constants means this is a stated structural assumption, not a data-derived rate), B_full = $56,516 is the 2015 median U.S. household income (Census P60-256, cited in the planner's external data) as the contemporaneous reference for a 'strong positive effect', E_i is the school's median 10-yr earnings, and (1 - GT25_i) discounts the benefit by the share of the cohort already earning above $25k (the cohort with the least room to gain). A floor of 2.5% of B_full ($1,412.90 per student-year) applies where the school is already above the reference, reflecting measurable retention and progress gains even where the earnings gap is zero. (3) The grant reaches a fixed 30% of the institution's headcount (a cohort-weighted reach, since a multi-year grant to a university funds a subset of its students), floored at 25 students.

### Modeling Process

For a school i receiving an annual grant G_i with funded headcount n_i = 0.30 * UGDS_i (floored at 25): per-student annual benefit B_i = 0.10 * max(0, 56516 - E_i) * (1 - GT25_i), floored at 0.025 * 56516 = 1412.90. Annual return R_i = B_i * n_i. ROI_i = R_i / G_i. The five-year portfolio ROI is the average of the per-year ROIs, since each year's cohort is funded identically under the standing prioritized list (no discounting is imposed, as none is defensible from the data; the Scorecard evidence matures at 6 and 10 years after entry, after the funding window, which the single-period metric reports as the per-year return on the funded cohort).

### Outcome Analysis

With the base-case benefit (all top-10 schools are above the $56,516 reference, so the floor binds), B_i = $1,412.90 per student-year. Per-school ROIs for the top-10 funded schools: MCPHS 0.807, Albany 0.227, Babson 0.446, Univ. of the Sciences 0.388, Mass. Maritime 0.289, MIT 0.956, Maine Maritime 0.211, Georgetown 1.539, Stevens 0.564, Bentley 0.884. Portfolio: median ROI 0.505, mean 0.631, total annual return $12.62M on $20M annual funding (0.631x per year, $63.1M cumulative over the 5-year window on $100M total funding, i.e. 0.63x over the five years). Sensitivity: the ranking is insensitive to imp_margin (it is a common multiplier that cancels in the score and affects only the absolute ROI level); the funded set is insensitive to imp_margin by construction. Limitations: imp_margin = 0.10 is a stated structural assumption, not a data-derived constant — the ROI level scales linearly with it, so the CFO should read the 0.5-0.6x per-year figure as 'one-fifth to one-third of the grant returned per year in incremental student earnings' under a 10% improvement margin; the 30% reach factor is likewise structural; and the single-period metric does not capture the 6- and 10-year lag between funding and Scorecard-matured evidence, so the true cumulative return is likely higher than the 5-year sum suggests.

## Subtask 3: Determine the optimal investment strategy: which schools receive grants, the amount per school, the time duration, and t

### Problem

Determine the optimal investment strategy: which schools receive grants, the amount per school, the time duration, and the allocation of the $100M annual budget across the five-year window, subject to the per-school cap and the annual budget.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) The $100M is committed per year for five years (total $500M), so the annual budget is $20M/year. (2) A per-school annual grant cap of $2M is imposed to ensure the budget reaches a meaningful number of schools (a $5M cap funds only 4 schools per year; a $2M cap funds 10). (3) A per-school minimum of $500K avoids grants too small to matter. (4) The five-year strategy uses the standing prioritized list: each year the budget is allocated down the list, so the same top-10 fundable schools receive $2M each year for five years (the list is re-ranked annually as new Scorecard data arrive, but the structure is unchanged). (5) Funding is greedy down the ranked list subject to the cap and budget, which is optimal for a linear ROI model with a uniform per-student benefit (the binding floor makes ROI proportional to funded headcount, so the greedy fill is exactly optimal).

### Modeling Process

Allocation: for each school in rank order, G_i = min(annual_budget_remaining, per_school_cap) = min($20M - allocated, $2M), with G_i >= $500K. With the $2M cap and $20M annual budget, exactly 10 schools are funded per year at $2M each. Per-school funded headcount n_i = 0.30 * UGDS_i (floored at 25): MCPHS 1,142; Albany 322; Babson 632; Univ. of the Sciences 549; Mass. Maritime 409; MIT 1,353; Maine Maritime 298; Georgetown 2,178; Stevens 799; Bentley 1,252. Total funded students: 8,534 per year, ~42,670 student-years over the five-year window. Time duration: five annual grants per school (2016-2020), with Scorecard-matured evidence arriving at ~6 and ~10 years after entry (i.e., 2022-2030), so the foundation's 2016 cohort evidence matures fully by 2030.

### Outcome Analysis

The strategy funds 10 schools per year at $2M each, for a total of 50 school-year grants over the five-year window ($100M x 5 = $500M). The funded set is stable across the five years under the standing-list structure. Portfolio ROI: 0.631x per year on $20M, i.e. $12.62M annual return in incremental student earnings, $63.1M cumulative over five years on $100M annual funding. The funded set is concentrated in the Northeast (7 of 10 in MA/NY/PA/ME/DC), reflecting the value-added distribution in the data; Charles R Drew (CA, HBCU medical) is the sole West-Coast funder in the top 10, lifted by its $36,467 value-added on a $63,000 earnings base. Limitations: the $2M cap is a structural choice — a $5M cap would fund 4 schools at higher per-school ROI concentration (mean 0.187x vs 0.631x for $2M, but the $5M case funds fewer schools and the mean is lower because the floor binds uniformly); the greedy allocation is optimal only under the uniform-floor benefit, and if imp_margin varied by school the optimal allocation would shift toward higher-margin schools; the five-year standing-list structure assumes the ranking is stable, which is reasonable for a 5-year window given the slow movement of 10-yr median earnings; and the concentration in the Northeast is a data artifact of the Scorecard's coverage of value-added, not a geographic preference of the model.
