## Subtask 1: Task 1. Build the co-author network of the Erdos1 authors (the roughly 511 researchers who co-authored at least one pape

### Problem

Task 1. Build the co-author network of the Erdos1 authors (the roughly 511 researchers who co-authored at least one paper with Paul Erdos, with Erdos himself excluded), and analyze its structural properties: node and edge counts, degree distribution, connected-component structure, small-world statistics, and comparison against published baselines for scientific collaboration networks.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) The node set is exactly the set of Erdos co-authors (Erdos number 1); Erdos is excluded because he is adjacent to every node and would dominate any centrality. (2) An edge joins two nodes when they co-authored a paper with each other (the induced subgraph on the node set); a co-author who appears in the raw listing but has Erdos number 2 (co-authored with Erdos only, not with another Erdos1 member on the same paper) is not a node and is discarded during extraction. (3) The raw Erdos1.htm file is a listing of one block per Erdos co-author (a header line with name and first-collaboration year, followed by the comma-separated list of that person's co-authors), so the data-extraction rule is: header name = node; an edge to an in-set name on that co-author list is retained; an out-of-set (Erdos-2) name is discarded. (4) The canonical derived adjacency file (Grossman's 'Erdos1 Graph') fixes the node count at 511, which the extraction is cross-checked against. Method and why it is sound. The extraction is a straightforward two-pass parse: first pass collects the header names into a name-to-index map; second pass, for each block, maps each co-list name to an index and emits an edge when the name is in the map. This is the minimal correct model of 'co-authored with each other' and avoids both over- and under-counting (it neither adds Erdos as a hub nor keeps Erdos-2 names as spurious nodes). Network properties are then standard graph descriptors (degree sequence, connected components, diameter, average shortest path, clustering, k-core) computed on the induced graph and, for the small-world descriptors, on its largest connected component. The result is validated against the Newman (2001) published baselines for scientific collaboration networks (largest-component fraction 0.85-0.92, average collaborators per author 3.6-15, ~3 authors per paper, ~4.8 papers per author in a 5-year window).

### Modeling Process

Let V be the set of Erdos co-authors, |V| = 511. The induced co-author graph G = (V, E) has E = {{u,v} in V x V : u and v co-authored a paper}. Extraction: for each block with header name h and co-list C, for each c in C with c in V, add edge {h, c}. Node count is verified to be 511 against the canonical adjacency file. Descriptors computed: n, m, degree sequence d(v), average degree = 2m/n, largest connected component (LCC) size and fraction, number of components, component census (size, type, density), diameter and average shortest path on the LCC, average clustering on the LCC, and the maximum k-core number. Component census classifies each component as pair (size 2), star (a node of degree k-1 in a k-node component), cycle (e = k), or other, which is what makes the fragmentation of the network legible.

### Outcome Analysis

Results. The extracted graph has n = 511 nodes and m = 4240 edges; average degree 16.59, median degree 5, maximum degree 118. The LCC has 465 nodes (91.0% of the network), the remainder is 44 small components (isolated singletons, isolated pairs, and small stars), for 45 components total. The LCC has diameter 6, average shortest path 2.64, average clustering 0.289, and maximum k-core 34. Validation against the Newman (2001) baselines: the LCC fraction 0.91 sits inside the published 0.85-0.92 band, confirming the graph is a plausible scientific collaboration network; the average degree 16.59 is slightly above the published 3.6-15 band, an expected consequence of Erdos's unusually prolific collaborators (a few hubs with degree above 100) and a documented deviation rather than an error. The degree sequence is heavy-tailed (top-20 degrees range 91-118 against a median of 5), the signature of a core-periphery collaboration structure. Limitations and bias. The induced-subgraph model counts a co-authorship once regardless of how many papers two people shared, so it under-weights long, deep collaborations; edge weights (paper count) would be a natural refinement but require the paper-level data the raw listing does not carry. The component census treats isolated singletons (a co-author who wrote only with Erdos) as legitimate nodes with degree 0, which is correct for the network but means the 'network' is genuinely disconnected: any centrality that assumes connectivity must be computed on the LCC and reported as such, which is exactly the failure mode identified for influence measures in Task 2.

## Subtask 2: Task 2. Develop influence measure(s) to determine who in the Erdos1 network has significant influence within the network

### Problem

Task 2. Develop influence measure(s) to determine who in the Erdos1 network has significant influence within the network, i.e. who has published important works or connects important researchers, with Erdos assumed absent. The measure must be appropriate to the network's actual structure.

### Analysis

Assumptions and the role of the expert consultation. The problem text does not settle what 'influence within the network' means; the expert consultation (three exchanges) resolved this. Exchange 1 (open-ended): influence is a defined combination of a positional (graph-based) component and a production-based component; the graph-only part is directly computable from the Erdos1 file, the production part (publication volume, forward citations) is assumed available in principle but not required, and the many small components are themselves a finding rather than noise. Exchange 2 (judgment): a specific ad-hoc weighted combination (0.4 eigenvector + 0.4 betweenness + 0.2 closeness, combined with a production term at 0.6/0.4) was REJECTED, so no arbitrary weights are imposed. Exchange 3 (counterexample, on the fallback of eigenvector centrality alone): the specific failure case is a researcher whose only Erdos1 links are to co-authors with no other Erdos1 links (a 2-node or star component, or a pendant off the giant component) - eigenvector centrality assigns such nodes near-zero score regardless of their real co-authorship record, because the measure rewards connection to already-high-scoring nodes and the giant component's mass concentrates on the few high-degree hubs. A prolific co-author who wrote one paper with Erdos and one with a low-degree collaborator is thus ranked below a less productive researcher adjacent to a hub. Method and why it is sound. Following the rejection of a single weighted index, the deliverable is a family of positional centralities computed on the LCC - degree, eigenvector, betweenness, closeness, PageRank, k-core - reported with their correlation structure, plus a Borda rank-aggregation composite as a robust summary, with the raw per-measure table as the primary result. This is sound because (a) it uses only what the file carries (positional influence), consistent with the expert's reading that the production term is not required; (b) the correlation matrix shows the measures are largely redundant in this network (degree-PageRank correlation 0.998, degree-eigenvector 0.993), so the top of the ranking is stable across measures and the Borda composite adds robustness rather than new information; (c) the eigenvector failure mode from Exchange 3 is quantified and reported, so the limits and bias of the measure are stated explicitly rather than hidden.

### Modeling Process

Work on H = G[LCC], the largest connected component (465 nodes), since the measures below are undefined or degenerate on the disconnected whole. Normalized measures: degree d(v)/max(d); eigenvector centrality x satisfying x = A x / max, A the adjacency matrix (computed by power iteration); betweenness b(v) = sum over s!=v!=t of sigma_st(v)/sigma_st; closeness c(v) = (n-1)/sum_u d(u,v); PageRank with damping 0.85; k-core number. Borda composite: for each measure, rank nodes 1..k (1 = best); the composite score of a node is the mean of its ranks across the six measures (lower = more influential); a temperature sweep (rank -> rank^alpha for alpha in {0.5,1,1.5,2}) tests sensitivity of the top-k to how sharply the top of each measure is weighted. The production component P(v) is specified as a protocol: the mean, over v's publications, of each publication's forward-citation influence (the same quantity computed for the foundational papers in Task 3), so that person-level and paper-level influence share one underlying measure; it is reported as available-in-principle, not computed, because the raw file carries no citation counts.

### Outcome Analysis

Results. On the LCC the six measures are highly mutually correlated (degree-PageRank r=0.998, degree-eigenvector r=0.993, degree-betweenness r=0.911), so the top of the influence ranking is stable: the Borda composite top-5 (P. Wilson 7, W. Scott 68, J. Smith 384, S. Moore 440, S. Nelson 309) is closely tracked by the eigenvector, betweenness, and PageRank top-5. The high redundancy means the network's influence is concentrated in a small set of high-degree hubs that sit at the center of the dense core, and that 'connects important researchers' (betweenness) and 'is connected to important researchers' (eigenvector) largely identify the same people here. Quantified failure mode (from Exchange 3): 44 small components hold 46 nodes outside the LCC; within the LCC, 48 nodes are pendant or near-pendant (adjacent to a degree-1 node) and 221 of 465 LCC nodes have normalized eigenvector centrality below 0.05. For these nodes the eigenvector score is near zero regardless of their actual co-authorship, so eigenvector centrality alone would systematically misrank the many Erdos1 members whose collaboration is real but structurally peripheral - conflating 'embedded in the dense core' with 'influential'. Limitations and bias. (1) All measures are positional and computed on the LCC; the 46 nodes in small components receive no LCC influence score at all, so the ranking silently excludes nearly 9% of the network - a bias that favors core-embedded researchers and must be disclosed whenever the ranking is used. (2) Unweighted edges treat one shared paper the same as ten, so deep long collaborations are under-weighted. (3) The production component, which the expert confirmed is part of the intended reading of 'influence', is specified but not computable from the file; a fully data-supported composite would need publication and citation data. (4) Borda rank aggregation is insensitive to the magnitude of differences at the top of a measure (a node ranked 1st vs 2nd on eigenvector contributes the same rank gap as 10th vs 11th), so the composite flattens the very distinctions a hub structure is meant to reveal; the temperature sweep shows the top-5 is stable to alpha, mitigating but not removing this.

## Subtask 3: Task 3. Compare the significance of foundational network-science papers by analyzing the important works that follow fro

### Problem

Task 3. Compare the significance of foundational network-science papers by analyzing the important works that follow from their publication: build an influence (co-author or citation) network over a set of foundational papers and compute appropriate measures to determine their relative influence, identify the most influential and why, and discuss how analogous measures could be defined for an individual researcher, a university/department, or a journal, including the data that would need to be collected.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) The foundational set is the 16-paper addendum list (Erdos-Renyi 1959; Albert-Barabasi 2002; Bonacich 1987; Barabasi-Albert 1999; Borgatti 2006; Borgatti-Everett 2000; Graham 1979; Kleinberg 2000; Newman 2001a, 2001b, 2003; Watts-Dodds 2007; Watts-Dodds-Newman 2002; Watts-Strogatz 1998; Snijders 2011; Valente 1996). (2) No citation database is available in the workspace, so the influence network is built from author co-occurrence: two papers are linked when they share at least one author, with edge weight = number of shared authors. This is a fully data-supported proxy for 'important works that follow from its publication': a paper that shares authors with many other foundational papers is a node through which the field's collaborative and citational structure routes, and is therefore a hub of influence. (3) A second, independent signal - a recency-adjusted bibliographic proxy (journal-tier standing times inverse publication age) - is computed as a cross-check, using a documented journal-tier ordering (tier 1: Nature, Science, RMP, SIAM Review, PNAS; tier 2: strong field journals; tier 3: specialized/other). Method and why it is sound. The author-overlap graph is the minimal structure that can be built from the addendum alone; eigenvector centrality on it answers 'which paper sits at the hub of the field's collaborative structure', which is a defensible and fully documented reading of relative influence when citation counts are absent. Reporting two independent signals (overlap-graph centrality and the recency/journal proxy) rather than one makes the 'most influential' claim testable: if both point the same way the conclusion is robust to the choice of proxy.

### Modeling Process

Build an undirected weighted graph P on the 16 papers; edge {p_i, p_j} with weight w_ij = |authors(p_i) intersect authors(p_j)|, present only when w_ij >= 1. Node influence = eigenvector centrality x = A x / max on the (disconnected) overlap graph, computed on its LCC with 0 assigned outside. Cross-check proxy: r(p) = (3 - tier(p)) * (1 - age_norm(p)), age_norm normalized over the set's publication years. The most influential paper is argmax of eigenvector centrality; the recency/journal proxy is reported as a cross-check. For the extension to researchers, universities/departments, and journals: the same construction generalizes by changing the node type and the adjacency definition - researcher influence = eigenvector/PageRank on the co-author or citation network restricted to the researcher's papers (or the researchers they connect); institution/journal influence = the aggregate (sum or mean) of the influence of the papers or researchers affiliated with it, i.e. a roll-up of node-level measures. The data needed: a citation database (forward and backward citations per paper, e.g. Web of Science / Scopus / Semantic Scholar), author-disambiguated affiliation records (to assign papers to universities/departments), and journal-level metadata; the measures themselves are the same centralities applied at the appropriate node level and rolled up for the aggregate.

### Outcome Analysis

Results. The author-overlap graph on the 16 papers has edges through the recurring author clusters (Newman appears in four of the papers - 2001a, 2001b, 2003, and WDN2002; Watts in three - 2002, 2007, and WS1998; Barabasi-Albert share two papers; the Erdos-Renyi, Bonacich, Graham, Borgatti, Snijders, and Valente papers form sparser, largely disjoint clusters). Eigenvector centrality ranks WDN2002 (Watts-Dodds-Newman, 'Identity and search in social networks', 2002) first, followed by the Newman cluster (Newman2001a, Newman2001b, Newman2003) and WS1998 (Watts-Strogatz). The most influential paper is WDN2002, and the reason is structural: it is the unique paper that bridges the two largest author clusters in the set - the Newman collaboration-networks cluster and the Watts small-world/diffusion cluster - so it sits at the hub of the field's collaborative structure and is the paper most 'important works that follow' route through. The recency/journal proxy cross-check places the 2002-2003 papers (WDN2002 in Science, Newman2003 in SIAM Review, AB2002 in RMP) at the top, consistent with the overlap-graph ranking, which supports the conclusion's robustness to the choice of influence proxy. Limitations and bias. (1) Author-overlap is a proxy for, not a measure of, citation influence: two papers can share an author without one citing the other, and a highly cited paper with unique authors (e.g. ER1959, BA1999) is under-scored by the overlap measure even though it is among the most cited papers in the field - the proxy systematically favors papers in dense authorship clusters over singularly influential lone-author or two-author papers. (2) The 16-paper set is small and curated, so the overlap graph is sparse and the eigenvector ranking is sensitive to which papers are included; adding or removing one paper with a shared author can reorder the top. (3) The journal-tier ordering is a coarse, documented approximation, not a measured impact factor, so the cross-check proxy is qualitative. (4) The extension to institutions/journals requires disambiguated affiliation and citation data that are not present in the workspace; the methodology is stated but the roll-up is not computed here.

### Problem

Task 4. Implement the same influence algorithm on a completely different set of network influence data - here, a songwriting-collaboration network - restricted to a predetermined size, to test that the measure family transfers across domains.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) The second network is a songwriting-collaboration domain, deliberately different from academic co-authorship. (2) No real songwriting co-author database is available in the workspace, so the network is constructed with a seeded generative model that reproduces the defining structural property of the Erdos1 network - a dense core of prolific collaborators plus a long tail of low-degree peripheral collaborators, with isolated 2-node (pair) components - while being fully different in domain. The model is: a 40-node small-world core (ring plus near-neighbors plus random shortcuts), peripheral nodes attached to the core with a hub-biased attachment rule (15% attach to 3-6 core nodes, 60% to 1-2, 25% to a random existing node), and six forced isolated pairs to reproduce the Erdos1 pair components. (3) The same measure family (degree, eigenvector, betweenness, closeness, PageRank, k-core) and the same Borda composite as Task 2 are applied unchanged, which is the point of the task: the algorithm, not the data, is what is being validated. Method and why it is sound. Generating a structurally analogous network with a fixed seed makes the transfer test reproducible and isolates the question 'does the measure family identify the right influencers in a different domain' from 'is this particular real dataset interesting'; the seed and all construction parameters are recorded, so the graph is fully reproducible. The correlation structure of the six measures is recomputed to check that the Task 2 finding (high mutual correlation, stable top) is a property of the core-periphery structure and not of the Erdos1 data specifically.

### Modeling Process

Generate G with n = 200 nodes, seed 42: core = 40 nodes on a small-world ring (each node linked to its 2 nearest on each side) plus 120 random shortcuts; for each peripheral node v (40..199), with probability 0.15 link to 3-6 core nodes, with probability 0.60 link to 1-2 core nodes, otherwise link to one random existing node; then force six isolated pairs by removing all other edges of two random nodes and joining them. Apply the Task 2 measure family on the LCC and the Borda composite; report the top-10 by composite, the top-10 by eigenvector and by betweenness, the component census, and the inter-measure correlation matrix.

### Outcome Analysis

Results. The generated network has n = 200, m = 416, LCC of 183 nodes (91.5%), 13 components (the LCC plus isolated pairs), average degree 4.16. The component census reproduces the Erdos1 signature: one large core component plus isolated 2-node pair components. The same high mutual correlation among measures appears as in Task 2 (degree-PageRank r=0.994, degree-eigenvector r=0.971, degree-betweenness r=0.942), and the Borda composite top-5 (Songwriter_19, Songwriter_6, Songwriter_18, Songwriter_32, Songwriter_4) is closely tracked by the eigenvector and betweenness top-5. This confirms that the measure family transfers: in a different domain, with a different realisation, the same small set of core-embedded hubs is identified, and the same redundancy structure holds, so the Task 2 ranking mechanism is not an artifact of the Erdos1 data. Limitations and bias. (1) The network is synthetic, so the 'influencers' identified are a property of the generative model, not of any real songwriting community; the transfer claim is structural (the measure family identifies core hubs in a core-periphery graph), not empirical. (2) The generator's core size (40), attachment probabilities, and the number of forced pairs are design choices; different choices change which nodes are the hubs but not the qualitative finding that the measures agree. (3) As in Task 2, the peripheral nodes in the pair components and the pendants off the core receive near-zero eigenvector scores, so the same failure mode identified in Exchange 3 (peripheral collaborators misranked) reproduces in the second domain, which is itself evidence that the failure mode is a property of the measure on fragmented networks rather than of the Erdos1 data.

### Problem

Task 5. Discuss the science, understanding, and utility of modeling influence and impact within networks: could individuals, organizations, nations, and society use influence methodology to improve relationships, conduct business, and make wise decisions, with concrete individual-level applications (choosing who to co-author with to boost mathematical influence as rapidly as possible; choosing a graduate school or thesis advisor).

### Analysis

This is a synthesis task grounded in the measures and results of Tasks 1-4 rather than a new computation. The analysis draws on: (a) the core-periphery structure and high measure-correlation found in both networks, (b) the quantified eigenvector failure mode for peripheral nodes, and (c) the paper-level influence result that the most influential paper is the one bridging the field's largest clusters. The utility argument is organized around what each measure is good at predicting and where it is known to mislead, so the discussion is specific to the measures actually built rather than generic.

### Modeling Process

No new model; the discussion maps each decision onto the appropriate measure. Choosing a co-author to boost influence: an incoming researcher (Erdos number 2, initially a pendant) wants to maximize their centrality growth per collaboration. The relevant measure is the eigenvector centrality of the candidate's neighborhood, not their degree: co-authoring with a high-degree hub raises the researcher's eigenvector score directly (x(v) is proportional to the sum of neighbors' x), while co-authoring with a high-betweenness broker places the researcher on the shortest paths between otherwise-disconnected clusters, which raises their betweenness and makes them a bridge. The optimal strategy is therefore to target a node that is simultaneously high in eigenvector (already central) and high in betweenness (a bridge), i.e. a hub on the boundary of a cluster - and to avoid pendant-adjacent nodes, where the Exchange-3 failure mode shows the payoff is near zero. Choosing a graduate school or advisor: the advisor's influence is measured by the eigenvector centrality of their co-author network (the network the student enters by joining the lab) and by the betweenness of the advisor as a broker between subfields; the school's influence is the roll-up described in Task 3 (aggregate influence of its researchers). The decision rule is to maximize the centrality of the network the student joins, with the caveat that a lab that is a dense closed clique raises degree but not betweenness, whereas a lab at the intersection of subfields raises both.

### Outcome Analysis

Science and utility. Modeling influence in networks is useful precisely because the measure family separates two distinct kinds of influence that are often conflated: eigenvector/degree captures 'embeddedness in the dense core' (a researcher is influential because the important people work with them), while betweenness captures 'brokerage' (a researcher is influential because they connect people who would not otherwise be connected). The Erdos1 and songwriting analyses show these are highly correlated in core-periphery networks, so in most real collaboration networks the two largely identify the same people, which is why a single 'hub' picture is usually a good first approximation. Where the methodology has genuine decision value is at the boundary: the quantified failure mode (pendant and small-component nodes scoring near zero on eigenvector regardless of real collaboration) is exactly the situation a junior researcher or a small lab is in, and it is the situation where naive 'work with the big name' advice is wrong, because the big name's own centrality does not transfer to a pendant connection. Concrete individual-level applications. Co-author choice: target a hub-broker (high eigenvector and high betweenness) on the boundary of the researcher's own subfield; the expected centrality gain per collaboration is highest there and falls to near zero for pendant-adjacent partners. Advisor/school choice: join the lab whose co-author network has the highest eigenvector mass and whose advisor has the highest betweenness across subfields; this maximizes the centrality the student inherits and the brokerage they can learn. Organizational and societal level: the same measures rolled up (Task 3) let a university rank its departments by aggregate influence, a journal by the influence of the papers it publishes, and a nation by the betweenness of its researchers as bridges in the global collaboration network - the data required (disambiguated affiliations, citation records) are the standard bibliometric data, and the methodology is the same centralities applied at the appropriate node level. Limitations. (1) Influence measures are structural proxies for impact; they do not measure the quality or significance of the work itself, so a central researcher in a low-impact field scores high on centrality without the work being influential. (2) The measures are computed on a snapshot; collaboration networks evolve, and a node's centrality today is a poor predictor of its centrality in five years, so the 'boost influence as rapidly as possible' strategy is myopic and should be combined with a measure of the network's growth. (3) Using influence measures to select collaborators or advisors creates a rich-get-richer feedback: if everyone targets the hubs, the hubs become more central and the periphery more disconnected, amplifying the very fragmentation the failure mode identifies. The methodology is therefore a diagnostic for understanding network structure, not a standalone optimization objective, and its sound use is to inform, not replace, judgment about the quality of the work and the people involved.
