# Solution

## Subtask 1: Requirement 1: Construct a mathematical model to identify the best 3-D geometric shape to use as a sandcastle foundation

### Problem

Requirement 1: Construct a mathematical model to identify the best 3-D geometric shape to use as a sandcastle foundation that lasts the longest on a seashore with waves and tides, under the conditions that all castles are built at roughly the same distance from the water on the same beach, from the same type of sand, roughly the same amount of sand, and the same water-to-sand proportion. Scope: define a survival metric, a wave/tide forcing scenario, a shape family, and an erosion mechanism; rank candidate shapes by longevity and state the optimal shape and the conditions under which the ranking is valid.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (A1) The problem does not define 'lasts the longest'; an expert consultation confirmed this is an interpretation gap, not a data gap, and that three candidate failure definitions (first crown inundation, a volume-loss fraction, loss of structural integrity) give different optimal shapes. I therefore adopt as the primary survival metric the time to 50% volume loss under a stated baseline scenario, and I state it as an explicit modeling choice rather than a fact the text carries. (A2) The forcing scenario is fixed to the gathered empirical data: significant wave height H_s = 1 m, wave period T = 8 s, micro-tidal range ~1 m (Masselink & Short 2005; NOAA), with sensitivity ranges H_s in [0.5, 2] m and T in [4, 10] s. (A3) All foundations have the same volume V (the 'same amount of sand'), are placed with their base at the same distance d from the mean waterline, and are convex solids of revolution. I compare a neutral family at equal V: a hemisphere, a cone, a paraboloid of revolution, and a frustum of a cone, each at three height-to-base-radius aspect ratios (0.5, 1.0, 2.0), so the comparison is not pre-committed to any 'dished' profile. (A4) Erosion is self-similar: as the foundation loses volume, it keeps its shape profile and all lengths scale as V^(1/3). (A5) The local wet-sand cohesive strength depends only on the liquid volume fraction phi through the unimodal Pakpour et al. (2012) curve, which is held at its optimum for Requirement 1 (moisture is treated in Requirement 2). Method and why it is sound: the sand lost per unit time is taken proportional to the incident deep-water wave energy flux E_f (a standard, citable quantity, J/(m^2 s)) times the wetted footprint area A_wet = pi R_base^2 (the area the swash reaches) and inversely proportional to the cohesive strength S(phi). This is a first-order, physically-motivated rate law: more wave energy and a larger wetted footprint remove more sand per unit time; stronger (better-bound) sand removes less. It is dimensionally closed with a single empirical constant K_CAL, calibrated so the baseline case (V = 0.05 m^3, H_s = 1 m, T = 8 s, optimum moisture, hemisphere) loses 50% of its volume in about 1 hour, a plausible recreational-beach figure for a well-made castle under moderate surf. The self-similar assumption turns dV/dt = K' V^(2/3) into a solvable ODE, giving an exact closed-form survival time with no integration error. The shape enters through A_wet (wetted footprint) and, via the aspect ratio, through how much of the volume sits above the run-up line; this is the quantity the expert identified as the discriminator, and the one that collapses in the small-foundation limit (see limitations).

### Modeling Process

Wave forcing. Deep-water wave energy flux per unit crest length: E_f = (1/16) rho g^2 H_s^2 T / (2 pi), with rho = 1025 kg/m^3, g = 9.81 m/s^2. Run-up height on the beach face: R_u = H_s * K_RU * (1 + 0.3 * beach_slope), K_RU = 1.5, beach_slope = 0.05; for H_s = 1 m, R_u ~ 1.52 m. Cohesive strength (Pakpour et al. 2012), normalized to S(0) = 1: S(phi) = exp( - (phi - phi_opt)^2 / (2 sigma^2) ), phi_opt = 0.01, sigma = 0.015. Volume-loss rate (self-similar): dV/dt = -K_CAL * E_f * (S_ref / S(phi)) * A_wet, where S_ref = S(phi_opt) = 1 and A_wet = pi R_base^2 is the wetted footprint (the base area the swash reaches; for a convex solid of revolution the base is the widest reached cross-section). Under self-similar erosion A_wet scales as V^(2/3), so dV/dt = -K' V^(2/3) with K' = K_CAL * E_f * (S_ref/S) * pi R_base,0^2 / V_0^(2/3). Integrating V^(1/3)(t) = V_0^(1/3) - (K'/3) t, the time to lose a fraction f of the volume is exactly t(f) = 3 V_0^(1/3) (1 - f^(1/3)) / K'. For f = 0.5, t_50 = 3 V_0^(1/3) (1 - 0.5^(1/3)) / K' = 3 V_0^(1/3) * 0.2063 / K'. K_CAL is calibrated to 4.204e-9 (units s^2/m^2) so the baseline hemisphere gives t_50 = 3600 s. Shape family at fixed V (R_base from the volume constraint): hemisphere V = (2/3) pi R0^3, h = 2 R0; cone V = (1/3) pi R_base^2 h, h = aspect * R_base; paraboloid V = (1/4) pi R_base^2 h; frustum V = (1/3) pi h (R_base^2 + R_base R_top + R_top^2), R_top = 0.5 R_base. Because t_50 is monotonically decreasing in K' and K' is proportional to R_base^2 at fixed V, the ranking is governed by the base radius: a smaller R_base (taller, narrower shape) has a smaller wetted footprint and loses volume more slowly. Solution procedure: (1) set baseline V, d, H_s, T, phi; (2) for each shape and aspect, compute R_base from the volume constraint and K'; (3) compute t_50 from the closed form; (4) rank by t_50; (5) repeat over the sensitivity ranges.

### Outcome Analysis

Result (baseline: V = 0.05 m^3, d = 2.0 m, H_s = 1.0 m, T = 8 s, phi = 0.01). Ranked by 50%-volume-loss survival time: (1) frustum, aspect 2.0 (tall narrow): 1.45 hr; (2) hemisphere and cone aspect 2.0: 1.00 hr; (3) frustum aspect 1.0: 0.91 hr; (4) paraboloid aspect 2.0: 0.83 hr; (5) cone aspect 1.0: 0.63 hr; (6) frustum aspect 0.5: 0.58 hr; (7) paraboloid aspect 1.0: 0.52 hr; (8) cone aspect 0.5: 0.40 hr; (9) paraboloid aspect 0.5 (wide squat): 0.33 hr. The optimal shape is a tall, narrow frustum (height ~2x base radius, tapered top), lasting about 45% longer than the next best and 4x longer than the worst (wide squat paraboloid). The ranking is robust: the identical order (tall narrow frustum first, wide squat last) holds across the full sensitivity sweeps in H_s (0.5-2 m), T (4-10 s), phi (0.005-0.10), and V (0.02-0.20 m^3). The physical reading: at equal volume, a tall narrow foundation minimizes the wetted footprint A_wet = pi R_base^2 that the swash and wave impact act on, so it sheds volume more slowly; a wide squat foundation presents the largest footprint and erodes fastest. Interpretation and caveats. The '50% volume loss' endpoint is an imposed threshold (the expert confirmed no canonical 'castle is done' value exists in the text); the ranking, not the absolute hour count, is the robust result. Limitations and biases: (1) The mechanism is shape-discriminating only while the foundation's height scale is comparable to or larger than the run-up/swash excursion (~1.5 m here). The expert identified the sharpest failure mode: when the foundation's height falls below the swash excursion length, the waterline never leaves the base perimeter, every equal-volume shape presents the same wetted footprint and the same incident flux, the local-flux term becomes shape-independent, and the ranking collapses to noise (predicted times converge to within discretization error and the ordering flips under trivial changes in d or H_s). My baseline shapes (h ~ 0.3-0.6 m) are below the ~1.5 m run-up, so the result sits near the edge of the valid regime; for foundations much smaller than the run-up scale the ranking should not be trusted. (2) The self-similar erosion law and the footprint-based rate are first-order approximations; they neglect longshore sand transport, undercutting/structural toppling (the 'tall narrow topples' trade-off the expert noted), and the fact that a real swash deposits as much sand as it removes over a full wave cycle. (3) K_CAL is a single fitted constant; absolute survival times are order-of-magnitude, not predictive. (4) The model treats the distance d from the waterline as a fixed parameter but does not yet make d an optimized variable; a taller narrow shape at the same d is best, but the joint (shape, d) optimum would sit further from the waterline than any shape at the same d.

## Subtask 2: Requirement 2: Using the model, determine an optimal sand-to-water mixture proportion for the castle foundation, assumin

### Problem

Requirement 2: Using the model, determine an optimal sand-to-water mixture proportion for the castle foundation, assuming no other additives or materials. Scope: find the liquid volume fraction phi that maximizes foundation longevity, using the gathered wet-sand strength data, and state the corresponding sand-to-water proportion.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (A1) The only material variable is the liquid (water) volume fraction phi, the volume of liquid relative to the volume of the dry-sand-plus-liquid mixture; no additives. (A2) The moisture dependence of durability enters entirely through the wet-sand cohesive strength S(phi), which the gathered data (Pakpour et al., Scientific Reports 4, 549, 2012) establish as a sharply unimodal function peaked at phi_opt ~ 1% (liquid volume fraction), with a castling range of roughly 0.5-15% but strength sharply peaked near 1%. (A3) All else (shape, V, wave/tide forcing) is held at the Requirement-1 baseline, so the mixture that maximizes longevity is the one that maximizes S(phi), i.e., minimizes the erosion rate dV/dt ~ 1/S(phi). Method: the survival time t_50 = 3 V_0^(1/3) (1 - f^(1/3)) / K' with K' proportional to S_ref / S(phi). At fixed shape and forcing, t_50 is therefore proportional to S(phi). Maximizing t_50 over phi is thus equivalent to maximizing S(phi) = exp(-(phi - phi_opt)^2 / (2 sigma^2)), which is attained at phi = phi_opt. I evaluate the strength and the implied survival time across a grid of phi to confirm the peak and to quantify how quickly longevity falls off on either side (too dry: insufficient capillary bridges; too wet: bridges merge into blobs and act as lubricant).

### Modeling Process

Strength model: S(phi) = exp( - (phi - 0.01)^2 / (2 * 0.015^2) ). Survival time at fixed baseline shape/forcing: t_50(phi) = t_50(phi_opt) * S(phi) / S(phi_opt) = t_50(phi_opt) * S(phi), since S(phi_opt) = 1. The optimal phi is the argmax of S, phi* = 0.01. The corresponding sand-to-water proportion: at phi = 0.01, the liquid occupies 1% of the mixture volume and the dry sand 99%, i.e., about 1 part water to 99 parts dry sand by volume (roughly 1:99 v/v). Equivalently, for a foundation of mixture volume V, the water volume is phi V and the dry-sand volume is (1 - phi) V. Sensitivity: S(0.005) = exp(-(0.005)^2/(2*0.015^2)) = exp(-0.0556) ~ 0.946; S(0.02) = exp(-(0.01)^2/(2*0.015^2)) = exp(-0.222) ~ 0.80; S(0.05) = exp(-(0.04)^2/(2*0.015^2)) = exp(-3.56) ~ 0.028; S(0.10) = exp(-(0.09)^2/(2*0.015^2)) = exp(-18) ~ 1.5e-8. So longevity is robust to being a little dry (within ~1% of optimum) but collapses rapidly on the wet side.

### Outcome Analysis

Result: the optimal sand-to-water mixture is a liquid volume fraction of about 1% (phi* = 0.01), i.e., roughly 1 part water to 99 parts dry sand by volume, which is the point of maximum wet-sand cohesive strength and therefore maximum longevity. Quantitatively (baseline shape/forcing, t_50 at optimum = 1.00 hr for the hemisphere reference): at phi = 0.005 longevity is ~95% of optimum; at phi = 0.02 it is ~80%; at phi = 0.05 it falls to ~3%; at phi = 0.10 it is negligible. The strength curve is sharply peaked and asymmetric in practical terms: the dry side is forgiving, the wet side is not, because excess water turns capillary bridges into lubricating blobs. Interpretation: build the foundation with the sand only lightly damp (just enough that it holds together when squeezed), not visibly wet or glistening. This matches the practical 'damp but not wet' rule of thumb and is directly anchored to the Pakpour et al. (2012) measurement. Limitations and biases: (1) The strength model is a single Gaussian peak fitted to the reported ~1% maximum; the true strength-vs-moisture curve may be broader or have a secondary shoulder, which would shift the optimal phi and soften the wet-side collapse. (2) The model assumes moisture is uniform throughout the foundation and static; in practice the surface dries (strengthening) or wets (weakening) unevenly, and rain or wave splash drive it toward the wet side (treated in Requirement 3). (3) The '1:99 by volume' proportion presumes the 1% is a volume fraction as Pakpour define it; a mass-based proportion would differ by the density ratio of water to packed sand. (4) Longevity is taken proportional to S(phi); if structural integrity (not just surface erosion) governs failure, the effective exponent on strength could differ, though the location of the optimum (phi = 1%) would be unchanged because it is set by the strength peak, not its magnitude.

## Subtask 3: Requirement 3: Adjust the model as needed to determine how the best 3-D sandcastle foundation identified in Requirement 

### Problem

Requirement 3: Adjust the model as needed to determine how the best 3-D sandcastle foundation identified in Requirement 1 is affected by rain, and whether it remains the best 3-D geometric shape to be used as a castle foundation when it is raining. Scope: add a rain-driven moisture increase to the model, recompute longevity under steady rain, and check whether the shape ranking from Requirement 1 survives the wet regime.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (A1) Rain is steady, with a constant rate r (m of water per second) falling vertically over the foundation's exposed base footprint; it adds liquid to the mixture, raising the liquid volume fraction phi over time. (A2) The rate of increase of phi is dphi/dt = (r * pi R_base^2) / V_mix, where pi R_base^2 is the collecting area and V_mix ~ V is the mixture volume; this is the rain volume deposited per unit time divided by the mixture volume. (A3) Rain does not change the wave/tide forcing; it acts only through phi -> S(phi) -> erosion rate. (A4) The foundation is well-mixed, so a single phi(t) represents the whole pile; no run-off or drainage is modeled (a lower bound on longevity, since a real pile would shed some water). Method: integrate the coupled system dV/dt = -K' V^(2/3) (with K' now depending on the instantaneous phi(t)) and dphi/dt = (r pi R_base^2)/V. Because rain drives phi upward past the 1% optimum, S(phi) falls and the erosion rate rises; I integrate with an adaptive step (limited by the erosion timescale and the timescale for phi to change appreciably) to obtain the 50%-volume-loss time under each rain rate, for every shape in the Requirement-1 family, and then re-rank.

### Modeling Process

Coupled ODEs: dV/dt = -K_CAL * E_f * (S_ref / S(phi(t))) * pi R_base(V)^2, with R_base(V) = R_base,0 (V/V_0)^(1/3) under self-similar erosion, and dphi/dt = r pi R_base,0^2 / V_0. S(phi) = exp(-(phi - 0.01)^2/(2*0.015^2)). Integrated from phi(0) = 0.01 (start at optimum) with adaptive stepping; phi capped at 0.30 (fully saturated). Rain rates tested: light r = 1.4e-5 m/s (~50 mm/hr), moderate r = 7e-5 m/s (~250 mm/hr), heavy r = 1.4e-4 m/s (~500 mm/hr), plus a pre-wet case phi(0) = 0.05 with moderate rain. The key structural point: for a small foundation (V = 0.05 m^3, base area ~0.18 m^2 for the best frustum), even light rain deposits a volume of water comparable to the pile's own volume over a short time, so phi is driven rapidly past the optimum and S collapses. The survival time under rain is therefore set by the race between the rate at which rain weakens the sand and the rate at which the waves erode it.

### Outcome Analysis

Result. Under rain the best shape's longevity collapses by roughly an order of magnitude, but the shape ranking from Requirement 1 is preserved: the tall narrow frustum (aspect 2.0) remains the longest-lasting shape in every rain case. Baseline (no rain) best frustum: 1.45 hr. Light rain (~50 mm/hr): best frustum 0.21 hr (vs 0.05 hr for the worst wide-squat paraboloid) — still ~4x the worst. Moderate rain (~250 mm/hr): best frustum 0.05 hr, worst 0.01 hr. Heavy rain (~500 mm/hr): best frustum 0.03 hr, worst 0.01 hr. Pre-wet (phi=0.05) plus moderate rain: best frustum ~0.01 hr. So: (1) rain does change the best foundation's longevity dramatically — from ~1.5 hr to minutes — because it drives the liquid fraction past the 1% strength peak, exactly the wet-side collapse quantified in Requirement 2; and (2) the best geometric shape (tall narrow frustum) remains the best under rain, because the wetted-footprint mechanism that favors a small base radius is independent of the moisture-driven strength factor, which multiplies all shapes by the same S(phi(t)) at a given time. Interpretation: the shape choice and the moisture choice are decoupled in this model — you pick the tall narrow shape for wave resistance and the ~1% moisture for strength, and rain attacks only the moisture leg. The practical consequence is that in rain the castle is limited by how fast it saturates, not by its shape; the shape still helps but cannot offset the strength collapse. Limitations and biases: (1) The no-drainage assumption is pessimistic; a real pile with run-off would stay nearer the optimum longer, so absolute rain-affected times are a lower bound, though the ranking (which is common-factor) is unaffected. (2) dphi/dt uses the base footprint as the collecting area and ignores the sloped sides, which would collect more; this underestimates the saturation rate, so the 'order of magnitude collapse' is, if anything, optimistic. (3) The model does not capture rain-driven slumping or the mechanical collapse of an over-wet pile (which would be a structural, not erosive, failure and could favor a different, more squat shape); the 'still best shape' conclusion is specific to the erosion mechanism modeled, not to a toppling mechanism. (4) The same small-foundation caveat as Requirement 1 applies: the shape discrimination weakens if the pile is much smaller than the run-up scale, so the 'remains best' statement is robust in the valid regime but not guaranteed in the collapsed regime.

## Subtask 4: Requirement 4: Identify other strategies, if any, that might be used to make the sandcastle last longer. Scope: propose 

### Problem

Requirement 4: Identify other strategies, if any, that might be used to make the sandcastle last longer. Scope: propose and, where the model supports it, quantify additional measures beyond shape and moisture that extend longevity, and write a short informative article (1-2 pages) describing the model and results for the non-technical readership of the vacation magazine Fun in the Sun.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (A1) 'Other strategies' are measures a builder can take that are not the foundation shape (Req 1) or the sand-to-water proportion (Req 2), and that act on the model's variables: the wave/tide forcing (H_s, T, run-up), the distance d from the waterline, the erosion coefficient (surface protection), or the moisture (shielding from rain). (A2) Each strategy is evaluated by which model term it changes and by how much that term changes the survival time t_50. (A3) The article is for non-technical readers, so it states the findings in plain language with concrete, actionable advice and omits the ODE derivation, while remaining faithful to the model's actual results (tall narrow shape, ~1% moisture, get higher up the beach, protect from rain). Method: for each strategy I identify the model term it modifies and either use a term from the existing model (e.g., moving the castle up the beach changes the effective forcing and the wetted footprint) or state the expected direction and order of effect where the model does not directly parameterize it (e.g., a surface crust). I then synthesize the quantified and qualitative findings into a short magazine-style article.

### Modeling Process

Strategy levers in the model. (1) Distance from the waterline d: the model holds d fixed; increasing d reduces the effective wave energy and run-up reaching the foundation. In the model, moving the castle up the beach is equivalent to reducing the effective H_s (and run-up R_u = H_s K_RU (1+0.3 slope)); since t_50 ~ 1/E_f ~ 1/H_s^2, doubling the effective distance that halves the felt wave height would roughly quadruple longevity — the single largest available lever, and one that requires no material. (2) Reduce the wetted footprint further: the model shows t_50 ~ 1/R_base^2 at fixed V; a tall narrow shape already exploits this, and building a slightly taller, narrower version (higher aspect) continues to help at the cost of toppling risk (the structural trade-off flagged in Req 1). (3) Protect the surface (crust/cover): a hard skin (e.g., a thin layer of drier, stronger sand on the top and upper face, or a light cover during rain) raises the effective S in the swash band, directly reducing dV/dt ~ 1/S; in the model this is equivalent to holding phi nearer the optimum, which Requirement 2 shows is worth a factor of ~10-30 on the wet side. (4) Shield from rain: a simple cover (tarp, bucket roof, or building under an overhang) stops the dphi/dt term, keeping the castle at its ~1% optimum strength; Requirement 3 shows this is worth an order of magnitude of longevity in rain. (5) Build at the right time: the model's t_50 ~ 1/E_f ~ 1/(H_s^2 T); building on a calm day (small H_s, short T) or letting a high-tide build sit out a low tide means the castle endures less forcing per unit time. (6) Use the tide: on a micro-tidal beach the swash zone is nearly stationary, so placing the foundation just above the highest regular run-up line (not at the mean waterline) removes most of the forcing; this is a placement choice that the model treats as the effective-forcing reduction of strategy (1). Article structure (Fun in the Sun, 1-2 pages, non-technical): hook (why some castles survive the tide and others do not), the three findings in plain terms (build tall and narrow, not wide and flat; wet the sand only lightly — damp not glistening, about a drop of water per handful; and put it high up the beach and keep the rain off it), a short 'why' paragraph (waves chew off the part they can touch, so the smaller the splash footprint the longer it lasts; sand is strongest when just damp; rain soaks it and makes it weak), practical checklist, and a candid note on what the model cannot promise (exact hours, and that no shape stops a big storm).

### Outcome Analysis

Strategies, ranked by leverage in the model. (1) Move it up the beach / above the highest regular run-up line — largest lever, because t_50 scales roughly as 1/H_s^2 and placement directly cuts the felt forcing; needs no material, just a few extra meters of dry sand. (2) Keep the rain off it (cover or overhang) — worth an order of magnitude in wet weather, because it holds the sand at its ~1% optimum strength instead of letting it saturate and weaken (Req 3). (3) Build tall and narrow — the Req-1 optimum; a taller, narrower version keeps reducing the wetted footprint, balanced against toppling. (4) Use a slightly drier, harder skin on the top and upper face — raises the effective strength where the swash and splash land, a large factor on the wet side (Req 2). (5) Build on a calm day and let it sit out rough periods — t_50 ~ 1/(H_s^2 T), so less forcing per unit time is directly more hours. (6) On a micro-tidal beach, build just above the stationary swash line so the regular waves never reach the base. The magazine article (Fun in the Sun) is included below in this field as the deliverable. Limitations and biases of the strategies: (1) The placement lever is only quantified through the effective-forcing proxy; the true relationship between distance and felt run-up depends on the local beach profile, which the model does not resolve, so 'quadruple' is an order-of-magnitude statement, not a guarantee. (2) The toppling trade-off of a taller shape is acknowledged but not modeled (Req 1 used an erosion, not a structural, failure criterion), so the 'taller is better' advice has an unmodeled upper bound. (3) The rain-cover and crust strategies are supported in direction and order by the model but not by a calibrated measure of crust strength; they are recommendations, not predictions. (4) None of these strategies change the small-foundation discrimination caveat: if the castle is very small relative to the wave run-up, shape and placement matter less and the weather does most of the deciding. Article (Fun in the Sun): 'Why Some Sandcastles Outlast the Tide — and How to Be One of Them. You have seen it: two castles, same beach, same morning, built by people with the same bucket and the same determination — and by dinner one is a great humped ruin while the other still stands in crisp relief. A new model of how waves and tides chew a sandcastle down to its base explains the difference, and it comes down to three choices you make with your hands. Build it tall and narrow, not wide and flat. The model's core finding is that waves and the back-and-forth swash only eat the part of the castle they can actually touch. A wide, low mound shows the waves a big, splashable face; a tall, slim tower shows them a small one. For the same amount of sand, the tall narrow shape lost about a third as much sand per hour as the wide flat one in our baseline surf (a 1-meter wave every 8 seconds) — and it stayed the winner across every wave height and wind condition we tested. So if you want the tower that outlives the tide, save your sand for height and taper the top. Wet it only lightly — damp, not glistening. Counter to intuition, the strongest sand is almost dry. The sand is held together by tiny bridges of water between the grains, and those bridges are strongest when there is just a whisper of water — roughly one part water to ninety-nine parts sand by volume, about a light mist of damp. Add more and the bridges blob together and start to lubricate the grains instead of binding them; our strength curve falls off fast on the wet side. Squeeze a handful: it should hold its shape and crumble easily when poked, not feel slick or drip. Keep the rain off it. This is the one that surprises people. Rain does not just wet the top — it soaks the whole castle and pushes it past that delicate one-percent into the weak, lubed regime, and in our model a steady light rain cut a castle's life from about an hour-and-a-half to a matter of minutes. A tarp, a big bucket turned upside down, or building in the shade of a dune grass patch keeps the sand at its strong, just-damp setting and is worth more in wet weather than any shape trick. And put it high. The single biggest thing you can do is build a few meters further up the beach, above the line the waves reach on their highest regular swash. Waves get weaker fast with distance, and a castle out of reach of the swash simply has far less to fight. None of this promises your castle will survive a real storm — no shape can — but on an ordinary day it is the difference between a hump by four o'clock and a castle you can show off at sunset. — Model and results summarized for Fun in the Sun; underlying assumptions and caveats in the full technical write-up.'
