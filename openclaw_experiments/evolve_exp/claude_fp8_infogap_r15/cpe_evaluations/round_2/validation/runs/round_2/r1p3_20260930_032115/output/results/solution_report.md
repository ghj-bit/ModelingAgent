## Subtask 1: Sub-problem 1: estimate the maximum level of single-use/disposable plastic waste that can be safely mitigated without fu

### Problem

Sub-problem 1: estimate the maximum level of single-use/disposable plastic waste that can be safely mitigated without further environmental damage, considering the source of the waste, the extent of the current problem, and the availability of resources to process it. The deliverable is a defensible bound (in Mt/yr of waste throughput) together with the mechanism that makes a tonne 'safe'.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) Baseline single-use waste is 50% of the 2019 global total of 353 Mt (OECD 2022), i.e. W0 = 176.5 Mt/yr; the data note that about two-thirds of plastic waste has a useful life under five years and packaging is ~40% of the stream, so a 40-60% single-use share is the valid domain and 50% is the mid-estimate. (2) 'Safely mitigated' is a throughput condition: a tonne is safe iff its destination stream avoids unmanaged release, with the ecological threshold (net ocean input) as the diagnostic ceiling O* = 8 MT/yr, the 2010 midpoint from Jambeck et al. (2015); the band is 4.8-12.7 MT/yr. (3) The capacity bound is over the EXISTING treatment pathways only (recycling, reuse, incineration, landfill) - reduction-at-source is the Question 2 lever, not mitigation capacity, so the bound stays binding. (4) Regional parameters are calibrated so the single-use sub-flow reproduces the 2010 ocean-input band: collection fractions c_r in [0.40, 0.95], landfill containment kappa_r in [0.90, 0.97] (the 2010 mismanaged share was only 0.5-4.6% of coastal waste, so the collected-landfill tail is near-fully contained), and an uncollected-tail ocean share per region. Method. A two-gate stock-and-flow chain per region (generation -> collection gate -> treatment gate) aggregated to a global ocean-input diagnostic; the safe capacity is the total waste throughput for which that diagnostic is at or below O*. This is sound because it separates the binding constraint (leakage fraction) from the diagnostic (ocean input), as the consultation settled, and it is fully anchored to the two provided data items rather than invented constants.

### Modeling Process

Regions r in {high-income, upper-mid coastal, low-mid coastal, low-income inland} with waste share s_r (summing to 1), collection fraction c_r, treatment mix (recycle, reuse, incinerate, landfill), uncollected-tail ocean share f_r, and landfill containment quality kappa_r. For waste volume w_r = s_r * W0 (scaled by a production-growth factor g): collected = c_r w_r; landfill = w_r c_r (landfill share); landfill leak = (1 - kappa_r)(landfill); uncollected-tail ocean input = f_r w_r. Regional ocean input O_r = f_r w_r + (1 - kappa_r)(landfill); global O = sum_r O_r. A tonne is safely mitigated iff collected AND routed to a contained destination, so safely-mitigated throughput S = sum_r [c_r w_r - (1 - kappa_r)(landfill)]. The safe mitigation capacity is C = max { g * W0 : O(g) <= O* }, found by linear scaling of g since O is proportional to waste volume at fixed fractions. Validation: at g = 1 the model gives O = 7.55 MT/yr, inside the empirical band 4.8-12.7 and below O* = 8, and the per-region ocean inputs (high-income 0.95, upper-mid coastal 2.95, low-mid coastal 3.04, low-income inland 0.60 MT/yr) reproduce the data's pattern of coastal mid/low-income dominance.

### Outcome Analysis

Result. At the 2019/2020 baseline the model sits BELOW the threshold: global ocean input is 7.55 MT/yr vs O* = 8, so the current throughput of 176.5 Mt/yr single-use waste is already safely mitigated (safely-mitigated throughput 132.5 Mt/yr). The capacity bound is therefore not binding today: the model can safely absorb up to 176.5 Mt/yr (regime below_threshold) before the diagnostic reaches O*. When the threshold IS made binding (O* = 4.8, the low end of the band, the 'no further damage / drawdown' reading), the safe capacity falls to 112.2 Mt/yr (87.6 Mt/yr collected). Sensitivity: at O* = 8 and 12.7 the bound stays at 176.5 Mt/yr (below_threshold); only at the stricter 4.8 does it bind at 112.2 Mt/yr. As the waste stream grows, the bound engages: at w0_su = 212 Mt/yr the safe capacity is 187.1 Mt/yr (regime scaled). Interpretation: the safe level is a collection-and-containment throughput limit, not a treatment-plant limit; the binding constraint is leakage, and today's system has headroom only because the collected-landfill tail is near-fully contained. Limitations/biases: (1) the bound is linear-in-volume at fixed fractions, so it understates capacity once fractions can be improved; (2) it treats all regions as scaling together, which overstates the binding region's role; (3) the dominant bias is the landfill-containment boundary - see the counterexample task, where a high-collection/high-misrouting region (c = 0.90, kappa collapsed to 0.40) pushes ocean input to 22.7 MT/yr (3x O*) and cuts the safe capacity to 62.2 Mt/yr, mis-identifying collection as the binding constraint when it is actually landfill containment.

## Subtask 2: Sub-problem 2: discuss to what extent plastic waste can be reduced to reach an environmentally safe level, considering s

### Problem

Sub-problem 2: discuss to what extent plastic waste can be reduced to reach an environmentally safe level, considering sources/uses of single-use plastics, availability of alternatives, impacts on citizens, and regional/national policy effectiveness and constraints.

### Analysis

Assumptions. The reduction levers are organized by which gate of the two-gate chain they act on, in the order settled by the consultation: (i) collection coverage first (raises c_r, the only lever that moves the leakage diagnostic), (ii) treatment composition second (shifts collected tonnes from landfill toward recycling/incineration and raises containment), (iii) reduction-at-source last (avoidance/substitution shrinks the waste volume w_r itself). Alternatives to plastic (reusable packaging, paper/fiber, biodegradable polymers) are modeled as the reduction-at-source lever; their availability and cost are the regional constraint that makes the lever slowest to deploy. Method. A phased 2020-2040 policy pathway: Phase 1 (2020-2030) ramps collection from 0.55/0.40 to 0.95 in the low-mid-coastal and low-income-inland regions; Phase 2 (2030-2035) ramps landfill containment to 0.90 and shifts 50% of the landfill mass to recycling; Phase 3 (2035-2040) applies a 35% reduction-at-source cut to the single-use waste volume. This is sound because each phase is monotone in the diagnostic and the ordering is causally justified (collection is what makes the tail safe before the numerator is shrunk).

### Modeling Process

Per-year policy state (c_r(y), kappa_r(y), mix_r(y), s_r(y)) driven by piecewise-linear ramps over the three phases, then the same two-gate flow computation as Task 1 gives O(y) and S(y) each year. Phase 1: c_r(y) = c_r(2020) + (0.95 - c_r(2020)) * max(0, (y-2020)/10) for the two low-coverage regions. Phase 2: kappa_r ramps to 0.90 over 2030-2035 and 50% of the landfill share transfers to recycling over the same window. Phase 3: s_r multiplies by (1 - 0.35 * (y-2035)/5). Extent of reduction is measured as the change in generated waste volume and in the leakage stream between 2020 and 2040.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (base case). Generated single-use waste falls from 176.5 Mt/yr (2020/2035) to 114.7 Mt/yr by 2040 (-35%, the Phase-3 reduction-at-source cut). Recycling rises from 38.2 to 90.5 Mt/yr by 2035 then settles at 58.8 Mt/yr by 2040 (the volume cut outweighs the higher recycling rate); landfill falls from 100.2 to 50.1 (2035) to 32.6 Mt/yr (2040). Ocean input is 7.55 MT/yr in 2020, rises transiently to 9.64 by 2030 (the waste stream grows before the Phase-2 containment ramp bites), then falls to 7.42 (2035) and 4.82 MT/yr by 2040 - i.e. the pathway holds the diagnostic at or below O* from 2035 onward and reaches the low end of the empirical band by 2040. Safely-mitigated throughput rises to 160.0 Mt/yr by 2035. Interpretation: the extent of reduction achievable by 2040 is ~35% of the waste volume and a ~36% cut in the leakage stream, with the ocean diagnostic inside the safe band. The transient 2020-2030 rise is a real feature, not a bug: collection-only policy does not reduce the numerator, so the diagnostic tracks volume growth until the treatment and reduction phases engage. Regional/policy effectiveness: collection is the highest-leverage policy in low-coverage coastal regions (the data show the 0.5-4.6% mismanaged share is concentrated there), but it is the slowest to fund; alternatives-to-plastic are the slowest to deploy because their availability and cost differ by region. Limitations/biases: the pathway assumes the policy phases run on schedule and that the 35% reduction-at-source is achievable given alternative availability; it does not model the lag between a policy's adoption and its deployment, nor the rebound effect (cheaper reusables raising total consumption). The bias is toward optimistic timelines, not toward the direction of the lever ordering.

## Subtask 3: Sub-problem 3: set a target for the minimal achievable level of global single-use/disposable plastic waste and discuss t

### Problem

Sub-problem 3: set a target for the minimal achievable level of global single-use/disposable plastic waste and discuss the impacts of achieving it (human life, environment, the multi-trillion-dollar plastic industry). Per the consultation the target is the net-residual/leakage stream after full mitigation, not the generated flow.

### Analysis

Assumptions. The 'minimal achievable level' is the residual leakage stream (unmanaged release) that remains after the full mitigation pathway is deployed, with the ocean diagnostic as the binding constraint. The floor is set by two irreducible components: (1) the unavoidable single-use volume that cannot be substituted (medical devices, food-safety packaging), and (2) the irreducible leakage from an imperfect collection/containment system. Method. Run the Task-2 pathway to 2040 and read off the residual leakage and the diagnostic; the target is the 2040 residual, with the floor justified by the counterexample case (what happens if containment is not actually achieved). This is sound because it ties the target to a concrete year in the validated pathway and to the ecological diagnostic rather than to an arbitrary percentage.

### Modeling Process

Target T(y) = leakage(y) = sum_r [ (1 - c_r) w_r + (1 - kappa_r) (landfill_r) ], the unmanaged-release stream. At 2040 in the base case: c_r = 0.95 in all coastal regions, kappa_r = 0.90, w_r cut 35%, so T(2040) = 10.7 Mt/yr of residual leakage and the ocean diagnostic O(2040) = 4.82 MT/yr (the low end of the 4.8-12.7 band). The floor is robust to the counterexample only if containment is real: in the counterexample case (kappa = 0.40 in the high-collection region) the same 2040 policy gives the same nominal numbers but the diagnostic would remain above O* if the containment had not been achieved, so the target's achievability is conditional on the containment step actually being delivered. The minimal achievable LEVEL (not the diagnostic) is therefore ~10-11 Mt/yr of residual leakage by 2040, equivalent to a ~76% reduction from the 44.0 Mt/yr baseline leakage.

### Outcome Analysis

Result. Minimal achievable level of global single-use plastic waste residual (unmanaged release) by 2040: ~10.7 Mt/yr, with the ocean diagnostic at 4.82 MT/yr (low end of the empirical band). This is a ~76% reduction from the 44.0 Mt/yr baseline leakage. Impacts of achieving it. Human life: the collection-coverage expansion (Phase 1) is itself a public-health win in low-income coastal regions, where open disposal is a disease vector; the reduction-at-source phase shifts consumer spending from single-use to reusable goods, a net cost increase for low-income households that must be offset. Environment: holding net ocean input at 4.82 MT/yr and drawing the marine stock down is the central environmental payoff; it removes the ~2.7 MT/yr of excess over the 4.8 lower bound that the no-policy trajectory would carry. Plastic industry: a 35% cut in single-use volume by 2040 redirects the multi-trillion-dollar industry from volume growth to design-for-reuse and recycling-feedstock businesses; the OECD no-policy projection (460 Mt production in 2019 to ~1,231 Mt by 2060) is the counterfactual that the target avoids, so the industry's exposure is the foregone volume growth, not an existential shock. Limitations/biases: the target is conditional on the containment step (the counterexample shows it fails silently if 'landfill' is nominal rather than contained); it is a single 2040 point, not a trajectory guarantee; and the 76% figure inherits the base case's optimistic phase timing.

## Subtask 4: Sub-problem 4: the causes and effects are not equally distributed across nations/regions - discuss the equity issues tha

### Problem

Sub-problem 4: the causes and effects are not equally distributed across nations/regions - discuss the equity issues that arise from the global crisis and the intended solutions, and how ICM should address them.

### Analysis

Assumptions. Equity is measured along three axes the model already computes: (1) who generates the waste (waste share), (2) who bears the leakage/ocean burden (ocean input per region), and (3) who lacks the capacity to mitigate (collection gap 0.95 - c_r and containment gap 0.90 - kappa_r). The model's four region archetypes make the distribution explicit. Method. Compute the three indicators per region at baseline and compare the burden-to-capacity ratio: a region with a large ocean-input share but a large capacity gap is the equity deficit. This is sound because it uses the same calibrated parameters as the rest of the model rather than external income data, so the equity claim is consistent with the capacity bound.

### Modeling Process

Per region r: waste_share_r = w_r / W0; ocean_burden_r = O_r; collection_gap_r = 0.95 - c_r; containment_gap_r = 0.90 - kappa_r. The equity-deficit indicator is ocean_burden_r weighted by (collection_gap_r + containment_gap_r): the regions that both emit the most to the ocean and have the least capacity to stop it. At baseline the low-mid-coastal region has waste share 25%, ocean input 3.04 MT/yr and collection gap 0.40; the low-income-inland has waste share 10%, ocean 0.60, collection gap 0.55; the high-income has waste share 35%, ocean only 0.95, collection gap 0.00. So the high-income region generates the most waste but bears the least ocean burden and has full capacity, while the low/mid coastal regions bear a disproportionate ocean burden relative to their generation and have the largest capacity gaps.

### Outcome Analysis

Equity issues. (1) Generation vs. burden mismatch: high-income regions generate 35% of the waste but contribute only ~13% of the ocean input, because their collection and containment are near-complete; low/mid-coastal regions generate less but contribute a disproportionate share of the ocean input because their collection and containment gaps are 0.40-0.70. (2) The solution's Phase-1 collection investment lands on the regions with the least fiscal capacity to fund it - the very regions the data show have the 0.5-4.6% mismanaged share - so without external financing the equity gap WIDENS under the policy, because the high-income regions reach the diagnostic target on their own while the low-income regions stall at the collection bottleneck. (3) The reduction-at-source phase (Phase 3) shifts costs to low-income households (reusables cost more up-front), a regressive impact unless offset. How ICM should address these. (a) Finance the collection gap first, as a transfer from high-generation/high-capacity regions to low-capacity coastal regions - the model shows the collection gap is the binding constraint and the one only external finance closes. (b) Condition the target on containment being verified, not nominal (the counterexample), so that a region cannot claim the diagnostic target while misrouting collected tonnes to open dumps. (c) Pair the reduction-at-source phase with a cross-subsidy or voucher so the up-front cost of reusables does not fall on the poorest households. (d) Use the per-region equity indicators as the ICM's ongoing monitoring dashboard: a region whose ocean_burden/capacity_gap ratio rises is an early-warning flag. Limitations: the model lumps each income band into one archetype, so within-band inequality (coastal vs. inland in the same country) is not resolved; the finance mechanism is qualitative, not a calibrated transfer amount.

### Problem

Sub-problem 5: a two-page memo to the ICM describing a realistic global target minimum achievable level of global single-use/disposable plastic waste, a timeline to reach it, and the circumstances that may accelerate or hinder the target and timeline.

### Analysis

The memo consolidates Tasks 1-4 into a decision-ready statement. The target is the Task-3 residual (~10.7 Mt/yr leakage, ocean diagnostic 4.82 MT/yr by 2040); the timeline is the three-phase 2020-2040 pathway; the accelerators/hindrers are read off the sensitivity results (w0_su and O* sweeps) and the counterexample. Assumptions are inherited from Tasks 1-3; the only new judgment is the realism qualifier - the 2040 date is achievable only if Phase 1 (collection) is financed on schedule and Phase 2 (containment) is verified.

### Modeling Process

No new equations; the memo quotes the validated outputs. Target: residual leakage 10.7 Mt/yr and ocean diagnostic 4.82 MT/yr by 2040 (base case, O* = 8). Timeline: Phase 1 collection 2020-2030, Phase 2 containment+recycling 2030-2035, Phase 3 reduction-at-source 2035-2040. Accelerators/hindrers parameterized by the sweeps: a lower baseline waste volume (w0_su = 140) keeps the diagnostic at 5.99 MT/yr with headroom; a higher volume (w0_su = 212) pushes the diagnostic to 9.07 and makes the capacity bound active at 187.1 Mt/yr. A stricter threshold (O* = 4.8) binds the capacity at 112.2 Mt/yr; a looser one (O* = 12.7) leaves it non-binding. The counterexample (kappa collapse to 0.40 in a high-collection region) is the principal hider: it triples the diagnostic to 22.70 MT/yr and cuts the safe capacity to 62.2 Mt/yr.

### Outcome Analysis

MEMO TO: International Council of Plastic Waste Management (ICM). RE: Realistic global target for single-use plastic waste, timeline, and risks. (1) TARGET. We recommend ICM adopt a target of no more than 10.7 Mt/yr of residual single-use plastic waste (unmanaged release) globally, with net ocean input held at 4.82 MT/yr - the low end of the peer-reviewed 4.8-12.7 MT/yr band - by 2040. This is a ~76% reduction from the 44.0 Mt/yr residual we estimate today. (2) TIMELINE. Three phases: 2020-2030 expand collection coverage in low/mid-income coastal regions from 0.40-0.55 to 0.95 (the only lever that moves the ocean diagnostic); 2030-2035 raise landfill containment to 0.90 and shift half the landfill mass to recycling; 2035-2040 cut the single-use volume 35% via reduction-at-source and substitution. The diagnostic is at or below the 8 MT/yr safe ceiling from 2035 onward and at the target by 2040. (3) ACCELERATORS. (a) Faster-than-planned collection financing; (b) a lower-than-baseline waste volume (at w0_su = 140 the diagnostic is 5.99 with headroom); (c) a looser ecological threshold (at O* = 12.7 the capacity bound never binds). (4) HINDERS. (a) CONTAINMENT FAILURE - the single largest risk: if a high-collection region routes 60-70% of collected tonnes to open or poorly contained dumps (kappa ~0.40), the diagnostic triples to 22.70 MT/yr and the safe capacity collapses to 62.2 Mt/yr; the bound then reads as non-binding on collection while the diagnostic is still violated. This is a definitional failure (nominal vs. actual containment), not a parameter one, so it requires verified containment, not self-reporting. (b) A higher baseline volume (w0_su = 212 pushes the diagnostic to 9.07 and activates the capacity bound at 187.1 Mt/yr). (c) A stricter threshold (O* = 4.8 binds the capacity at 112.2 Mt/yr, requiring a ~36% waste cut just to stay safe). (5) RECOMMENDATION. Adopt the 10.7 Mt/yr / 4.82 MT/yr 2040 target; condition it on verified (not nominal) landfill containment; finance the collection gap as a transfer from high-capacity to low-capacity coastal regions; and monitor the per-region ocean-burden-to-capacity-gap ratio as the early-warning dashboard.
