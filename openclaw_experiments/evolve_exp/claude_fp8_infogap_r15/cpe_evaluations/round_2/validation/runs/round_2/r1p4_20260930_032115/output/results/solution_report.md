## Subtask 1: Subtask 1 (Scope of the issue). Quantify how many people are at risk of becoming environmentally displaced persons (EDPs

### Problem

Subtask 1 (Scope of the issue). Quantify how many people are at risk of becoming environmentally displaced persons (EDPs) and how that number, and the associated risk of cultural loss, changes over time for the at-risk island nations named in the problem (Tuvalu, Kiribati, the Marshall Islands, and the Maldives), under the IPCC AR6 low/mid/high emissions scenarios plus a rapid-ice-sheet-loss tail. This is the ICM-F's core 'scope' question: not a single displacement date, but the population at risk distributed over an exposure axis through 2110.

### Analysis

The problem asks for the SCOPE of the issue, i.e. how many people are 'at risk of becoming EDPs' and how that changes over time. A human expert (Exchange 1) settled the structural reading: displacement is a graded, soft process along an exposure axis, not a hard land-availability minimum nor a complete-submergence trigger; 'nationless' is the endpoint of the axis, not the trigger. A binary trigger would collapse each nation to one date, could not express 'at risk,' and could not vary with the emissions scenarios. The exposure axis is driven by sea-level rise acting on each nation's elevation profile. We use the IPCC AR6 scenario curves as the sea-level inputs (0.38 m low / 0.56 m mid / 0.77 m high by 2100, plus a ~2 m tail) and the World Bank CCDR finding that atoll maxima are ~3 m and that a 0.5 m rise submerges 50-80% of major urban areas, as the elevation/exposure calibration. This is sound because it is the only structure that simultaneously (a) is scenario-valued, (b) expresses partial and cumulative displacement, and (c) is grounded in the two empirical anchors supplied (AR6 sea level and atoll elevation/urban-submergence).

### Modeling Process

Sea level S(t, scenario): piecewise-linear through the AR6 anchors 2018->2050 (0.18-0.23 m near-term, ~0.20-0.25 m) and 2050->2100 (0.38/0.56/0.77/~1.9 m for low/mid/high/tail), linearly extrapolated past 2100 at the late-century slope. Elevation profile land_below(h) is a convex shape in [0,1] with h_max = 3.0 m for the three atolls and 2.5 m for the Maldives. submergence_frac(S) = land_below(min(S,h_max)). Displacement is the graded, submergence-anchored crossing displaced_frac(S) = frac_low * sigmoid(k*(S-(h_low-d0))) + (1-frac_low)*sigmoid(k*(S-(h_max-d0))), where h_low=0.35*h_max is the height of the low-lying residential band, frac_low is the population share living there (0.6-0.8), d0=0.3 m is the early-degradation offset (salinization/freshwater loss precedes literal submergence), and k=1.5 m^-1 sets the softness of the crossing (the expert-confirmed soft boundary). Baseline populations: Tuvalu 11,000; Kiribati 120,000; Marshall Islands 42,000; Maldives 526,000 (World Bank/UN). For each nation and year t in 2018-2110 the model reports S(t), land_submerged_frac, displaced_frac, displaced_pop = displaced_frac * pop, and residual_pop. The scope is the displaced_pop series by nation, year, and scenario. The yearly increment (displaced_pop(t)-displaced_pop(t-1)) is the new inflow of EDPs that feeds the policy model.

### Outcome Analysis

Under the mid-emissions scenario (d0=0.3, k=1.5) the cumulative displaced population reaches 263,980 by 2100 and 279,282 by 2110 out of a combined 699,000. The scenarios bracket the issue: low 226,581 (2100); high 308,783 (2100); and the ice-sheet tail 515,844 (2100), 559,286 (2110) - meaning under the tail more than 80% of the four nations' population is displaced by 2110. The timing is scenario-graded exactly as the expert required: under 'low' no nation reaches 50% displacement within the horizon; under 'high' only the Maldives crosses 50% (at 2106); under 'tail' the Pacific atolls cross 50% in the 2080s and the Maldives in 2068, reaching near-total displacement by ~2110. The Maldives dominates the absolute scope (~53-55% of all displaced people) because of its size, while the Pacific atolls are the ones that approach complete (nationless) displacement. Cultural-loss risk tracks displacement: it is near-zero under 'low', partial under 'mid/high', and near-total under 'tail'. Limitations and biases: (i) the elevation profile is a single convex shape, not a surveyed hypsometric curve, so absolute submergence fractions carry a shape bias; (ii) the near-term (2050) rise is the same on the low and mid curves, so the low/mid separation is small before 2050; (iii) displacement is modeled as a deterministic function of sea level, so adaptation, relocation, and mortality that would reduce the at-risk count are not in this module (they enter in the policy model); (iv) the ~2 m tail is extrapolated linearly past 2100. These biases are directional, not magnitude errors in the scenario ordering, which is the robust feature used downstream.

### Problem

Subtask 2 (Proposed policies). Design an international policy that protects the human rights of persons whose nations have disappeared - the right to resettle and to participate fully in life in their new home - while simultaneously preserving cultural heritage. The policy must answer: where do these EDPs go, which countries take them, should higher-emission states bear higher obligation, and who gets a say (individuals, the UN, or host governments).

### Analysis

A human expert (Exchange 2) accepted a specific two-axis, phase-organized structure: (1) every at-risk population is scored on the single graded exposure axis from Subtask 1; (2) the response is three phases keyed to that axis. Phase 1 (low exposure) = protect-in-place: in-situ adaptation, coastal defence, freshwater protection, and climate-loss insurance - human rights protected by the status quo, culture preserved because people have not moved. Phase 2 (mid exposure) = planned relocation: pre-planned, consent-based cross-border relocation with cultural-preservation obligations (community clustering, language/culture infrastructure, portable rights) triggered before the population is forced to flee. Phase 3 (high/near-nationless exposure) = absorb-and-protect: permanent resettlement in host states subject to a host-absorption-capacity constraint and a cultural-preservation floor. The objective is lexicographic: (i) minimize unprotected/unresettled EDPs (hard, primary), subject to (ii) a hard cultural-preservation floor, with host-cost a soft consideration only among feasible allocations. This is sound because it makes the human-rights protection objective non-tradeable (first in the lexicographic order) and treats cultural preservation as a constraint rather than a utility term that can be bought off with cost savings, which matches the ICM-F's stated priority of 'specifically focusing on protecting the rights ... while also aiming to preserve culture.'

### Modeling Process

The policy model (policy_model.py) takes the yearly EDP inflow from Subtask 1 and allocates it across host states. A Phase-1 protection lever protect_frac in [0,1] is the fraction of newly-exposed people whom in-situ adaptation keeps from becoming EDPs, so the inflow to resettlement is gross*(1-protect_frac). Host states each have an absorption capacity (total seats over the horizon), a hosting cost per person per year, and a cultural-affinity coefficient (1 for a same-region/same-culture host, 0 for none). The cultural floor has two parts: a SHARE constraint - at least 'floor' of a cohort's resettled members must be in a culturally coherent enclave (same origin-nation cluster) - and an absolute COHERENCE threshold M_min: an enclave is coherent only if the origin's cumulative presence in the host reaches M_min, because a community below M_min cannot sustain its own schools, places of worship, and daily language use. The allocation is greedy-lexicographic: for each origin and year, fill the designated host first (the realistic UN mechanism: one primary resettlement destination per origin, which is what builds enclave mass), then the cheapest other hosts; arrivals join an enclave once the enclave is open (presence >= M_min) and sit on a cheaper 'cultural-preservation fund' track before it opens (rights protected, but no coherent enclave yet); if no host has residual capacity the person is counted unprotected. The floor check reports, per origin, the enclave share of its resettled population and whether share >= floor.

### Outcome Analysis

The policy answers the four questions. WHERE: a designated primary host per origin (a regional Pacific host such as Fiji for the Pacific atolls; the largest-capacity host for the larger Maldivian cohort) with overflow to other hosts, plus a cultural-preservation fund for sub-threshold cohorts. WHO TAKES THEM: a portfolio of host states with hard absorption capacities and per-person hosting costs, chosen so total capacity exceeds the worst-case inflow, so that 'unprotected' is driven to zero. WHO GETS A SAY: the lexicographic order encodes it - the EDP's own protection right is the primary objective (individuals' rights first), the cultural floor is a binding constraint the UN enforces (the intergovernmental say), and host states participate through their capacity/cost parameters (the government say) without being able to veto protection. RECOMMENDED CONFIGURATION: protect_frac = 0.3, floor = 0.5, tiered M_min, one designated host per origin. Under this configuration, total unprotected EDPs = 0 (the primary objective is met in every scenario), 78,229 are resettled within the 2030-2110 policy horizon, and the cultural floor is met for all four origins when M_min is tiered to ~500 (Tuvalu 51.8%, Kiribati 94.3%, Marshall Islands 85.5%, Maldives 99.0% enclave share). Limitations: the host capacities, costs, and affinities are structural placeholders, not negotiated figures; the lexicographic order makes host-cost strictly subordinate, so it does not capture a host's political refusal (modeled only as exhausted capacity); and 'full participation in life in the new home' is represented by the rights-participation guarantee baked into the floor, not by a measured integration metric.

### Problem

Subtask 3 (Development of the model used to measure policy impact). Describe how the model that measures the potential impact of the proposed policies was developed: its modules, the interface between them, the parameters, and how each was calibrated or bounded. This must show the model as a coherent system, not two separate analyses.

### Analysis

The model is a two-stage coupled system. Stage 1 (exposure) converts the physical forcing (IPCC AR6 sea-level scenarios) and the geographic exposure (atoll elevation, low-band population concentration) into a scenario-valued, time-resolved stream of newly-displaced persons. Stage 2 (policy) converts that stream, together with the host-portfolio and the policy levers (protect_frac, floor, M_min, host designation), into outcomes: numbers protected in place, resettled in coherent enclaves, placed on the cultural-preservation fund, left unprotected, and the hosting cost. The coupling is the yearly EDP inflow: the policy model reads only the increment (displaced_pop(t)-displaced_pop(t-1)) from the exposure model, which is the correct interface because a person displaced in year t-1 is already being protected and should not be re-allocated in year t. Development followed the interaction policy: the expert first fixed the structural reading of the exposure axis (Exchange 1), then the expert accepted the phase/lexicographic structure of the policy module (Exchange 2), and finally the expert identified the failure mode that the policy module must be able to surface (Exchange 3). Each module's parameters were chosen to be bounded and scenario-valued rather than to fit an empirical target, and both scripts are parameterized by command line with a --sweep flag so any value can be tested without new code.

### Modeling Process

Module 1 (exposure_model.py): inputs are scenario in {low, mid, high, tail}, d0 (early-degradation offset, m), k (softness, m^-1), and per-nation {pop, h_max, frac_low}. It outputs, per nation and year, S(t), land_submerged_frac, displaced_frac, displaced_pop, residual_pop. Module 2 (policy_model.py): inputs are protect_frac, floor, M_min, designate (none or a per-origin primary host), and the host portfolio {capacity, cost, affinity}. Its state is, per (origin, host), the cumulative presence (which opens the enclave at M_min) and the residual host capacity. The allocation is the lexicographic greedy described in Subtask 2. Outputs are, per origin: cumulative_protected, cumulative_enclave, cumulative_fund, cumulative_unprotected, enclave_share, floor_met; and globally: total_unprotected, total_resettled, total_hosting_cost. A single command recomputes either module over a grid of its parameters, e.g. exposure_model.py --scenario mid --sweep d0=0.3, k=1.5 and policy_model.py --sweep protect=0.0/0.3/0.6, M_min=3000/5000/10000.

### Outcome Analysis

The development is validated by three checks. (1) Structural: the exposure module produces a scenario-graded, non-front-loaded displacement ramp (verified: yearly increments grow gently from 2030 to 2110 and the 50%-crossing year moves from 'never' under 'low' to the 2080s under 'tail'), which is the property a binary trigger could not produce. (2) Coupling: the policy model reads increments, so total resettled (111,953 at protect=0) equals the sum of yearly increments and is not double-counted. (3) Failure-mode: the policy module reproduces the expert's counterexample - with no primary-host designation the small, dispersed Tuvaluan and Marshallese cohorts fail the coherence threshold (enclave share 0.0, floor_met False) while the large Maldivian cohort meets it (enclave share 0.83), i.e. the share floor is satisfiable in aggregate but infeasible in coherence, exactly as the expert described. The model's value is that it makes this infeasibility visible and quantified rather than averaging it away. Bias: both modules use structural placeholders for parameters the data does not carry (host capacities/costs, enclave M_min), so absolute policy outcomes should be read as a decision procedure and a robustness ranking, not as a point forecast.

### Problem

Subtask 4 (How the model was used to design and improve the policies). Show concretely how running the policy model over its parameter space changed the proposed policies, i.e. that the model did more than check a fixed policy - it drove the choice of the protection level, the coherence threshold, the host-designation mechanism, and the fund degradation.

### Analysis

The policy model is used as a design tool, not a checker: the recommended policy is the point in the parameter space that the model identifies as meeting the lexicographic objective. Each lever was varied and the model's outputs (total_unprotected, per-origin floor_met, total_hosting_cost) read back to decide the setting. This is the ICM-F's requested explanation of how the model was used to design and/or improve the proposed policies.

### Modeling Process

Four levers were swept. (1) protect_frac (Phase-1 protection intensity): 0.0 -> 0.3 -> 0.6. The model shows total_resettled falls from 111,953 (protect=0.0) to 78,229 (0.3) to 44,656 (0.6), so every unit of in-situ protection avoids a resettlement, at the cost of not appearing in the resettled/protected-in-host count. The recommended 0.3 balances protection against the fact that under the 'tail' scenario even high protection cannot stop eventual displacement. (2) M_min (coherence threshold): 3000 / 5000 / 10000 / 2000 / 1000 / 500. At M_min=5000 the floor is met only for the large cohorts; at M_min=500 it is met for all four origins. The model therefore shows a single global M_min is infeasible for the small atoll nations and that the threshold must be tiered by cohort size. (3) designate (none vs one primary host per origin): the model shows designation raises the per-origin enclave share (e.g. Kiribati 0.15 -> 0.51) at higher hosting cost, demonstrating that concentrating a cohort in one host is what makes the floor attainable. (4) floor (0.3/0.5/0.7): the recommended 0.5 is the value at which the tiered design is binding but not infeasible. The recommended policy (protect=0.3, floor=0.5, tiered M_min down to ~500, one designated host per origin, fund track for sub-threshold cohorts) is the configuration the model selects as the lowest-cost one that sets total_unprotected=0 and floor_met=True for all origins.

### Outcome Analysis

The model improved the policies in four specific ways. First, it quantified the value of Phase-1 protection (each 0.1 of protect_frac removes ~11,000-16,000 people from the resettlement queue), which is the evidence that in-situ adaptation is the cheapest human-rights protection and should be the first phase. Second, it proved a single global coherence threshold is infeasible, forcing the policy to tier M_min by cohort size and to degrade to a cultural-preservation fund for the smallest cohorts - a policy refinement that would not have been obvious without the model. Third, it demonstrated the host-designation mechanism (one primary destination per origin) is the lever that makes cultural preservation attainable, converting it from a slogan into an allocable rule. Fourth, it confirmed that total_unprotected can be driven to zero within the host-portfolio capacity, which is the human-rights guarantee the ICM-F asked for. Implications: accepting the recommended policy means the UN commits to a phased, consent-based, culture-floored resettlement system with in-situ protection first; rejecting it (e.g. insisting on a single global M_min or on cost-minimization as the primary objective) leaves the small atoll nations' cultures unprotected precisely when the model shows they are most exposed, and under the tail scenario leaves a large, unprotected, culture-losing population with no systemized response.

### Problem

Subtask 5 (Importance of implementing the policies, backed by the analysis). Explain, with the model's results as evidence, why the proposed policies matter and what the consequences are of implementing versus not implementing them. This is the closing argument to the ICM-F as it advises the UN.

### Analysis

The importance argument rests on three model results: the scale of the at-risk population, the scenario-graded timing (the window to act is now, under 'low'; it closes within decades under 'high/tail'), and the infeasibility of cultural preservation without the floored, tiered, designated design. The argument is that the cost of acting (protect-in-place plus a capped, capacity-bounded resettlement system) is a small, bounded, and partly avoidable cost, while the cost of inaction is an unbounded, culture-losing, rights-violating displacement that no ad-hoc response can absorb.

### Modeling Process

The evidence is the set of computed outcomes. Scale: 226,581 (low) to 515,844 (tail) of the 699,000 people across the four nations are displaced by 2100. Timing: the 50%-displacement year for the Pacific atolls is 'never' under 'low', the 2080s under 'tail', i.e. the response window depends on the emissions path and is shortest under the high-emission paths the UN's own climate mandate targets. Cost: the recommended policy's hosting cost is a few hundred million dollars over the horizon against a population in the hundreds of thousands, and Phase-1 protection (protect_frac=0.3) removes ~33,700 people from the resettlement queue at in-situ cost. Feasibility boundary (the expert's counterexample, modeled): without the tiered floor and host designation, the lexicographic objective has no feasible point for the small atoll nations - 'minimize unprotected' is undefined and their cultural preservation is impossible - so the floored, tiered, designated design is not optional but the minimum structure that keeps the objective defined.

### Outcome Analysis

Implementing the policies: (a) protects human rights first, because the lexicographic order makes an unprotected EDP the primary failure and the model shows it can be driven to zero; (b) preserves culture where physically possible, because the tiered floor and host designation are exactly the conditions under which the enclave share reaches the floor; and (c) is affordable and bounded, because the cost is a capped hosting cost plus cheaper in-situ protection, and Phase-1 protection shrinks the problem before it becomes a resettlement problem. Not implementing (or implementing a weaker policy): under the 'tail' scenario more than 80% of the four nations' population is displaced by 2110 with no systemized response, the small atoll nations' cultures are lost because no coherent enclave can form (the modeled infeasibility), and the displacement is unmanaged, costlier, and rights-violating. The model therefore supports a strong recommendation: adopt the phased, floored, tiered, consent-based system now, while the 'low' and 'mid' scenarios still leave a large protection window, because the same system is the only structure that remains feasible if the emissions path turns out to be the high or tail case. The central implication is that the decision to build the system is a decision made under the low-emission assumption's window of feasibility, and deferring it forfeits a window that the model shows closes with the emissions scenario.
