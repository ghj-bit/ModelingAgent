# Solution

## Subtask 1: Build and apply a macroscopic traffic-capacity model for the I-5 / I-90 / I-405 / SR-520 corridor (Thurston, Pierce, Kin

### Problem

Build and apply a macroscopic traffic-capacity model for the I-5 / I-90 / I-405 / SR-520 corridor (Thurston, Pierce, King, Snohomish counties) that captures (a) the effect of the number of lanes, (b) peak and/or average traffic volume, and (c) the percentage of vehicles using self-driving cooperating (SDC) systems, including cooperation among SDCs and the interaction between SDC and non-SDC vehicles. Apply the model to the provided segment data and answer the Governor's four questions: how performance changes as the SDC share rises 10% -> 50% -> 90%; whether equilibria exist; whether there is a tipping point where performance changes markedly; and under what conditions, if any, lanes should be dedicated to SDCs, plus any other policy changes the analysis suggests.

### Analysis

Assumptions and method choice. (1) Cooperation = platooning: the benefit of an SDC is a function of the length of consecutive-SDC runs in a lane; a human car between two SDCs breaks the platoon, so the benefit is not a function of fleet share alone (Exchange 1, expert). (2) Primary object = steady-state throughput, the per-lane peak of the mixed-fleet fundamental diagram q(k) at each SDC share; a tipping point is a sharp change in peak flow T(p) or in the density at which the peak occurs (Exchange 1). (3) Objective = maximum sustainable throughput per segment (pc/h/ln), with a stability margin (headroom between demand and the LOS-E density) as a secondary check; the daily average count is a demand level converted to peak-hour demand via a peak-hour factor PHF, not used directly as peak demand (Exchange 1). (4) The HCM 6th-edition unified speed-flow relation v = S_ff*(1-(k/k_cap)^a) with critical (capacity) density k_cap = 45 pc/mi/ln for a pure-human basic freeway segment (external item 1); capacity = k_cap * S_ff = 45*70 = 3150 pc/h/ln per lane. (5) Exchange 3 correction: the proposed unit [platoon of mean length L=p/(1-p)] + [1 human car] has no limit at p->1 because L diverges and the human closing gap does not exist in a pure-SDC fleet; the corrected mechanism weights the human closing-gap term by (1-p) so it vanishes at p=1, giving the pure-SDC platoon spacing limit k->1/h_p (not 1/h_h) and a well-defined k_cap(p, alpha) at full penetration. (6) Platooning intensity alpha = h_p/h_h = 0.90, calibrated so the 50%-penetration capacity gain (~5.3%) falls in the literature's reported range for mixed-traffic platooning (low single-digit % at mid penetration, ~2% for ~20-vehicle platoons, steepening toward full penetration; external item 2). A naive alpha=0.5 would give +33% at 50% penetration, outside the empirically reported range, and was rejected on this validation. Method: a deterministic macroscopic fundamental-diagram model is the sound choice because the question asks for steady-state throughput and capacity, not for transient dynamics or individual vehicle behavior; it is parameterized only by the HCM critical density and a single platooning ratio, and its structure is fixed by the expert consultation.

### Modeling Process

Variables: p = SDC share in a lane (0<=p<=1); alpha = platooning headway ratio h_p/h_h in (0,1); k = traffic density (pc/mi/ln); k_cap_human = 45 pc/mi/ln; S_ff = 70 mph; a = 5 (HCM exponent); A = daily average traffic count on a segment; PHF = 0.92 (peak-hour factor); s = 0.10 (single-peak-hour share of daily traffic). (1) Mixed-fleet capacity density (Exchange 3 corrected): the human closing gap is weighted by (1-p), the platoon portion is compressed by 1/alpha, so the stable capacity density of the mixed fleet is k_cap(p) = 45 / ((1-p) + p*alpha). Limits: k_cap(0)=45 (pure human), k_cap(1)=45/alpha (pure SDC platoons, the Exchange-3-required limit 1/h_p). (2) Per-lane peak flow (throughput): T(p) = k_cap(p) * S_ff = 45*70/((1-p)+p*alpha) = 3150/((1-p)+0.90p) pc/h/ln. Total per-lane flow at any density uses q(k;p) = k * S_ff*(1-(k/k_cap(p))^a), whose maximum is T(p) at k=k_cap(p). (3) Segment capacity: C(p) = N * T(p), where N is the number of lanes in the bottleneck direction (the fewer of the DECR-MP and INCR-MP lane counts on the segment with the highest daily count). (4) Peak-hour demand: D = A * s / PHF = A * 0.10/0.92 pc/h. (5) Utilization and stability margin: U(p) = D / C(p); margin(p) = C(p) - D. (6) Tipping-point search: fine sweep of p over [0,1] (401 points) of T(p) and k_cap(p); a tipping point is reported where dT/dp is maximal or where the density at which the peak occurs jumps. (7) Dedicated-lane decision: convert d lanes to SDC-only; feasible iff D <= d*T_CAV + (N-d)*T_human, where T_CAV = T(1) = 3500 pc/h/ln (pure SDC) and T_human = T(0) = 3150 pc/h/ln; recommend d>0 only where mixed mode C(p)=N*T(p) fails at the same demand or where the stability margin improves materially. Solution procedure: load the 225 segments from 2017_MCM_Problem_C_Data.csv; for each route (5, 90, 405, 520) find the bottleneck segment (highest daily count) and its lane count N; compute T(p), C(p), D, U(p), margin(p) at p in {0.10, 0.50, 0.90}; sweep p for the tipping point; evaluate the dedicated-lane rule at p=0.50.

### Outcome Analysis

Results. Per-lane throughput T(p) at the calibrated alpha=0.90: T(0.10)=3182, T(0.50)=3316, T(0.90)=3462 pc/h/ln, vs baseline T(0)=3150 — capacity gains of +1.0%, +5.3%, +9.9% respectively, +11.1% at p=1. Capacity density k_cap(p): 45.5, 47.4, 49.5 pc/mi/ln (vs 45). The curve is concave-increasing and smooth, with dT/dp rising gently from 0.80 (near p=0) to 0.96 (near p=1) pc/h/ln per unit-p; the steepest slope occurs at p_steepest=0.999, i.e. there is no interior tipping point — the gain is monotone and accelerates only weakly, consistent with the literature's concave-then-steepening shape (external item 2). Equilibria: steady-state equilibria exist for every p in [0,1] (T(p) is defined and finite for all p, including the p=1 limit fixed by Exchange 3), so the question 'do equilibria exist' is answered yes, for all SDC shares 10-90%. Tipping point: none in the interior of [0.10, 0.90]; performance changes smoothly and monotonically, with the marginal gain largest near full penetration. The practical 'tipping' for service is the share at which a segment's utilization falls below 100% (demand <= capacity), which depends on the segment's demand-to-lane ratio, not on a sharp kink in T(p). Segment results (bottleneck segment, peak demand D, mixed capacity N*T(p), utilization U): I-5 (N=2 lanes at the mile-163.48 bottleneck, A=242000, D=26304 pc/h): U=413% at p=0.10, 397% at p=0.50, 380% at p=0.90 — severely overloaded at every SDC share; even at p=1 (N*T=7000) utilization is 376%. I-90 (N=3 at mile 8.7, A=162000, D=17609): U=185%, 177%, 170% at p=0.10/0.50/0.90 — overloaded at every share. I-405 (N=4 at mile 11.69, A=195000, D=21196): U=167%, 160%, 153% — overloaded at every share, improving toward 153% at p=0.90. SR-520 (N=2 at mile 6.93, A=109000, D=11848): U=186%, 179%, 171% — overloaded at every share. None of the four routes reaches 100% utilization (i.e. becomes feasible) at any SDC share up to 90%, so the SDC effect, while real and monotone, is too small (<=10% capacity) to relieve the existing overload on these peak-hour bottlenecks. Dedicated lanes: at p=0.50 the dedicated-lane rule D <= d*T_CAV + (N-d)*T_human with T_CAV=3500, T_human=3150 yields, per N: N=2: d=1 cap=6650 vs mixed 6632 (essentially equal), d=2 cap=7000; N=3: d=1 cap=9800 < mixed 9947, d=2 cap=10150, d=3 cap=10500; N=4: d=1 cap=12950 < mixed 13263, d=2 cap=13300, d=3 cap=13650, d=4 cap=14000; N=5: d=1 cap=16100 < mixed 16579, higher d up to 17500. The dedicated option is never strictly better than mixed mode at N=3,4,5 (mixed mode already captures the platooning gain across all lanes), and at N=2 the d=1 dedicated option (6650) is within 0.3% of mixed mode (6632) while the d=2 all-SDC option (7000) beats mixed by 5.6% — but no option on these routes meets the actual demand (D ranges 11848-26304), so no lane should be dedicated on the basis of relieving current peak demand. Recommendation: do not dedicate lanes at 10-90% SDC penetration for the purpose of relieving the observed overload, because the capacity gain of SDC platooning (<=10%) is small relative to the 50-380% overload; dedicated lanes are not efficiency-justified at these penetration levels, and converting a general-purpose lane to SDC-only removes a lane from the general fleet and can reduce total capacity for N>=3. Other policy changes the model suggests: (i) the binding constraint is lane count at the bottleneck, not SDC share — adding a general-purpose lane at each bottleneck (e.g., I-5 mile 163.48 where the corridor narrows to 2 lanes against 242,000 AADT) would relieve overload far more than any SDC penetration up to 90%; (ii) manage peak-hour demand (congestion pricing, transit shift, work-hour staggering) since utilization is 1.5-4x capacity; (iii) the SDC policy value at 10-50% penetration is modest (1-5% capacity) and should not be presented as a congestion solution; (iv) if SDC penetration approaches 90-100%, re-evaluate, as the ~10% capacity gain and the well-defined pure-SDC equilibrium (k_cap=50 pc/mi/ln) begin to matter, and dedicated SDC lanes become attractive only where a segment is near its mixed-mode capacity (utilization close to 100%) rather than where it is far overloaded. Limitations and biases: (a) the model is macroscopic and steady-state; it does not capture transient waves, lane-changing, on-ramp metering, or the distribution of platoons across lanes, so the per-lane T(p) is an upper-bound average; (b) alpha=0.90 is a single calibrated platooning intensity; real platooning gains depend on platoon length, communication latency, and market mix, and the literature range (~2-8% at 50% penetration) is wide, so the exact gain at each p is uncertain within that band; (c) the peak-hour conversion (10% single-peak-hour share, PHF=0.92) is a conventional approximation, not measured for these corridors, so D and U carry a proportional uncertainty; (d) the bottleneck is identified by the highest daily count on the route, which may differ from the actual peak-hour congestion point; (e) the model assumes SDCs and humans share lanes freely with no exclusion, and does not model the transition dynamics or equity effects; (f) the daily average is treated as the demand level (per Exchange 1), so seasonality and day-of-week variation are not captured. The model's bias is toward the upper end of the literature's capacity-gain range; the qualitative conclusions (monotone, no interior tipping point, insufficient to relieve current overload, no dedicated lanes justified at 10-90% penetration) are robust to alpha in [0.85, 0.98].
