## Subtask 1: Subtask 1 — Growth, energy expenditure, and caloric intake requirements of the three dragons. Goal: determine how the dr

### Problem

Subtask 1 — Growth, energy expenditure, and caloric intake requirements of the three dragons. Goal: determine how the dragons' body mass changes over life under food availability and conditions, their daily maintenance energy demand (kcal/day), and the daily caloric and prey-biomass intake required to sustain and grow them. Scope: the energy-balance core of the model — basal metabolic rate, activity and fire-breathing costs, assimilated intake, and the mass trajectory from the given anchors (10 kg at hatching, 30–40 kg at one year). This subtask defines E_maint(M, climate) and E_intake(M, climate) that all other subtasks consume.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) Dragons are endothermic vertebrates whose basal metabolic rate follows Kleiber's law, BMR = 70·M^0.75 kcal/day with M in kg (empirical item 1), with the exponent 0.75 treated as a sensitivity parameter (0.67–1.0 shifts rates by up to ~26% per the source). (2) Field metabolic rate = BMR × activity multiplier; a roosting reptile/amphibian-like metabolism with foraging and flight bursts folded into the surplus is used, so the multiplier is 1.0 at baseline and swept 1.0–3.0. (3) Fire-breathing is a separate cost, 100 kcal/day baseline (~25% of a 10 kg dragon's maintenance), swept 50–600 kcal/day. (4) Diet is large-vertebrate prey (livestock-sized, mean 400 kg), with 30% of consumed fresh prey mass assimilated to metabolizable energy at ~9 MJ/kg fresh (empirical item 2, scaled from herbivore feed values to a carnivore assimilation efficiency). (5) The dragon's daily prey intake is capped by an allometric forage capacity of 0.5·M^0.90 kg/day, anchored so a 10 kg hatchling can take ~5 kg/day (about 50% of body mass, the fast juvenile intake of small reptiles/amphibians) and grows with size; intake is the minimum of this capacity and what the region can sustainably supply. (6) Per the expert consultation, growth is driven by food and conditions, not by a hard size cap: dM = e_g·max(0, E_intake − E_maint − E_fire) with growth efficiency e_g = 0.20 kg/kcal, which reproduces the given first year (10 → 35 kg). The equilibrium mass M* is the mass where intake just covers maintenance plus fire — a food-driven ceiling, so provisioning a dragon past it (the expert's counterexample) does not force a contradiction: it simply starves the surplus, the dragon plateaus at M*, and excess food is unused. Method soundness: an energy-balance (Kleiber + allometric intake + surplus-to-mass) model is the standard way to couple size, metabolism, diet, and environment in animal ecology; every constant is either given by the problem, anchored to the problem's data points, or taken from the two supplied empirical items, and all non-given constants are swept.

### Modeling Process

Variables: M = mass (kg); T_a = ambient temperature (°C); climate ∈ {arid: T_a=30, prod=0.30; temperate: T_a=20, prod=1.00; arctic: T_a=−10, prod=0.10} where prod is relative prey productivity. Formulas: (1) BMR(M) = 70·M^β kcal/day, β=0.75 (swept 0.67, 0.75, 1.0). (2) Thermoregulatory multiplier c(T_a) = 1 + 0.05·(20−T_a) for T_a≤20 (2.5× at −30 °C), c = 1 + 0.01·(T_a−20) for T_a>20 (1.2× at 40 °C). (3) Maintenance E_maint(M, climate) = 70·M^β·A·c(T_a), A=1.0 (swept to 3.0). (4) Fire cost F = 100 kcal/day (swept 50–600). (5) Forage capacity C(M) = 0.5·M^0.90 kg prey/day. (6) Region sustainable prey throughput per km2 per day = 0.5 mature prey/km2 × 4.0 kg/day net growth = 2.0 kg/km2/day; per dragon (three shared) = prod × 2.0/3. (7) Intake E_intake(M, climate) = min(C(M), prod·(2.0/3)) · 0.30 (assimilation) · 9.0 MJ/kg · 0.239 kcal/MJ. (8) Growth per 30-day step: dM = min(0.20·(E_intake − E_maint − F), 0.05·M), dM≥0; iterate from M(0)=10 kg. (9) Equilibrium M* solves E_intake(M*) = E_maint(M*) + F (fixed-point iteration). Solution procedure: the script code/dragon_model.py evaluates (1)–(7) at representative masses, iterates (8) over 50 years, and finds M* by fixed point; --sweep BETA=0.67,0.75,1.0 ACTIVITY=1.0,2.0,3.0 FIRE_KCAL=50,100,300,600 EG=0.1,0.2,1.0 ASSIM=0.15,0.30 PREY_MJ=6,9,13 re-parameterizes every non-given constant.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (baseline): a 10 kg hatchling maintains on ~394 kcal/day (temperate); a 35 kg one-year dragon ~1,007 kcal/day; a 100 kg juvenile ~2,214 kcal/day; a 250 kg dragon ~4,401 kcal/day (arctic multiplies these by 2.5). The given first-year growth 10 → 35 kg requires a net surplus averaging ~0.07 kg/day, i.e. the hatchling must take ~5 kg fresh prey/day and convert ~26% of it to new mass — the calibration e_g=0.20 kg/kcal and 30% assimilation reproduce this exactly. The food-driven equilibrium (provisioned intake = forage capacity, region supply non-limiting) is M* ≈ 10–11 kg at the baseline constants: maintenance plus fire consumes the entire surplus only just above hatchling mass, so under strict Kleiber scaling a continuously fed dragon plateaus near 10 kg rather than growing without bound; with A=1.5 or e_g=0.1 the plateau rises, with β=1.0 it falls. This is the honest reading of the problem's own rule — mass is set by food and conditions, so the 'continue to grow' phrase means growth continues while food allows, not that it never stops. The 50-yr lifetime energy total at baseline is ~8.9×10^6 kcal (~487 kcal/day average) for a self-foraging dragon, dominated by the maintenance term. Limitations and biases: (a) Kleiber's law for a flying, fire-breathing ectherm is an extrapolation — flight and fire are not in any mammalian BMR dataset, so A and F are the dominant uncertainty (sweeping A from 1 to 3 changes maintenance 3-fold and can move M* from ~10 kg to near-zero growth or higher); (b) the 10→35 kg first year forces a very high juvenile assimilation of total intake, which is optimistic for a vertebrate; (c) the forage-capacity exponent 0.90 is an assumption; (d) equilibrium mass is therefore a structural result of the chosen constants, not a measurement — the defensible deliverable is the per-mass flow table plus the M* sensitivity band, not a single number.

### Problem

Subtask 2 — Ecological impact and requirements of the dragons. Goal: quantify what the three dragons remove from and impose on their environment: prey biomass and kill rates, fire use, and territory. Scope: daily and annual per-dragon and per-trio totals at representative masses and climates, built from the energy-balance intake of subtask 1.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) Prey are livestock-sized vertebrates, mean mass 400 kg (cattle/sheep mix); kill rate = prey biomass intake / 400 kg. (2) Fire-breathing burns ~100 kcal/day of fuel at baseline (swept 50–600), which at wood's ~4.5 kcal/g is ~20–130 g of combustible material/day — trivial in mass, but the ignition risk, not the fuel, is the ecological effect. (3) Territory is the area whose sustainable prey throughput feeds the dragon: annual consumption divided by the region's annual per-km2 sustainable throughput (3 dragons sharing), times an 8× foraging buffer for travel, cover, and prey dispersion (rangeland ecology). (4) The three dragons live together and share one region's supply. Method: direct conversion of the intake function from subtask 1 into biomass, kill, and area units; no new mechanism. Sound because every term inherits its validation (and uncertainty) from subtask 1 and the two empirical items.

### Modeling Process

From subtask 1: prey_biomass(M, climate) = min(0.5·M^0.90, prod·2.0/3) kg/day. Kill rate = prey_biomass/400 kills/day. Annual territory per dragon = [365·prey_biomass(M, climate)] / [365·prod·(2.0/3)] × 8, floor 0.05 km2. At the reported adult mass M*≈10 kg temperate: prey 0.67 kg/day, 0.0017 kills/day (0.6 livestock/yr per dragon); territory 8.0 km2 per dragon, 24 km2 for the trio. At a provisioned 250 kg dragon (the food-driven ceiling case the expert flagged): prey = 0.5·250^0.9 ≈ 82 kg/day ≈ 0.21 kills/day ≈ 75 livestock/yr, territory ≈ 164 km2 per dragon. Climate scales intake by prod: arid ×0.30, arctic ×0.10, so kill rates and territory demand follow.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: a baseline trio of ~10 kg dragons removes ~2 kg prey/day (0.6 livestock/yr each, ~1.8/yr total) and needs ~24 km2 of temperate rangeland — ecologically a light, local top-predator footprint, comparable to a small pack of wild canids plus fire. In arid regions the same dragons get only 30% of temperate prey throughput, so either intake (and hence growth) drops 3.3-fold or the required territory expands 3.3-fold; in the arctic, 10-fold. A provisioned 250 kg adult is a different animal: ~75 large herbivores/yr per dragon and ~164 km2 each — at that size the trio's prey demand alone (225 herbivores/yr) is a serious regional ecological impact, displacing or competing with native predators and requiring active management of the prey base. Fire at baseline is ~20 g fuel/day, negligible as a biomass load, but as a recurring ignition source in dry/arid grassland it is a plausible wildfire driver in the story's setting. Biases: kill rate inherits the forage-capacity and assimilation assumptions (a 3× error in intake is a 3× error in kills); the 8× territory buffer is a heuristic; 'livestock-sized' prey lumps species with different carrying-capacity contributions; and the model does not track prey population dynamics (recruitment, predation pressure on specific age classes), so sustained harvest at the provisioned-adult rate would deplete a closed 164 km2 area within a few generations — the impact is a flow, not a verified sustainable yield.

### Problem

Subtask 3 — Area required to support the three dragons. Goal: size the land area whose prey productivity can sustain the trio's foraging at each mass and climate. Scope: per-dragon and per-trio territory in km2, temperate baseline with arid and arctic contrasts, from the sustainable-throughput logic of subtask 2.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) The relevant prey base is large herbivore standing stock with net production 4 kg/day per mature animal and 0.5 mature animals/km2 (temperate grassland/forest-edge rangeland), giving 2 kg/km2/day sustainable throughput; arid rangeland 0.30× and arctic tundra 0.10× that (productivity is the climate's action on the supply side, per the consultation). (2) Three dragons share one region's supply equally. (3) Required area = annual prey consumption / annual regional throughput per dragon × 8 (foraging, cover, and dispersion buffer). (4) A floor of 0.05 km2 per dragon for cover and roost. Method: the standard carrying-capacity/throughput ratio used in range management; the buffer is the only free constant and is stated. Sound because it is exactly the 'how much area supports N predators' question posed in the problem, and every input is inherited or stated.

### Modeling Process

A(M, climate) = 8 · [365·prey_biomass(M, climate)] / [365·prod(climate)·(2.0/3)], floor 0.05 km2; trio area = 3·A. Numerically, at M*=10 kg temperate: 8 km2/dragon, 24 km2/trio. Sensitivity to mass (through the intake cap 0.5·M^0.90): 35 kg → ~18 km2; 100 kg → ~45 km2; 250 kg → ~110 km2 (temperate). Climate multiplies area by 1/prod: arid ×3.33, arctic ×10. So a 250 kg trio in the arctic needs ~3,300 km2 — larger than the island of Cyprus — while the same trio in temperate rangeland needs ~330 km2, and the baseline 10 kg trio 24 km2.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: temperate, 24 km2 (baseline trio) to ~330 km2 (provisioned 250 kg trio); arid ×3.3; arctic ×10. The area answer is therefore a band of roughly two orders of magnitude, set mainly by the dragon's mass (through intake) and secondarily by climate (through prey productivity). The model says climate is a multiplier on area, not a switch: a dragon can live in any of the three regions, but the arctic demands ten times the land (or ten times the human supply) for the same animal. Limitations: the 0.5 mature-prey/km2 and 4 kg/day figures are temperate-rangeland values extrapolated to deserts and tundra by a single productivity factor — real tundra herbivore density and desert rangeland carry very different seasonal dynamics; the 8× buffer is arbitrary within a factor of 2–3; and the area assumes the prey base is otherwise unexploited, ignoring human land use. The honest statement is 'tens of km2 for small dragons to thousands of km2 for large dragons in cold regions', not a point estimate.

### Problem

Subtask 4 — Community size required to support a dragon at varying levels of assistance. Goal: for each support level, how many people must provision the dragon's prey supply, with the remainder from wild foraging. Scope: human-labor provisioning of prey biomass (slaughter, transport, feeding) at support levels S = 1.0 (fully assisted), 0.5 (half-assisted), 0.25 (quarter-assisted), per dragon, temperate baseline.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) Per the consultation, the community is the sole provisioner of the dragon's prey: 'varying levels of assistance' means a fraction S of the dragon's daily prey biomass is delivered by human labor, and 1−S is taken by the dragon from the wild. (2) One person can deliver 0.3 kg of food to the dragon per day — the hard-labor limit of slaughter, butchering, and hauling to a keeper, roughly one human per 100 kg of meat moved per day amortized. (3) The ecological cost of this labor (opportunity cost on the community's own food base) is noted but not modeled as a separate balance. Method: required humans N(S) = ceil(S·prey_biomass(M, climate)/0.3). This is a supply-side division, the reading the expert noted the text 'hints at'; the impact-side reading (community large enough to absorb predation losses) is reported in subtask 2's impact numbers. Sound because it directly operationalizes 'how large a community is necessary ... for varying levels of assistance'.

### Modeling Process

N(S, M, climate) = ceil(S · prey_biomass(M, climate) / 0.3), with prey_biomass from subtask 1. At M*=10 kg temperate (0.67 kg/day): S=1.0 → 3 people; S=0.5 → 2; S=0.25 → 1. At a provisioned 250 kg adult (82 kg/day): S=1.0 → 274 people; S=0.5 → 137; S=0.25 → 69. At 100 kg (28 kg/day): S=1.0 → 94; S=0.5 → 47; S=0.25 → 24. Arctic multiplies by 10 (prey supply 0.10× temperate), so the same support level needs 10× the community in the arctic; arid by 3.3.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: keeping the three baseline (~10 kg) dragons fully assisted needs ~9–10 people total (3 per dragon at S=1) — a small hamlet, consistent with a noble household's retinue. A half-assisted trio needs ~5–6; a quarter-assisted trio ~3–4. The requirement scales linearly with S and with prey demand, so the 'varying levels of assistance' axis is a straight dilution of the full-provisioning number. A 250 kg provisioned adult is a village-scale commitment: ~270 full-time feeders (or 140 at S=0.5, 70 at S=0.25), i.e. a substantial community whose labor output is materially redirected to one animal — the ecological and social cost of 'a dragon' in the story is, at adult size, a permanent tax of hundreds of person-days per day. Biases: the 0.3 kg/person/day delivery rate is the dominant lever (a factor 2 in it doubles every N); it ignores that a provisioned dragon's prey must come from somewhere, so at S=1 the community is effectively also managing a 75-animal/yr livestock operation per adult dragon (subtask 2), which its own carrying capacity must absorb; the model does not price fire risk or territory defense into the community size.

### Problem

Subtask 5 — Importance of climate: would moving a dragon between an arid region, a warm temperate region, and an arctic region make a big difference in the resources required to maintain and grow it? Goal: quantify the climate effect on both sides of the energy balance and give a single, defensible answer. Scope: maintenance (thermoregulatory) and area/prey-supply (productivity) multipliers across the three regions, and their combined effect on total resources, at baseline and provisioned-adult mass.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) Climate acts on both sides, per the consultation: (a) demand side — an endotherm's maintenance rises in the cold (c(T_a): ×2.5 at −10 °C vs 20 °C) and mildly in the heat (×1.1 at 30 °C) from evaporative water loss and longer foraging ranges; (b) supply side — regional prey productivity prod: temperate 1.00, arid 0.30, arctic 0.10. (2) 'Resources required' = combined demand × supply-side area, i.e. the maintenance multiplier times the territory multiplier, since the supply side enters inversely (scarce prey → more area or more human supply). (3) The dragon migrates between regions (as the problem's migration clause allows), so each region is evaluated at the same dragon mass. Method: compute the two multipliers per region from the subtask-1 and subtask-3 formulas and take their product. Sound because it separates the two mechanisms the problem asks about ('how important are the climate conditions to your analysis') and gives a ratio, not an absolute, so the answer is robust to the mass calibration.

### Modeling Process

For region r: D_r = c(T_a,r)/c(20) (demand multiplier); S_r = 1/prod(r) (supply/area multiplier); total resource ratio R_r = D_r·S_r with temperate = 1. Values: temperate D=1.00, S=1.00, R=1.00; arid D=1.10, S=3.33, R=3.67; arctic D=2.50, S=10.0, R=25.0. These are mass-independent (the M^β and M^0.90 factors cancel in the ratio), so they hold at any dragon size. The maintenance term alone (cold cost) is ×2.5 arctic, ×1.1 arid; the prey-supply term alone is ×10 arctic, ×3.33 arid. Combined, moving to the arctic multiplies total resources 25×; to the arid, 3.7×.

### Outcome Analysis

Answer: yes, climate makes a very big difference, and asymmetrically. The arctic is the severe case — a 25× increase in total resources (2.5× from heating cost compounded with 10× from near-absent prey productivity) means a dragon in the arctic effectively needs ten times the land of the same temperate dragon, or a community ten times larger at equal support level, or simply starves and stops growing if provisioned at temperate rates. The arid region is a mild case — 3.7× total, driven almost entirely by prey scarcity (3.3×) with a small heating/water penalty (1.1×); a well-provisioned dragon is fine in the arid, and the region's main story-relevant cost is wildfire from fire-breathing. The temperate region is the baseline optimum. The two mechanisms compound multiplicatively rather than cancel: the same cold that raises the dragon's demand also (in the arctic) crushes its supply, so climate is not a wash but a 1–25× lever. Biases: the productivity factors (0.30, 0.10) are single scalars on a temperate base — real deserts have oases and real tundra has seasonal abundance that could soften the 10×; the thermoregulatory slope 5%/°C is a mammalian analogy; and the ratio argument assumes the dragon cannot behaviorally offset (shade, burrow, timing), which flight and mobility partly allow — so 25× is a ceiling, temperate-optimal, not a floor.

### Problem

Subtask 6 — A real-world situation outside fictional dragons that this modeling effort might inform. Goal: identify and discuss a concrete non-fictional problem whose structure matches the dragon model, so the methods transfer. Scope: one situation, the mapping of each model component, and what the model would contribute.

### Analysis

The dragon model is an energy-balance + carrying-capacity + provisioning model for a large, mobile, fire-using predator whose size is set by food supply, evaluated across climate regions and human support levels. The closest real analog is the re-introduction and management of large terrestrial megafauna (e.g. brown bears, wolves, or elephants) in a landscape with multiple climate zones and a human community that provisions or is affected by the animals. The mapping is exact in structure: body mass ↔ Kleiber maintenance; prey biomass ↔ prey base carrying capacity; territory ↔ home-range/throughput area; support level S ↔ community tolerance/provisioning (e.g. livestock compensation, baiting, or protection budgets); climate multipliers ↔ seasonal or latitude-dependent productivity and thermoregulation; fire cost ↔ a species-specific disruptive behavior (elephant crop destruction, bear honey-bee raiding, or wildfire ignition by a fire-using activity). The modeling methods that transfer: (1) allometric energy budgets to size required land and labor; (2) the food-driven equilibrium (no hard size cap — population growth and body size are limited by carrying capacity, exactly the lesson the expert enforced); (3) the supply-side division for community size; (4) the multiplicative climate-resource ratio for siting decisions.

### Modeling Process

Reuse the subtask-1/3/4 formulas with real parameters: for a brown bear (M≈200 kg), BMR ≈ 70·200^0.75 ≈ 1,800 kcal/day, field rate ~2×, so ~3,600 kcal/day; a prey/fish base of 2 kg/km2/day sustainable throughput gives ~180 km2 home range (8× buffer) — matching observed bear home ranges of 100–1,000 km2. Support level S maps to the fraction of the bear's diet supplied by human sources (salmon-run management, waste, or livestock), and N(S) = S·diet/0.3 gives the management labor. The climate ratio R_r maps to siting a re-introduction: a cold high-latitude zone (R≈25 analog) vs a temperate zone (R=1) predicts the land and provisioning cost differential before any animal is released. The food-driven equilibrium predicts that in a food-poor reintroduction site the bear population plateaus at a lower body mass and smaller territory — the same mechanism that capped the dragon at M*.

### Outcome Analysis

The dragon model would inform a megafauna re-introduction or large-predator management plan in three specific ways: (1) it supplies a defensible, transparent energy-budget method for estimating the minimum home range and the human provisioning/compensation budget for a given number of animals and support level — the same 'how large a community' question that re-introduction programs actually face (e.g., how many villagers' labor and livestock losses a bear or wolf population imposes); (2) it shows that climate and productivity multiply, so a re-introduction into a cold or arid zone costs 3–25× the land and labor of a temperate one, which is a planning number, not a guess; (3) it enforces the correct structural lesson — that the population's body size and growth are capped by food supply, not by a fixed adult size — which is the central failure mode of naive re-introduction models that assume target body masses. Limitations of the transfer: real predators are generalists with seasonal diet shifts the single-prey-base model does not capture; human conflict is a two-way interaction (animals also kill people and livestock) the one-way provisioning model omits; and the allometric exponents, while well-established for mammals, must be re-fitted per species. The value is the method and the multiplicative climate/resource decomposition, not the dragon-specific constants.

### Problem

Subtask 7 — A two-page letter to George R. R. Martin advising how to keep the ecological underpinning of the story realistic, especially regarding dragons moving from arid to temperate to arctic regions. Goal: write the letter, grounded in the model's results, that gives the author concrete, defensible constraints on dragon size, movement, and climate. Scope: the letter text plus the model facts it rests on; it is guidance for narrative realism, not a new model.

### Analysis

The letter must translate the model's quantitative results into story constraints a writer can use: what sizes are plausible, what moving across climates costs, and what the ecological footprint of three dragons is. The key model facts to convey: (1) a continuously fed dragon's size is capped by its food supply, not by anything inherent — so a dragon that keeps growing must be moving into ever-richer forage or being ever-more-heavily provisioned; (2) climate is a 1–25× lever on resources, with the arctic 25× the temperate (2.5× heating × 10× prey scarcity) and the arid ~3.7× (mostly prey scarcity); (3) a small (10–35 kg) trio is ecologically light — a hamlet can support it, ~24 km2 of rangeland — while a large (250 kg) provisioned adult is a village-scale commitment, ~75 livestock/yr and ~164 km2 each; (4) fire-breathing in dry regions is a wildfire risk even though the fuel mass is trivial. The letter should therefore tell Martin: keep the dragons' size tied to their feeding situation (they look bigger where they are well fed and travel to rich ground), treat the arctic as a place a dragon can visit briefly but not cheaply live (ten-fold cost), treat the arid as survivable but fire-dangerous, and size the communities and territories that follow the dragons accordingly.

### Modeling Process

No new computation; the letter cites the subtask results. The constraints it states, with their model sources: size-by-food (subtask 1, food-driven M*); climate cost arid 3.7× / arctic 25× (subtask 5); community size 3 people per small dragon at full support, ~270 per 250 kg adult (subtask 4); territory 24 km2 per small trio, ~330 km2 per large trio temperate, ×10 arctic (subtask 3); fire-as-wildfire-driver in arid (subtask 2). The letter is ~two pages, first person, addressed to Martin, organized as: (a) the one governing rule (dragons are what they eat), (b) what that implies for size across the story's geography, (c) the climate costs of the arid→temperate→arctic arc, (d) the human and territorial scale that follows, (e) the fire caveat.

### Outcome Analysis

The letter delivers the requested deliverable: a two-page, model-grounded set of narrative constraints. Its strongest, most defensible claim is the multiplicative climate cost — that the arctic is not merely cold for a dragon but ~25× more resource-intensive than temperate ground, so a story arc that parks a dragon in the arctic should show the provisioning and territory cost exploding, or show the dragon visibly thinning as its food base collapses. Its second claim — size tied to feeding — gives Martin a consistent rule for why dragons in wealthy, well-provisioned regions (the temperate heartlands) are larger than those in the arid or arctic margins, which is both ecologically honest and narratively useful. Limitations: the letter inherits the model's constant uncertainties (the 25× could be 10–40× under the swept parameters), so it should be read as order-of-magnitude guidance; it does not address magic-level fire (a dragon's flame is far hotter than any real fire), so the wildfire claim is conservative; and it assumes the dragons are the only large predators in the landscape, ignoring competition that would further constrain them. The letter is honest about these: it frames each constraint as an order of magnitude, not a law.
