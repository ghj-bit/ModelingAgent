## Subtask 1: Model the energy expenditures and caloric intake requirements of the three dragons of Game of Thrones across their life 

### Problem

Model the energy expenditures and caloric intake requirements of the three dragons of Game of Thrones across their life cycle, from ~10 kg hatchlings to adult mass, in three climate regimes (arid, warm temperate, arctic). Goal: a daily energy-demand function of body mass, age, and climate that drives every downstream quantity (prey biomass, area, community size). Scope: the full growth trajectory (not a single adult snapshot), a per-activity energy budget, and a soft caloric constraint (growth modulated, maintenance protected).

### Analysis

The problem statement fixes only ~10 kg at hatch and ~30-40 kg at one year, and says dragons 'continue to grow throughout their life depending on the conditions and amount of food.' Per the expert consultation (Exchange 1), the analysis is anchored on the full growth trajectory with area and community support evaluated at each age; no terminal mass is licensed by the text, so the asymptote K is a declared assumption (1, 3, or 10 t swept). The caloric constraint is soft (Exchange 1): when intake is short, growth is modulated and maintenance is protected, so the required area is a variable, not a floor. The energy budget is split per-activity rather than using one lumped multiplier (Exchange 2's configuration was rejected): basal metabolic rate (Kleiber's law), sustained flight, foraging/search, and cold thermoregulation are separate terms, each scaled by body mass, with a regime-specific foraging-search factor for arid and arctic. Climate is a continuous temperature gradient sampled at the three named points (Exchange 1), so the arid-to-temperate-to-arctic movement is comparable along one axis. The Kleiber exponent (0.67 surface-area law, 0.75 metabolic, 1.0 linear) is treated as a sensitivity parameter, not a fixed truth, because the exponent choice shifts predicted metabolic rate by up to ~26% for a 100 kg animal (external data item 1).

### Modeling Process

Mass trajectory: logistic M(t) = K / (1 + ((K - M0)/M0) e^{-r t}), M0 = 10 kg at hatch, r (per year) fitted so M(2 yr) = 500 kg (a declared anchor consistent with the observed 10 -> 35 kg over the first year; r ~ 1.98-2.30 /yr depending on K). At K = 3000 kg the dragon is ~76 kg at 1 yr, ~500 kg at 2 yr, and near asymptote by 5 yr. Daily energy demand (kcal/day) for one dragon of mass M (kg) in a regime with mean temperature T (deg C): E(M, T) = [KLEIBER * M^k_exp + ACT_FLIGHT * M + ACT_FORAGE * M + THERMO_COST * M * max(0, T0 - T)] * SF * KCAL_PER_W_DAY, where KLEIBER = 3.4 W per kg^0.75 (k_exp swept over 0.67, 0.75, 1.0), ACT_FLIGHT = 0.15 W/kg, ACT_FORAGE = 0.05 W/kg, THERMO_COST = 0.08 W/kg per deg C below T0 = 30 deg C, KCAL_PER_W_DAY = 20.65, and SF is the foraging-search factor (arid 1.3, warm temperate 1.0, arctic 1.5). Regimes: arid T = -15 deg C, NPP = 0.45 kg C/m^2/yr; warm temperate T = 12 deg C, NPP = 1.20; arctic T = -10 deg C, NPP = 0.15. Caloric intake: the dragon must consume prey whose metabolizable energy equals E; prey biomass per day = E (kcal) * 4.184 (MJ/kcal) / (ETA_ASSIM * RHO), with ETA_ASSIM = 0.25 (assimilation efficiency) and RHO = 6.5 MJ/kg prey (carnivore diet of livestock-sized vertebrates, 200-500 kg). For three dragons at K = 3000 kg, warm temperate, age 30: E = 130,059 kcal/day, prey = 1,004,615 kg/day (~1,005 t/day).

### Outcome Analysis

Adult (age 30) daily energy demand at K = 3000 kg: 130,059 kcal/day in warm temperate, 343,033 kcal/day in arid (search + cold), 358,637 kcal/day in arctic (search + cold). The climate effect is dominated by the foraging-search factor (1.3-1.5x) and cold thermoregulation (0.08 W/kg per deg C below 30 deg C, i.e. 28-45 deg C below neutral in the cold regimes), not by a baseline metabolic difference: arctic demand is 2.7x warm-temperate. The Kleiber exponent is the largest single sensitivity: at K = 3000 kg, warm temperate, k_exp = 0.67 gives 116,598 kcal/day, k_exp = 0.75 gives 130,059, k_exp = 1.0 gives 312,229 — a 2.7x spread. The soft constraint means that if intake falls short, the dragon loses growth rather than maintenance; in the arctic at K = 3000 kg the required prey (2,770 t/day) far exceeds what a 67,409 km^2 arctic area can sustain at low NPP, so the soft constraint is the binding limit and the dragon would not reach full mass there without provisioning. Limitation: the per-activity coefficients (flight, forage, thermo) are assumed, not measured, and the 0.08 W/kg/deg C thermoregulation cost is the dominant climate term; a factor of 2 error there would shift the arctic/temperate ratio by ~40%.

### Problem

Estimate the area required to support the three dragons (their prey base and the primary production that supports the prey) as a function of age, climate regime, and provisioning fraction p in [0,1]. Goal: a hunting-area function that shows how the required territory scales with dragon mass, climate, and the level of human assistance. Scope: prey as livestock-sized herbivores, an explicit herbivore transfer factor as a sensitivity range (7:1, 10:1, 15:1), and regime-specific net primary productivity.

### Analysis

The area chain is: dragon energy demand -> prey biomass (kg/day) -> prey multiplied by the herbivore transfer factor (prey are herbivores, so the plant matter needed is prey biomass times the transfer) -> divided by the regime's net primary productivity (kg C/m^2/yr) to give area. The provisioning fraction p (Exchange 1: the assistance axis) removes the provisioned share of the chain from the wild area: area scales with (1 - p). The herbivore transfer is a sensitivity range, not a constant (Exchange 2's configuration was rejected), so 7:1, 10:1, and 15:1 are all reported. The area is a variable, not a floor (Exchange 1: soft constraint), because at low provisioning the dragon can reduce its wild intake by foraging in a larger area or by accepting slower growth. The primary production is the limiting resource: a dragon's area requirement is set by how much plant matter its prey chain must draw from the ground, and that is climate-dependent through NPP.

### Modeling Process

Area (km^2) = [prey_kg_day * (1 - p) * TROPHIC * 365] / NPP * 1e-6, where prey_kg_day = E (kcal) * 4.184 / (ETA_ASSIM * RHO) * 3 (three dragons), TROPHIC is the herbivore transfer factor (7, 10, or 15), NPP is the regime's net primary productivity (kg C/m^2/yr: arid 0.45, warm temperate 1.20, arctic 0.15), and the factor 365 converts daily to annual. At K = 3000 kg, k_exp = 0.75, TROPHIC = 10, age 30: warm temperate area = 3,056 km^2 (p = 0), 1,528 km^2 (p = 0.5), 30.6 km^2 (p = 0.99); arid area = 21,492 km^2 (p = 0); arctic area = 67,409 km^2 (p = 0). The area scales linearly with (1 - p) and inversely with NPP.

### Outcome Analysis

At adult (K = 3000 kg, warm temperate, TROPHIC = 10), the three dragons require 3,056 km^2 of warm-temperate range at zero provisioning — roughly the area of a small county — and this grows to 67,409 km^2 in the arctic (22x) because NPP is 0.15 vs 1.20 kg C/m^2/yr (an 8x NPP ratio) compounded by the 1.5x arctic search factor. The herbivore transfer is a direct linear sensitivity: TROPHIC = 7 gives 2,139 km^2, TROPHIC = 15 gives 4,584 km^2, a 2.1x spread around the 10:1 base. Provisioning reduces area linearly: p = 0.5 halves it, p = 0.99 reduces it to 1% of the p = 0 value. The arctic is the binding constraint: at K = 3000 kg the required arctic area (67,409 km^2) is larger than most national parks by an order of magnitude, and the soft constraint implies the dragon would not sustain full mass there without human provisioning. Limitation: NPP values are regime-averaged and do not capture seasonal variation, fire, or the fact that a dragon's hunting range is not a uniform grazing area but a corridor; the 1e-6 m^2-to-km^2 conversion assumes the prey chain's plant draw is spread uniformly over the area, which overstates the effective hunting territory for a mobile predator. The 365-day annualization ignores the growing season: in the arctic the effective NPP window is short, so the true required area is larger than the annual NPP estimate.

### Problem

Determine the size of the human community necessary to support the three dragons at varying levels of assistance (provisioning fraction p in [0,1]), as a function of dragon age and climate. Goal: a population function N(p, age, regime) that quantifies how much human agricultural output and land are needed to offset the dragons' caloric demand. Scope: the assistance axis as a provisioning fraction (Exchange 1), provisioning as a transfer with its own human cost (Exchange 3), and the community size computed per age and per p on the growth trajectory.

### Analysis

Per the expert consultation (Exchange 3), provisioning is a transfer, not a subtraction: the community itself must produce the provisioned share of the dragons' diet, and the humans who produce it must eat. If provisioning were modeled as pure subtraction from demand (N proportional to (1 - p)), then as p -> 1 the required human population would go to zero while the dragon's own demand is still rising — a multi-tonne growing animal 'supported by zero community,' which is non-physical and contradicts the soft-constraint reading. The corrected model gives the community a nonzero floor: the provisioned share is produced by humans at HUMAN_PROVISION_COST (3,500 kcal/human/day of their own food, which is what they keep), and the unprovisioned remainder's chain is supported at HUMAN_FOOD_SURPLUS (2,000 kcal/human/day). The humans keep only TROPHIC_EFF_PLANT_TO_PREY (0.1, the plant-to-prey efficiency) of the plant energy they grow as their own food, so the headcount per kcal of plant is 1 / (2,000 * 0.1) = 1/200, not 1/2,000. This floor is the model's central correction and is the boundary the expert identified as the failure mode of the naive (1 - p) model.

### Modeling Process

N(p) = N_provisioned + N_unprovisioned, where prey_kcal = prey_biomass_kg_day * RHO * 4.184 (kcal/day for three dragons), plant_kcal = prey_kcal * TROPHIC (kcal/day of plant matter), N_provisioned = plant_kcal * p / (HUMAN_PROVISION_COST * 0.1), N_unprovisioned = plant_kcal * (1 - p) / (HUMAN_FOOD_SURPLUS * 0.1). At K = 3000 kg, k_exp = 0.75, TROPHIC = 10, warm temperate, age 30: N(p = 0) = 455,359, N(p = 0.5) = 357,782, N(p = 0.9) = 286,560, N(p = 0.99) = 262,156. The floor as p -> 1 is N_provisioned = plant_kcal / (3,500 * 0.1) = plant_kcal / 350. In the arctic at the same age: N(p = 0) = 1,255,650, N(p = 0.99) = 722,896 — a floor of 723k humans, not zero. The community size at p = 0.99 is 57% of the p = 0 value, not 1%, because the provisioned share still requires its own producing population.

### Outcome Analysis

The community size is dominated by the dragon's prey chain, not by the dragons' own caloric demand: at K = 3000 kg the three dragons eat ~1,005 t of prey per day in warm temperate, and the human community that must produce that (at 0.1 plant-to-prey efficiency) is ~455,000 people at zero provisioning. Provisioning reduces the community but with a hard floor: at p = 0.99 the community is still 262,000 (warm temperate) or 723,000 (arctic), because the humans who feed the dragons must eat themselves. The arctic is the worst case: low NPP means a larger prey chain per unit area, and the community floor is 723k — a small city — to fully provision three adult dragons. The p -> 1, high-age, arctic corner is the model's weakest physical prediction (Exchange 3): the community floor is large but the area it would need to produce that share (at arctic NPP) is enormous, so the 'fully provisioned' scenario is physically strained even though it is not non-physical. Limitation: HUMAN_PROVISION_COST and HUMAN_FOOD_SURPLUS are assumed (3,500 and 2,000 kcal/human/day) and the 0.1 plant-to-prey efficiency is a single value; a realistic agricultural system has variable yield and waste, so the true community size is a range, not a point. The model does not account for the land the humans themselves need (HUMAN_LAND = 0.5 km^2/person was set but not used in the population calculation), so the area required for the provisioning community is understated; at 455,000 people that is an additional ~227,000 km^2 of human farmland, which dwarfs the dragon's hunting area.

## Subtask 4: Assess how climate conditions (arid, warm temperate, arctic) affect the resources required to maintain and grow the drag

### Problem

Assess how climate conditions (arid, warm temperate, arctic) affect the resources required to maintain and grow the dragons, and how important climate is to the analysis; then draft the letter to George R. R. Martin on maintaining the ecological underpinning of the story with respect to dragon movement between these regions. Goal: a climate-sensitivity result that quantifies the resource cost of moving a dragon between regimes and a narrative recommendation grounded in that result. Scope: the three named climates as points on a continuous temperature/NPP gradient (Exchange 1), the arid-to-temperate-to-arctic movement analysis, and the non-dragon situation the modeling might inform.

### Analysis

Climate enters the model in three ways: (1) the foraging-search factor (arid 1.3, arctic 1.5, warm temperate 1.0) captures the reduced prey density and longer travel in dry and cold regimes; (2) thermoregulation cost (0.08 W/kg per deg C below 30 deg C) captures the metabolic penalty of cold; (3) net primary productivity (arid 0.45, warm temperate 1.20, arctic 0.15 kg C/m^2/yr) sets the carrying capacity of the prey base. Per Exchange 1, the climate pass is a continuous gradient sampled at the three named points so that the movement analysis is comparable along one axis; a categorical three-regime comparison would not support a statement about what happens 'between' regimes. The movement analysis is therefore a comparison of the resource requirements (energy, prey, area, community) at the three points, with the understanding that any regime between them has requirements interpolated on the same gradient. The non-dragon situation the modeling informs is the ecology of large introduced or re-introduced carnivores (e.g. wolf or bear re-introduction programs, or the planning of reserves for megafauna), where the same energy-scaling, prey-chain, and area-requirement structure applies and where climate-driven NPP and prey density set the carrying capacity.

### Modeling Process

Climate sensitivity is computed as the ratio of resource requirements between regimes at fixed dragon mass (K = 3000 kg, age 30, k_exp = 0.75, TROPHIC = 10, p = 0): energy ratio arctic/temperate = 358,637 / 130,059 = 2.76, arid/temperate = 343,033 / 130,059 = 2.64; area ratio arctic/temperate = 67,409 / 3,056 = 22.1, arid/temperate = 21,492 / 3,056 = 7.0; community ratio arctic/temperate = 1,255,650 / 455,359 = 2.76, arid/temperate = 1,201,017 / 455,359 = 2.64. The area ratio is much larger than the energy ratio because NPP varies by 8x (temperate/arctic) while energy varies by 2.76x: the climate effect on area is dominated by the prey base, not by the dragon's own metabolism. Moving a dragon from temperate to arctic multiplies its required area by 22x and its supporting community by 2.8x; moving it from temperate to arid multiplies its required area by 7x. The letter to Martin is grounded in these ratios: a dragon that is ecologically sustainable in a warm temperate region (3,056 km^2, ~455,000-person community) requires 22x the area and 2.8x the community to be sustainable in an arctic region, and the arid region is intermediate in area (7x) but similar in community (2.6x).

### Outcome Analysis

Climate is highly important to the analysis: the arctic requires 22x the area of the warm temperate at the same dragon mass, driven entirely by the NPP ratio (0.15 vs 1.20 kg C/m^2/yr), while the dragon's own energy demand is only 2.76x higher. The practical implication for the story is that a dragon raised in a warm temperate region and moved to an arctic one would need a territory 22 times larger to sustain itself, and a human community 2.8 times larger to provision it; the arid region is less severe in area (7x) but still 2.6x in community. The movement arid-to-temperate-to-arctic is therefore not a neutral relocation: each step toward cold and low NPP multiplies the resource requirement, and the arctic is the limiting case where the soft constraint binds (the dragon cannot reach full mass without provisioning). For the letter to Martin: the ecological underpinning holds as long as the dragons' territories are scaled to the NPP of their climate — a dragon in a temperate region needs a territory on the order of a few thousand km^2, and a dragon moved to an arctic region needs a territory on the order of tens of thousands of km^2 and a correspondingly larger human support population; the story's realism requires that the dragons' home ranges grow with the harshness of the climate, not shrink. The non-dragon situation this informs is megafauna re-introduction and reserve planning: the same Kleiber-scaled energy demand, prey-chain transfer, and NPP-limited area structure applies to large carnivores (wolves, bears, big cats), and the climate-driven NPP sensitivity is the same factor that determines whether a re-introduced population can be sustained in a given region — a result directly transferable to conservation planning for large predators in climate-vulnerable landscapes. Limitation: the NPP values are regime-averaged and do not capture seasonal or inter-annual variation, and the 22x arctic area ratio is an upper bound that assumes the dragon's prey chain draws the full annual NPP of the region; a real ecosystem has a carrying-capacity limit below total NPP, so the true arctic area requirement is larger still. The letter's recommendation (scale territory to climate NPP) is robust to the parameter uncertainties because the NPP ratio, not the absolute NPP, drives the result.
