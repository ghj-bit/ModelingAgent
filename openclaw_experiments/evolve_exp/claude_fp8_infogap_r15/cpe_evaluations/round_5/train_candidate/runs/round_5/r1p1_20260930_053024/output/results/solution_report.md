## Subtask 1: Part 1. Using the NFLIS data, build a mathematical model describing the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic

### Problem

Part 1. Using the NFLIS data, build a mathematical model describing the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid (fentanyl-family) and heroin identification counts in and between five states (OH, KY, WV, VA, PA) and their counties, 2010-2017. Identify where each substance likely started in each state; name drug-identification threshold levels the U.S. government should worry about; and predict where and when the model says those thresholds will be crossed.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) Per the expert consultation, the observed identification count E[c,t] is taken as the direct state variable of a spatial birth-death epidemic on the county graph (the latent-prevalence layer was rejected in Exchange 2 and its unidentifiability flagged in Exchange 1); E therefore measures lab-processed law-enforcement submissions filtered through an underlying market, not raw prevalence. (2) No GIS coordinates are supplied, so within-state adjacency is the fully-connected same-state channel (uniform over same-state peers) and the between-state channel runs through the state aggregate; this is the minimal structure the data supports. (3) 'Started in' a state is read as the set of counties with the largest 2010 counts (seed detection), because fentanyl identifications are already present in every state in 2010 at low levels, so ignition is a matter of where the mass first concentrated, not a single zero-to-nonzero event. (4) The alert threshold is population-normalized (per 1000 households, since DP02 has no population field, total households is the size proxy) exceeding k times the state median, with k swept over {1.5, 2.0, 3.0}; absolute-count percentiles are reported as secondary. Method: a two-channel spatial birth-death model E_hat[i,t+1] = (1-gamma) E_hat[i,t] + beta_w sum_j A[i,j] E_hat[j,t] S[i,t] + beta_b H_state[i,t], with susceptibility S and state aggregate H_state, fit by grid search over beta_w, beta_b, gamma minimizing 2014-2017 RMSE. This method is sound for the slow phase but, as the fit shows, it is the wrong mechanism for the 2014-2017 fentanyl explosion (see outcome).

### Modeling Process

State variable E[c,t] (observed count). Two spreading channels: within-state A[i,j]=1/(k_s-1) for same-state peers i!=j (k_s = #counties in state), else 0; between-state H_state[i,t]=mean over state members. Update: E_hat[i,t+1]=max(0,(1-gamma)E_hat[i,t]+beta_w(A E_hat[:,t])[i]*S[i,t]+beta_b H_state[i,t]); S[i,t]=clip(1-E_hat[i,t]/max_2010,0.2,1.0). Fit by grid search beta_w in [0.05,0.5] (9), beta_b in [0,0.2] (5), gamma in {0.3,0.5,0.7}, minimizing 2014-2017 RMSE. Synthetic best: beta_w=0.5, beta_b=0.2, gamma=0.3 (RMSE 210.6 on 2014-2017; per-year RMSE 0.5,1.4,1.6,2.8 for 2010-2013 then 22.5,85.9,183.4,368.6 for 2014-2017). Heroin best: beta_w=0.163, beta_b=0.2, gamma=0.3 (RMSE 348.9; per-year 0.5,84,196,266,316,373,378,324). Seed detection: 2010 top-5 counties per state by count. Threshold: rate[r,t]=E[r,t]/(hh_r/1000); alert if rate[r,t]>k*median_state(rate[:,t]).

### Outcome Analysis

Where it started (2010 seed counties, top by count): KY - Campbell, Russell, Jefferson, Carlisle, Logan (synthetic) / Kenton, Jefferson, Campbell, Boone, Fayette (heroin); OH - Hamilton, Van Wert, Shelby, Summit, Highland (synthetic) / Hamilton, Montgomery, Delaware, Cuyahoga, Mahoning (heroin); PA - Westmoreland, Luzerne, Philadelphia, Allegheny, McKean (synthetic) / Philadelphia, Allegheny, Bucks, Lackawanna, Luzerne (heroin); VA - Wise, Stafford, Roanoke, Bath, Alleghany (synthetic) / Richmond, Portsmouth City, Norfolk City, Roanoke, Henrico (heroin); WV - Upspur, Ohio, Roane, Boone, Marshall (synthetic) / Monongalia, Berkeley, Marion, Hancock, Kanawha (heroin). The heroin seeds are stable, urban, and persistent (Philadelphia, Hamilton/Cincinnati, the KY-Illinois border Kenton/Jefferson, the WV-OH Monongahela Valley) - a diffuse, already-mature 2010 market. The synthetic (fentanyl) seeds are different: OH Hamilton (Cincinnati) and the PA western counties (Westmoreland, Luzerne) are the early fentanyl concentration points, and OH emerges as the dominant fentanyl epicenter (2017 top rates: Hamilton 14.4/1000 hh, Lake 9.0, Montgomery 7.8, Cuyahoga 5.7). Thresholds and their dynamics: at k=3.0 the number of alert counties (per-1000-hh rate > 3x state median) is small and concentrated (e.g. KY 13, OH 27, PA 15, VA 20, WV 2 in 2017), while at k=1.5 it is broad (KY 34, OH 60, PA 26, VA 38, WV 10). The government concern is the synthetic series: state-level fentanyl multiplies 2014->2017 by KY x11.7, OH x13.1, PA x22.7, VA x12.0, WV x6.2, and the alert-county set for fentanyl is expanding, whereas the heroin alert set is flat-to-shrinking (heroin peaks 2015 then declines: 51,074 in 2015 to 36,152 in 2017). Prediction: extrapolating the fitted fentanyl growth, every state's k=1.5 alert set is still growing through 2017 and is projected to keep widening into the mid-2020s; the k=3.0 'severe' set is where intervention should focus (the top-decile high-rate counties, dominated by OH). Key limitation/bias: the linear birth-death mechanism fits 2010-2013 well (RMSE <3) but diverges badly on 2014-2017 (RMSE to 369 for fentanyl) - the fentanyl spread is a regime shift / phase transition (supply-side flooding of the illicit market), not a smooth spatial diffusion, so the model under-predicts the explosion and the 'started-in' inference is most reliable for the slow heroin phase. The per-1000-household threshold is also biased by the expert-flagged case: a county whose lab submissions route to a neighbor's lab, or whose ACS household count is a 5-year estimate centered on a different year, can cross the alert line from a reporting-boundary or denominator artifact rather than a real market change.

### Problem

Part 2. Using the U.S. Census socio-economic data provided, test whether opioid use or trends-in-use is associated with the ACS variables, and modify the Part 1 model to include any important factors.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) The provided ACS files are DP02 (households, demographic, educational-attainment), which do NOT contain poverty, unemployment, or income (those are in DP03/DP05 and are not provided); the only usable socioeconomic fields are total households (size proxy) and educational attainment for population 25+ (bach25, hs25, assoc25, nosch25). (2) County-year counts are modeled as a linear function of these covariates with state and year fixed effects; the ACS covariate is held at the 2014 5-year estimate (mid-window, since no 2017 ACS is provided) and merged to the NFLIS county FIPS (exact join, 461/461 counties matched). (3) The ASPE prior (higher poverty/unemployment -> higher opioid measures) is used as the a-priori sign expectation, but because poverty/unemployment are absent from DP02, the test is run on the education variables that are present. Method: OLS of log(1+count) and raw count on the five covariates plus state and year dummies, reporting standardized coefficients so direction and relative strength are readable across scales. This is sound because it isolates the within-state, cross-county variation (state/year dummies absorb state-level and national-trend confounds) and the standardized coefficients make the education effects comparable to each other.

### Modeling Process

Model: y[c,t] = beta0 + sum_j beta_j x_j[c] + sum_state gamma_s 1{state=s} + sum_year delta_t 1{year=t} + eps, with y = log1p(count) (primary) and y = count (robustness); x = {hh_total, bach25, hs25, assoc25, nosch25}. Solved by least squares (np.linalg.lstsq), standard errors from sigma^2 (X'X)^-1. Synthetic (n=1514 county-years): R^2=0.535; standardized coefficients hh_total +0.30 (t=12.8, strongly positive - larger counties report more, a size effect), nosch25 -0.15 (t=-4.6), hs25 -0.14 (t=-2.4), bach25 +0.006 (t=0.11, null), assoc25 +0.014 (t=0.62, null). Raw-count standardized: hh_total +0.43, bach25 -0.11, hs25 -0.05, assoc25 +0.01, nosch25 -0.04. Heroin: hh_total +0.86 (dominant size effect), bach25 -0.21, nosch25 -0.06, hs25 -0.02, assoc25 -0.02. Modification of Part 1: the size effect (hh_total) is folded into the threshold as the per-1000-households rate (already done in Part 1), and the education effect is added as a county-level offset to the alert score: a county with a higher 'less-than-9th-grade' or lower HS-graduation share gets a small upward nudge to its alert probability, consistent with the (negative) education coefficients.

### Outcome Analysis

The dominant, robust association is county size (total households): larger counties have far more identifications (standardized +0.30 log, +0.43 to +0.86 raw), which is why the per-1000-household normalization in Part 1 is essential - without it the 'alert' map is just a map of big cities. Among the education variables, the association is present but modest and, in the log model, negative: counties with a higher share of adults without a high-school diploma (nosch25) or who are HS graduates (hs25) show slightly LOWER log-identifications, while the raw-count model shows the expected negative sign for bach25 (-0.11) and nosch25 (-0.04). This is the opposite sign from the ASPE poverty prior, but it is an education-specific result and is confounded with size and urbanicity (rural, low-education counties have smaller labs and fewer submissions, which the data cannot separate from a true prevalence effect - the exact lab-routing failure mode flagged by the expert in Exchange 3). Interpretation: within this data, use is concentrated in large, urban, higher-education-attainment counties (the fentanyl epicenter OH Hamilton/Cincinnati is a large, relatively educated metro), and the education signal is weak and sign-ambiguous. Limitation/bias: DP02 lacks the poverty/unemployment/income variables the hypotheses actually implicate, so the test cannot confirm the ASPE economic-driver prior; the education coefficients are likely capturing urbanicity/laboratory-coverage rather than a causal education->opioid link, and the 2014-centered ACS estimate introduces the denominator-year mismatch the expert warned about. The honest conclusion is that the provided socio-economic data show a strong size association and a weak, sign-ambiguous education association, and that the key economic drivers (poverty, unemployment) are not in the supplied tables.

## Subtask 3: Part 3. Using a combination of Part 1 and Part 2 results, identify a possible strategy for countering the opioid crisis;

### Problem

Part 3. Using a combination of Part 1 and Part 2 results, identify a possible strategy for countering the opioid crisis; test its effectiveness with the model; identify the significant parameter bounds on which success (or failure) depends. Also produce a 1-2 page memo to the Chief Administrator, DEA/NFLIS, summarizing significant insights.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) The strategy is a targeted supply+demand intervention applied to the top-decile high-rate ('alert') counties from Part 1: (i) suppress within-state spread in those counties (law-enforcement interdiction, lab-throughput / source disruption) by a fraction reduction_w of beta_w, and (ii) reduce local retention/growth (treatment, naloxone, prescription controls) by a fraction reduction_d of the local growth rate g. (2) The epidemic is projected 2018-2025 from the 2017 state; the per-county growth g is fit from the 2015-2017 ratio, capped and dampened (x0.7) to represent expected post-2017 moderation, and a soft retention ceiling (counts may rise at most 1.6x year-over-year) represents lab-throughput saturation. (3) Success is defined as a cumulative reduction of at least `target` (default 0.3, 30%) of the no-intervention 2018-2025 total identification count. Method: simulate baseline vs. intervention on the fitted fentanyl growth, sweep reduction_w and reduction_d over [0,0.9] (10x10), record the cumulative reduction and the success region, and report the critical values (for each reduction_d the minimum reduction_w that succeeds, and vice versa). This is sound because it isolates the two levers of the strategy and finds the boundary of the success region rather than a single point estimate.

### Modeling Process

Growth g[c]=clip((E[c,2017]/max(E[c,2015],1))^(1/2)-1, -0.3, 1.2), smoothed 50/50 toward the state mean, then x0.7 (moderation). beta_w=0.15, beta_b=0.05. Projection for t=2018..2025: E_hat[c,t]=(1+g_t[c]) E_hat[c,t-1]+beta_w*(A E_hat[:,t-1])[c]*(1 - E_hat[c,t-1]/max_2017), with, in alert counties from intervention_t0: g_t[c]*=(1-reduction_d) and the inflow term *(1-reduction_w); all counts capped at 1.6x previous. Baseline total (no intervention) 2018-2025 for synthetic = 3.46M; alert counties = 41 (top 10% by 2017 per-1000-hh rate). Reduction = (baseline - intervened)/baseline. Grid sweep over reduction_w x reduction_d.

### Outcome Analysis

For synthetic opioids (the active crisis), the strategy is effective and has a clear success region: the maximum achievable cumulative reduction over 2018-2025 is 0.568 (at the strongest combined lever), and at a moderate combined effort (reduction_w=0.5, reduction_d=0.5) it achieves a 0.40 reduction - well above the 0.30 success target. The parameter bounds: success (>=30% reduction) requires a demand-side component - reduction_d must reach about 0.4 before any supply-side reduction_w alone can tip the outcome (for reduction_d=0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 no value of reduction_w achieves the target; at reduction_d>=0.4 even reduction_w=0.0 succeeds). In other words, the binding constraint is the local growth/retention lever (treatment + prescription controls), not interdiction alone: cutting the spread into alert counties (reduction_w) matters, but it cannot by itself stop the local epidemic. For heroin the same strategy is largely ineffective (max achievable reduction 0.057, and at 0.5/0.5 a -0.0003, i.e. essentially no effect) because the heroin market is already mature and its alert set is flat-to-shrinking - the crisis that needs countering is the fentanyl one, consistent with Part 1. Significance of the bounds: (a) the intervention must start by 2018 (intervention_t0) to capture the full 2018-2025 window; (b) it must be applied to the top-decile high-rate counties (alert_frac=0.1, ~41 counties), not spread thinly; (c) the demand-side lever (reduction_d) has a threshold near 0.4 below which the strategy fails regardless of supply-side effort. Limitation/bias: the projection inherits the Part 1 growth fit (dampened from the 2015-2017 ratio) and the 1.6x retention ceiling, both of which are assumptions, not data; the strategy is judged on identification-count reduction, which the expert flagged can move for lab-routing reasons unrelated to the true market, so a real evaluation would need a prevalence or mortality outcome (the external CDC context - 70,237 drug-overdose deaths in 2017, ~68% opioid, driven by synthetic opioids - is the directional sanity check that the fentanyl-focused strategy targets the right substance). Memo to the Chief Administrator, DEA/NFLIS: (1) The data show a regime shift, not a gradual spread: synthetic-opioid (fentanyl) identifications multiply 6-23x from 2014 to 2017 across all five states (PA x22.7, OH x13.1, KY x11.7, VA x12.0, WV x6.2), while heroin peaks in 2015 and declines by 2017. (2) Ohio is the fentanyl epicenter - Hamilton (Cincinnati), Cuyahoga (Cleveland), Montgomery (Dayton), and Lake counties carry the highest per-1000-household rates in 2017 - and the fentanyl alert set is still expanding, so without action the high-rate county count is projected to keep widening through the mid-2020s. (3) Heroin is a mature, urban, largely stable market (Philadelphia, Cincinnati, the KY-Illinois border, the Monongahela Valley); it is not the growth front. (4) County size dominates the identification count (the per-1000-household normalization is essential), and the supplied census tables (DP02) carry no poverty/unemployment/income fields, so the economic drivers of the crisis cannot be quantified from the provided data - we recommend adding DP03/DP05 to any follow-up. (5) A targeted strategy - combining interdiction in the top-decile high-rate counties with treatment/prescription controls - is modeled to cut 2018-2025 fentanyl identifications by ~40% at a moderate effort, but it fails if the demand-side (treatment) lever is below roughly 40% effectiveness; interdiction alone is insufficient. Prioritize the demand-side lever and start immediately in the Ohio and western-PA fentanyl clusters. (6) Caveat: identification counts are a lab-processing proxy and can move with reporting boundaries and ACS denominator revisions, so threshold alerts should be confirmed against prevalence or mortality data before resource commitment.
