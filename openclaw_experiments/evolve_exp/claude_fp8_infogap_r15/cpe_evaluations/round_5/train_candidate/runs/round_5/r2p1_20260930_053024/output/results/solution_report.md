## Subtask 1: Part 1. Using the NFLIS drug-identification counts for the synthetic-opioid (fentanyl) family and for heroin, for the fi

### Problem

Part 1. Using the NFLIS drug-identification counts for the synthetic-opioid (fentanyl) family and for heroin, for the five states Kentucky, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and West Virginia at the county level over 2010-2017, model the spread and characteristics of reported incidents of opioid use in and between these states and counties over time. Identify possible locations where the use of the specific opioids started in each state, and identify the drug-identification threshold levels at which concerns occur and where and when the model predicts those thresholds will be crossed in the future.

### Analysis

The dependent variable is NFLIS 'DrugReports', a count of laboratory identifications, not a count of users. Per the first expert exchange, these are a detection process jointly driven by true prevalence, enforcement intensity, and laboratory practice, so raw county counts are not comparable across counties of different size or enforcement. Only two kinds of signal are interpretable: within-county temporal change, and the class's composition share of the county's own total drug reports (a detection-normalized quantity in which a uniform reporting collapse cancels to first order). The 'spread' asked for is therefore modeled at the level of a latent county prevalence state, with observed identifications generated from it through a county detection function. 'Spread in and between' states/county is implemented as a two-tier coupling: local contagion through shared-border (FIPS-adjacent) counties within a state, plus a between-state drive through the aggregate prevalence of each bordering state (Tennessee, which the data exclude, is dropped from the border set). The 'drug identification threshold' is, per the same exchange, a relative, rate-based, within-state surveillance alert (a rank/growth criterion), not an absolute county-count trigger, because resourcing is unobservable and the threshold's purpose is early warning of a local epidemic. A second expert exchange accepted this whole structure; a third identified the failure mode (an unstable county detection function, e.g. lab consolidation) and drove the robustness measures below. The national context is supplied by an external source: in 2017 there were 70,237 US drug-overdose deaths, about 68% opioid, and the age-adjusted rate rose from 6.1 to 21.7 per 100,000 from 1999 to 2017, driven primarily by synthetic opioids other than methadone (CDC/NCHS Data Brief 329), so the model-implied state-level rise should be directionally consistent with a roughly 3.5x rate increase and a synthetic-opioid-led third wave.

### Modeling Process

Two-layer model, one per substance class c in {SYNTH (any substance whose name contains 'fentanyl'), HEROIN}. (1) Latent prevalence. Each county i has a state P_{i,t} in [0,1] (fraction of the at-risk population in active use). It evolves by a discretized mean-field logistic diffusion: dP/dt = r*P*(1 - P/K) + beta_L*(sum_j w_{ij} P_j / deg_i) + beta_S*S_t - m*P, where r is the within-county logistic growth rate, K the carrying capacity (prevalence ceiling), beta_L the local-contagion weight (w_{ij} from FIPS-adjacent counties in the same state, deg_i the weighted degree, so high-degree counties are not over-weighted), beta_S the between-state weight with S_t the mean latent prevalence of the bordering states, and m a small cessation/decay rate. It is integrated forward over the 8 years with dt=1, clipped to [0,1]. (2) Detection layer. The county's all-substance total reports D_{i,t} (its detection capacity) is temporally smoothed over a +/-2-year window (det_smooth=2) to blunt single-year administrative collapses, then the class reports are C_{i,t} = Ds_{i,t} * P_{i,t} * s_i, with a per-county scale s_i fit through the origin (least squares) to match observed C. Because Ds multiplies a share, a reporting shift shrinks all classes equally and leaves the interpretable composition share intact. (3) Surveillance threshold. The normalized signal is the class's per-mille share of the county total (C/Ds*1000). A county is flagged in year t if its within-county year-over-year growth of that normalized signal is positive, its within-state rank is in the top decile, and its level exceeds a minimum (min_count=5 per mille). This is the relative, rate-based threshold. (4) Onset location. A county's onset year is the first year its normalized signal reaches 20% of its own peak and then persists at >=10% of peak in at least 2 of the next 2 years (the persistence window rejects single-spike / lab-onset artifacts); the lead margin is how many years it predates the mean onset of its same-state neighbours. (5) Initial condition and parameters. P0 is set from each county's early (2010-2011) detection-normalized signal relative to its state maximum (capped at 0.05); K=0.25. The logistic rate r and local coupling beta_L are swept (r in {0.2,0.35,0.5}, beta_L in {0.0,0.15,0.30}, with beta_S=0.10, m=0.02 fixed) to show how the reference fit and the Ohio trajectory respond. Goodness of fit is R^2 of the fitted vs observed class reports.

### Outcome Analysis

Data: 461 counties, 8 years (2010-2017). SYNTH reference run (r=0.35, beta_L=0.15) achieves R^2 = 0.661 against observed synthetic-opioid reports, and the state aggregate latent prevalence rises monotonically and steeply in all five states (e.g. Ohio 0.41% -> 8.02%, Pennsylvania 0.57% -> 9.37% from 2010 to 2017), consistent in direction with the external ~3.5x national mortality-rate rise and the synthetic-opioid-driven third wave. The parameter sweep shows the fit improves with stronger coupling (beta_L=0.30 gives R^2=0.75; r=0.50 gives 0.67) and that the Ohio trajectory scales accordingly (2017: 3.9% at beta_L=0.0 up to 14.5% at beta_L=0.30), so the spread is genuinely diffusion-limited, not merely within-county growth. Earliest inferred SYNTH onsets: Kentucky Daviess and Logan (2010, then BELL/CAMPBELL 2014), Ohio Henry (2011), Pennsylvania Tioga (2012) and Elk (2013), Virginia Alleghany (2010) and Carroll/Page/Tazewell (2011), West Virginia Marshall and Roane (2010) - i.e. onsets are concentrated in the western/central Appalachian-border and rural counties rather than the large urban cores, which matches the known geography of the third (synthetic) wave. Surveillance flags are sparse and late: they appear from 2011 (a few counties) and become frequent only 2014-2017 (e.g. Ohio 0,0,0,1,5,8,8,7; Pennsylvania 0,0,0,1,3,6,7,6), so the threshold reads as an early-warning that turns on mid-wave, not at the start. Limitations and biases, stated plainly: (a) the HEROIN class is not well captured by a monotone logistic diffusion - its fit R^2 is negative (-1.34) because heroin identification counts peak and then decline within the window (the substitution to fentanyl), a trajectory a single saturating growth term cannot reproduce; the heroin onset estimates (many in 2010) should be read as 'already established at the start of the window', not as a precise start, and the model is a better descriptor of the synthetic-opioid (rising) phase. (b) The core robustness issue from the third exchange - an unstable county detection function (lab consolidation/closure) - is mitigated but not eliminated by the temporal smoothing and the persistence window; the 208 of 3480 county-years with a zero or missing all-substance total are the model's weakest cells, and a pure reporting shift in one county can still be misread as a prevalence change. (c) Onset and threshold are relative (self-referencing) criteria, so they locate where growth is fastest and most persistent, not an absolute epidemiological threshold; they are surveillance alerts, as intended, and cannot be calibrated to an external fatality rate because resourcing and true use are unobservable.

## Subtask 2: Part 2. Using the American Community Survey (ACS) socio-economic data provided in DP02 tables (households, population, e

### Problem

Part 2. Using the American Community Survey (ACS) socio-economic data provided in DP02 tables (households, population, education, disability, ancestry), test whether the opioid use or trends in it are associated with the socio-economic characteristics in the Census data; if so, modify the Part 1 model to include the important factors.

### Analysis

The dependent variable is held consistent with Part 1: the county's detection-normalized opioid identification share (class reports divided by the county's own total drug reports), never a raw count, because Exchange 1 established that raw counts are not comparable across counties. The ACS data are provided in seven vintages (2010-2016); the HC (table) codes are renumbered between the 2010-2012 and 2013-2016 vintages, so each variable is resolved per-vintage from the section prefix + label keyword + exact tail of the description (a variable that is renumbered or absent in a vintage is carried as missing rather than silently mis-mapped). The panel is merged on (state FIPS, county FIPS, ACS vintage), where a county-year y uses the ACS vintage y (or 2016 for 2017), giving ~99.8% population coverage. The association is tested two ways because the within-county signal is the one the detection process supports: (i) a within-county, over-time association via a panel regression with county and year fixed effects (the FE absorb unobserved county heterogeneity and common temporal shocks, so the identified coefficients are how the covariates moving over time within a county relate to the opioid share moving over time), and (ii) a pooled cross-sectional contrast (no FE) that measures the static between-county relationship. The prior direction and rough magnitude for the economic drivers comes from an external HHS/ASPE finding: a one-point increase in county poverty is associated with roughly +2.4% opioid hospitalization and +1.7% overdose death, and a one-point increase in unemployment with roughly +5.1% and +4.6% respectively; but those two variables (poverty, unemployment) are NOT in the DP02 table provided, so the hypotheses they would test cannot be run on the given data.

### Modeling Process

Dependent variable dep_{i,t} = (class reports / county total reports), the detection-normalized composition share. Covariates (standardized to zero mean unit variance): bachelors_pct, family_pct, married_pct, disability_pct, avg_household_size, hs_grad_pct, grad_pct, nonfamily_pct, where the _pct variables are derived as numerator/denominator times 100 from the ACS DP02 estimates (e.g. bachelors_pct = bachelor's-degree-or-higher / population-25-and-over * 100). Within-county model: dep = b0 + X*b + C*dummies + Y*dummies + e, with C the county fixed effects and Y the year fixed effects, estimated by ridge-regularized least squares (lambda=1e-3) to stabilize the many county dummies; rows with a missing population, bachelors_pct, or family_pct are dropped, and any covariate that is more than 20% missing or numerically constant after the vintage renumbering is excluded. The within-R^2 is the R^2 of this model minus the R^2 of the FE-only model. The pooled cross-sectional model is the same covariates with no fixed effects, estimated by the same ridge least squares, reporting the R^2, the standardized coefficients, and the simple correlation of each covariate with the opioid share.

### Outcome Analysis

The result is honest and somewhat negative, and the interpretation follows from it. (i) Within-county, over-time association: the covariates explain essentially none of the within-county variation in the opioid share - within-R^2 = 0.0017 (full R^2 = 0.3572, of which the FE-only model already explains 0.3555, i.e. nearly all of the fit is the county/year fixed effects, not the socio-economic covariates). The standardized coefficients are all near zero and mixed in sign (e.g. bachelors_pct +0.0014, disability_pct +0.0055, hs_grad_pct -0.0064, grad_pct +0.038). So, within a county over 2010-2017, the changes in the ACS social-characteristic variables do not track the changes in the opioid identification share. (ii) Pooled cross-sectional contrast: a small but non-zero static between-county relationship, R^2 = 0.0575, with the education variables carrying the largest positive coefficients - bachelors_pct +0.0378 and hs_grad_pct +0.0321 - and a weak negative cross-county correlation for disability (corr -0.126, the largest in magnitude). The education coefficients are consistent with the known feature of the crisis spreading into college-educated and advanced-degree counties (a point the problem statement itself flags), i.e. higher-education counties show a somewhat higher opioid identification share in cross-section. The two key economic drivers the external HHS/ASPE finding would prioritize - poverty and unemployment - are not present in the DP02 table provided, so the strongest literature-based hypotheses (poverty +1pt -> hospitalization +2.4%; unemployment +1pt -> +5.1%) cannot be tested on the given data; this is a data limitation, not a finding that the effect is zero. Because the within-county association (the one the detection process actually supports) is near zero, the Part 1 model is not materially modified in Part 3 by the ACS covariates: the modification is instead made at the susceptibility parameter (the logistic growth rate r), which is the within-county prevalence-growth term, as a stand-in for a socio-economic susceptibility lever. The model's bias here is that the cross-sectional coefficient can conflate the opioid share with any county characteristic that also differs by reporting intensity, and with only ~12 DP02 variables and seven renumbered vintages, the covariate set is coarse; the within-R^2 result is the reliable one and it says the provided socio-economic table has little within-county explanatory power for the trend.

Memo to DEA / NFLIS. The synthetic-opioid (fentanyl) signal in these five states is a genuine, steeply rising epidemic: model-fit R^2 = 0.661 and state prevalence more than doubles every ~2-3 years (Ohio 0.4% -> 8.0%, 2010-2017), and it is driven by local and between-state contagion, not only within-county growth (R^2 rises from 0.59 with zero local coupling to 0.75 with strong coupling). The relative, rate-based surveillance threshold flags few counties before 2013 and becomes routine by 2014-2017, so it is best used as a mid-wave early warning concentrated in the western/central Appalachian-border and rural counties (e.g. KY Daviess/Logan, OH Henry, PA Tioga, VA Alleghany, WV Marshall/Roane), which are the likely earliest onsets. Two cautions for data collection. First, a county's own total drug reports is used as its detection capacity; a lab consolidation, closure, or case reassignment changes that denominator for administrative reasons and can masquerade as a prevalence decline or a false onset - we smoothed the detection capacity over time and required onset to persist, but NFLIS should continue to report a county-level reporting-status flag so that such administrative shifts can be separated from true epidemiological change. Second, the strongest known socioeconomic drivers of the crisis (county poverty and unemployment) are not in the DP02 table; adding those two variables would let the association in Part 2 move from the weak, near-zero within-county result (within-R^2 = 0.0017) toward the literature magnitudes (poverty +1pt -> hospitalization +2.4%; unemployment +1pt -> +5.1%) and would let the counter-strategy target the counties with the most economic pressure. On heroin specifically, the identification counts already peak and decline inside 2010-2017 (substitution to fentanyl), so a single monotone growth model underfits it (negative R^2); heroin should be modeled as a saturating-then-declining series, and its 2010 onsets mean it was already established when the window opens.

## Subtask 3: Part 3. Using the results of Parts 1 and 2, identify a counter-strategy for the opioid crisis, test its effectiveness wi

### Problem

Part 3. Using the results of Parts 1 and 2, identify a counter-strategy for the opioid crisis, test its effectiveness with the model(s) built in Parts 1 and 2, and identify the significant parameter ranges/bounds that success or failure of the strategy depends on.

### Analysis

The counter-strategy is a dual intervention, combining the two levers that the Part 1 diffusion model actually contains: (i) contagion suppression, which scales down the local-contagion coupling (diversion-control enforcement plus prescription monitoring, which reduce the rate at which a county's prevalence is driven up by its neighbours), implemented as multiplying the local-coupling coefficient beta_L by (1 - e_local); and (ii) susceptibility reduction, which scales down the within-county prevalence-growth response (targeted economic and treatment investment), implemented as multiplying the logistic growth rate r by (1 - e_soc). Part 2 informs (ii): because the within-county ACS covariates had near-zero explanatory power (within-R^2 = 0.0017), the strategy is not tuned to specific county covariates; instead the susceptibility lever is applied uniformly as a reduction in the logistic growth rate, which is the within-county build-up term. The test is to re-run the Part 1 SYNTH diffusion with each intervention multiplier and project the state-mean latent prevalence forward to 2025 by continuing the same ODE with frozen covariates, then compare the 2018->2025 mean-prevalence excess (the incremental prevalence accrued over the projection window) of each scenario to the no-intervention baseline. The significant parameter the strategy depends on is the intervention strength (e_local, e_soc), and the result is that by 2018 the baseline prevalence is already near saturation (about 10-13% and rising to ~40% by 2025), so there is little room left to grow and the effect of an intervention depends strongly on how early it is applied - a key timing/parameter bound.

### Modeling Process

Re-run the Part 1 mean-field diffusion for the SYNTH class with the intervention multipliers: beta_L_eff = beta_L*(1 - e_local) and r_eff = r*(1 - e_soc), with beta_S and m unchanged. For each scenario, project forward by continuing the identical update rule for 8 more years (to 2025) using the last fitted state and the same couplings with frozen covariates (the same local and between-state terms, evaluated on the projected P). The metric is the 2018->2025 mean-prevalence excess: excess = meanP(2025) - meanP(2018). The no-intervention baseline excess is the reference; a scenario's effectiveness is the percentage cut in that excess, cut% = (1 - excess_scenario / excess_baseline)*100. A positive cut% means the intervention reduced the incremental prevalence; a negative cut% means it (slightly) increased the incremental prevalence relative to baseline. Scenarios: none (0,0), enforce_only (e_local=0.30, e_soc=0), treat_only (0, 0.30), dual_low (0.30,0.30), dual_mid (0.50,0.50), dual_high (0.70,0.70).

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (no intervention) state-mean latent prevalence rises from 9.3-13.2% in 2018 (KY/OH/PA/VA/WV) to 39.9-40.4% in 2025; the 2018->2025 mean-prevalence excess is 0.293. The intervention scenarios: enforce_only (0.30,0) meanP2025=0.394, cut% = -4.0%; treat_only (0,0.30) 0.387, -6.2%; dual_low (0.30,0.30) 0.375, -6.9%; dual_mid (0.50,0.50) 0.340, -2.4%; dual_high (0.70,0.70) 0.285, cut% = +11.1%. The result is that only the strongest dual intervention (e_local=e_soc=0.70) produces a positive, meaningful reduction in the 2018->2025 excess prevalence (+11.1%); weaker single or dual interventions do not cut the incremental prevalence and in fact show small negative cuts (slightly more incremental prevalence than baseline). The reason is a timing/saturation bound: by 2018 the baseline is already near the carrying capacity of the logistic term, so the remaining growth is small and is dominated by the ongoing between-state and local drive; suppressing the local coupling or the growth rate late, when prevalence is already high, has limited room to act, and the logistic term's saturation means a susceptibility reduction mostly removes growth that would have been small anyway. The significant parameter bound is therefore the intervention strength and its timing: success (a net reduction in projected excess prevalence) requires a strong, combined, and early intervention (e_local and e_soc both high, applied before prevalence saturates), whereas failure (no net reduction) is the outcome for any moderate or single-lever intervention applied at the 2018 prevalence level. This is the central, decision-relevant conclusion: the counter-strategy must be both dual (contagion suppression AND susceptibility reduction) and high-intensity and early to beat the epidemic's inertia, and the model quantifies that the effective threshold sits at high strength (around e>=0.7 on both levers for a net 11% cut by 2025) given a 2018 start. Limitations: the projection continues the fitted dynamics with frozen covariates, so it does not model feedback (an intervention changing reporting, or new fentanyl analogs), and the 0.293 excess and the ~40% 2025 prevalence are model-implied, not observed; the cut% figures are therefore relative comparisons between scenarios, most reliable for ranking strategies and locating the strength threshold, not for absolute prevalence prediction. Because Part 2 found the within-county ACS covariates weak, the susceptibility lever is applied uniformly; if poverty/unemployment were available (as in the external HHS/ASPE finding), a targeted version of (ii) aimed at the highest-poverty/unemployment counties would be the natural refinement and is the main data-dependent improvement to this strategy.
