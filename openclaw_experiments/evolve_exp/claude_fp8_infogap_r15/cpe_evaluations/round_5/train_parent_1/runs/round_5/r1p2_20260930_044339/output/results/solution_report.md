## Subtask 1: Task 1 asks for a model that provides a measure of the ability of a region to provide clean water to meet the needs of i

### Problem

Task 1 asks for a model that provides a measure of the ability of a region to provide clean water to meet the needs of its population, accounting for the dynamic nature of the factors affecting both supply and demand. Scope: define an interpretable, time-varying availability index with a clear supply side (volume and quality), a demand side (full sectoral withdrawal), and a classification rule that turns the index into a scarcity status.

### Analysis

Assumptions (settled with the expert in Exchange 1, then structured in Exchange 2 and accepted as a whole): (1) The index must measure clean, serviceable supply, not gross volume — the Falkenmark thresholds are defined on volume per capita, but the task asks for the ability to provide CLEAN water, so a clean-serviceability factor φ(t)∈(0,1] multiplies total supply to capture economic scarcity from sanitation, treatment, and water-quality limits; this is kept distinct from the physical loss/inefficiency share c(t)∈[0,1) that captures conveyance, evaporation, and non-recoverable return-flow losses, so the volume channel and the quality channel enter separately. (2) The measure is the continuous, uncapped ratio of clean serviceable supply to demand — non-renewable augmentation (desalination, treated imports, managed recharge) is legitimate supply, so a hard zero-capped deficit would misstate the region's true provision ability. (3) The denominator is the region's FULL sectoral withdrawal demand (agriculture + industry + domestic), not a minimum-essential standard, because the task frames needs as population-wide and sectoral; a minimum-standard denominator would measure poverty, not provision capacity. (4) Time-varying drivers: population grows, climate reduces the renewable inflow, the agricultural allocation share decays as the economy develops, and (post-intervention) efficiency and augmentation improve. Method and why it is sound: a transparent two-factor supply model plus a sectoral demand model collapsed to a single ratio W(t) is the minimal structure that separates the physical and economic scarcity channels the problem names, is fully parameterizable from regional data, and admits direct classification via the Falkenmark per-capita thresholds applied to the clean-serviceable per-capita supply. The uncapped ratio makes the index monotone in every driver, which is what lets it be read as a measure of 'ability to provide clean water' rather than an artifact of capping.

### Modeling Process

Variables (year t, baseline year 2016): P(t) regional population (million); Q_renewable(t) natural renewable inflow = rainfall-runoff + renewable groundwater recharge (Mm³/yr); c(t) physical loss/inefficiency share; A(t) non-renewable augmentation = desalination + treated imports + managed recharge (Mm³/yr); φ(t) clean-serviceability (water quality × treatment/sanitation coverage) factor; a(t),i(t),d(t) time-varying agricultural/industrial/domestic allocation shares with a+i+d=1; q_a,q_i,q_d per-capita sectoral withdrawal rates (m³/capita/yr). Supply: S(t) = (1 - c(t))·Q_renewable(t) + A(t). Clean serviceable supply: S_clean(t) = φ(t)·S(t). Demand (full sectoral): D(t) = P(t)·[a(t)·q_a + i(t)·q_i + d(t)·q_d] (Mm³/yr). Index: W(t) = S_clean(t)/D(t), uncapped. Per-capita clean serviceable supply: s_pc(t) = S_clean(t)/P(t) (m³/capita/yr). Classification (Falkenmark, applied to s_pc): s_pc < 500 → absolute scarcity; 500 ≤ s_pc < 1000 → scarcity; 1000 ≤ s_pc < 1700 → stress; s_pc ≥ 1700 → secure. Headline 'critical' state = W<1, i.e. clean serviceable supply no longer covers full sectoral demand. Solution procedure: initialize the year-0 state from regional data, advance one year at a time updating P, Q_renewable, c, φ, A, and the allocation shares per the driver laws, recompute S, S_clean, D, W, s_pc, and classify; run the same state for the no-intervention and post-intervention phases, where interventions modify only φ, c, A, and the allocation shares in the intervention phase.

### Outcome Analysis

The index is fully interpretable: each of the three drivers (volume, quality, demand) has its own term, and the classification is a direct mapping to the Falkenmark bands the UN map uses, so the model's output is comparable to the 'heavily/moderately overloaded' labels of Task 2. Limitations and biases: (i) φ is a single aggregate factor — it cannot distinguish salinization from organic pollution, and it is hard to estimate without region-specific quality data, so its value is the largest single source of uncertainty; (ii) the uncapped ratio can overstate adequacy when augmentation dominates the renewable base (the failure mode the expert identified in Exchange 3 — see Task 5); (iii) q_a,q_i,q_d are treated as fixed per-capita rates, so improvements in sectoral efficiency other than the allocation drift are not captured; (iv) the model is annual and deterministic, so it does not resolve the high interannual rainfall variability that is the actual proximate cause of water stress in semi-arid regions; (v) it is a national-aggregate measure and does not resolve within-region distribution inequity, which the problem's 'distribution of clean water' language points to. These are stated as model weaknesses in the report per Task 6.

### Problem

Task 2 asks to pick one country or region that is heavily or moderately overloaded on the UN water-scarcity map, and to explain why and how water is scarce there, addressing both the social and environmental drivers and both physical and economic scarcity.

### Analysis

Region chosen: Israel. It is 'heavily overloaded' (severely water-stressed) on the UN/UNEP map, sits in a semi-arid to arid climatic zone, and is the canonical case of a region whose scarcity is simultaneously physical (low and highly variable renewable water) and economic (historically, low treatment/sanitation coverage and distribution losses limiting the clean share). Selecting Israel is well-suited to the Task-1 model because it has all four driver channels active at measurable magnitude — a small renewable base, a large and growing desalination augmentation term, a historically low and improving serviceability factor, and a large and shrinking agricultural share — so the model's separate volume and quality channels are both exercised. Environmental drivers: Mediterranean-climate rainfall concentrated in a short winter, long dry summers, low and interannually erratic renewable runoff, plus rising temperatures and shifting rainfall from climate change. Social drivers: rapid population and economic growth, a historically heavy and still-dominant agricultural (irrigation) share of withdrawal, and infrastructure quality (treatment, conveyance losses, sanitation) that historically limited how much of the physical water actually reached users as clean water.

### Modeling Process

The explanation is encoded as the sign and magnitude of each driver in the Task-1 state. Physical scarcity is the small renewable base and its decline: Q_renewable is modest (baseline 1100 Mm³/yr, set to a regional-scale renewable inflow) and falls at the climate rate (1.2%/yr) in the no-intervention phase. Economic scarcity is the serviceability factor: φ(2016)=0.85, i.e. only ~85% of total supply is clean and serviceable, with the rest lost to quality and treatment/sanitation limits; the physical loss share c(2016)=0.30 captures conveyance and evaporation losses. Demand is split a=0.62 (agriculture), i=0.18 (industry), d=0.20 (domestic), with q_a=25, q_i=35, q_d=130 m³/capita/yr, reflecting the region's historically irrigation-dominant withdrawal (consistent with the FAO finding that agriculture is the dominant sectoral share). Augmentation A(2016)=300 Mm³/yr is the desalination/import term. Baseline state: P=8.6 million, so clean serviceable supply S_clean(2016)=910 Mm³/yr, full demand D(2016)=411 Mm³/yr, index W(2016)=2.21, and per-capita clean serviceable supply s_pc(2016)=106 m³/capita/yr — deep in the Falkenmark absolute-scarcity band (<500).

### Outcome Analysis

The result is a consistent picture: Israel is heavily overloaded because its clean serviceable supply per capita (106 m³/capita/yr) is roughly one-fifth of the Falkenmark absolute-scarcity threshold, even though its total supply-to-demand ratio (W=2.21) looks adequate. The gap between the two is exactly the economic-scarcity channel — the region has water in the ground and at the coast, but the clean, serviceable share is what is short. The physical driver (small, climate-exposed renewable base) sets the ceiling; the economic driver (low φ and high c) is what keeps the usable share below the physical supply. Both are present, matching the problem's requirement to address physical and economic scarcity. Bias note: the baseline is a stylized national aggregate calibrated to land in the correct Falkenmark band and to reflect the region's known desalination-led supply mix; the absolute values of Q_renewable, A, and φ are order-of-magnitude regional scales, not a precise national water-balance ledger, so the classification and the direction of every projection are the robust outputs, not the exact numbers.

### Problem

Task 3 asks to use the Task-1 model to show what the water situation will be in 15 years (2031) in the chosen region, to explain how that situation impacts the lives of citizens, and to incorporate the environmental drivers' effects on the model components.

### Analysis

Method: run the no-intervention projection from the Task-2 baseline for 15 years, letting the environmental drivers act on the model components exactly as the model defines them — climate reduces Q_renewable by 1.2%/yr, population grows by 1.2%/yr, and the agricultural allocation share decays at 3%/yr toward a floor of 0.45 as the economy develops (with the industrial share rising correspondingly). No efficiency, augmentation, or quality improvement is applied in this phase, so the result isolates the environmental-and-population-driven trajectory. This is the 'no intervention' baseline that Task 5 contrasts against. The 15-year horizon is the problem's explicit ask; a longer extrapolation is carried separately in Task 5 to answer 'when' critical scarcity arrives.

### Modeling Process

No-intervention driver laws over t=0..15: P(t)=8.6·(1.012)^t million; Q_renewable(t)=1100·(0.988)^t Mm³/yr; c(t)=0.30 and φ(t)=0.85 held constant (no efficiency/quality investment); A(t)=300 Mm³/yr constant (desalination at current capacity); a(t)=max(0.45, 0.62·(0.97)^t), i(t)=0.18+(0.25-0.18)·(1-(0.97)^t), d(t)=1-a(t)-i(t). Then S,S_clean,D,W,s_pc as in Task 1. Key outputs: 2016 W=2.21, s_pc=106; 2021 W=1.70, s_pc=95; 2026 W=1.37, s_pc=86; 2031 W=1.23, s_pc=78. The index falls monotonically because the climate-driven supply decline (Q_renewable drops ~16% over 15 yr) and population/demand growth (P up ~18%, D up ~58%) both push against a flat clean share.

### Outcome Analysis

In 2031 the region is still 'able to provide' clean water in the W sense (W=1.23 > 1, so clean supply still covers full demand), but the margin has nearly halved from the 2016 level (2.21 → 1.23) and the per-capita clean serviceable supply has fallen to 78 m³/capita/yr — about 8% of the Falkenmark absolute-scarcity threshold, deep in absolute scarcity. Impact on citizens: (1) the shrinking per-capita clean supply means rationing becomes routine, with the domestic sector (q_d=130 m³/capita/yr, the highest per-capita need) the first to feel shortfalls because agriculture holds the largest share; (2) the falling margin (W approaching 1) means a single bad rainfall year — not represented in this deterministic model — can push the region into a hard deficit, so resilience to the very variability that is the region's defining hydrologic feature is eroding; (3) because the agricultural share is still ~45% of withdrawal even after drift, food production and agricultural employment remain exposed; (4) water quality/sanitation coverage, frozen at φ=0.85, means a rising share of any new supply is lost to the economic-scarcity channel rather than reaching users as clean water. The environmental drivers are the cause of all of this: the climate term directly cuts Q_renewable, and the population term directly lifts demand. Bias/limitation: the 15-year no-intervention path is a smooth deterministic trend; it under-states the year-to-year risk (the region's real stress is driven by wet/dry cycles the model averages over) and it assumes current desalination capacity is frozen, which is optimistic about how fast physical constraints bind.

### Problem

Task 4 asks to design an intervention plan for the chosen region that takes all the drivers of water scarcity into account, to discuss the plan's impact on surrounding areas and the wider water ecosystem, and to assess the plan's overall strengths and weaknesses in that larger context and how it mitigates scarcity.

### Analysis

The intervention is designed to act on every driver the model carries, so that the Task-5 projection can attribute the change to specific levers: (i) physical-loss reduction — modernize conveyance, fix leaks, and shift to closed-loop/drip irrigation to cut c from 0.30 toward 0.15 over 8 years; (ii) clean-serviceability improvement — expand treatment and sanitation coverage and restore water quality to lift φ from 0.85 toward 0.97 over 8 years; (iii) augmentation — expand desalination and managed aquifer recharge to raise A by 45 Mm³/yr per year during the 8-year ramp (to +360 Mm³/yr at full ramp) and to offset part of the climate decline in Q_renewable via 20 Mm³/yr per year of managed recharge; (iv) demand-side allocation — let the agricultural share continue its structural decline toward the 0.45 floor as the economy develops, and price water to reflect scarcity so the per-capita sectoral rates q_a,q_i,q_d do not drift upward. These four levers map one-to-one onto the model terms (c, φ, A, and the allocation shares), which is why they are the intervention's parameters. The larger-context analysis (surrounding areas and ecosystem) is treated as a qualitative overlay on the model's national aggregate, since the model does not resolve transboundary or groundwater-ecology effects.

### Modeling Process

Intervention phase driver laws over t=0..15 (ramp duration 8 yr): c(t)=0.30-(0.30-0.15)·min(1,t/8); φ(t)=0.85+(0.97-0.85)·min(1,t/8); A(t)=300+45·min(t,8); Q_renewable(t)=1100·(0.988)^t + 20·t (managed recharge offsetting climate decline); P(t) and the allocation-share laws identical to the no-intervention phase. All other Task-1 terms unchanged. The plan's mitigation is read off the difference in W and s_pc between the intervention and no-intervention phases.

### Outcome Analysis

Impact on the region (model result): by 2031 the intervention lifts W from 1.23 (no intervention) to 2.53 — a 105% higher margin — and lifts per-capita clean serviceable supply from 78 to 160 m³/capita/yr (+105%), roughly doubling the distance from the hard-deficit line. The four levers each contribute: loss reduction (c 0.30→0.15) returns ~37% of the renewable inflow that was previously lost; quality improvement (φ 0.85→0.97) converts ~12% more of total supply into clean serviceable supply; augmentation (A +360 Mm³/yr) adds a growing clean, non-renewable term; and the allocation drift plus scarcity pricing keeps the dominant agricultural share from re-expanding. Impact on surrounding areas and the water ecosystem (qualitative overlay, the model's national aggregate does not resolve these): (a) desalination increases brine discharge to the coastal marine environment, a net ecosystem cost that must be managed with diffuser design and outfall siting; (b) managed aquifer recharge and reduced over-extraction improve regional groundwater levels, which benefits neighboring basins and dependent ecosystems (wetlands, spring-fed rivers) but can also alter downstream baseflow that adjacent areas rely on; (c) expanded treatment reduces nutrient and salinity loading to shared rivers and the coast, a positive externality for downstream users; (d) scarcity pricing and irrigation modernization can shift withdrawal timing and volume, affecting shared water tables and the timing of flows to neighboring users. Strengths: the plan attacks all four model drivers simultaneously, so it is robust to any single driver dominating; it is reversible and phased over 8 years, so it can be scaled to budget and to how fast the climate decline actually materializes; the quality and loss levers are the cheapest in the long run because they make existing water usable rather than creating new water. Weaknesses in the larger context: desalination is energy-intensive and adds a carbon footprint and a brine impact; the plan is a national aggregate and does not guarantee that the benefits reach the most water-stressed sub-regions or that the ecosystem and transboundary costs are allocated fairly; the augmentation term, once built, can mask the underlying physical-scarcity signal (the Exchange-3 failure mode), so the plan must be paired with monitoring that keeps the renewable-base health visible rather than letting W alone drive decisions. Overall, the plan mitigates scarcity by raising both the volume and the clean share of supply and by curbing the fastest-growing demand, and it does so while its largest externalities (brine, energy, transboundary baseflow) are concentrated in the augmentation and recharge components rather than the efficiency components.

### Problem

Task 5 asks to use the Task-4 intervention and the Task-1 model to project water availability into the future, and to answer: can the region become less susceptible to water scarcity? Will water become a critical issue in the future, and if so, when will that scarcity occur?

### Analysis

Method: run the post-intervention projection for 15 years (the problem horizon) and extrapolate both the no-intervention and post-intervention trajectories to a 60-year horizon to locate the 'critical' crossing — defined as W<1, the point at which clean serviceable supply no longer covers full sectoral demand, i.e. the region can no longer meet its own needs from its clean supply. Comparing the two trajectories at the same horizon isolates the intervention's effect on susceptibility: if the intervention keeps W high and defers or prevents the W<1 crossing, the region is less susceptible.

### Modeling Process

Same state and driver laws as Task 3 (no intervention) and Task 4 (post-intervention), advanced to t=15 and to t=60. No-intervention: W falls 2.21→1.23 (2031)→1.03 (2041) and crosses W<1 in 2043 (≈27 years after baseline); per-capita clean supply falls 106→78 (2031)→64 (2041) and is below 100 m³/capita/yr from 2019 onward. Post-intervention: W rises to a 2024 peak of 2.79 then eases to 2.53 (2031) and 1.96 (2076); per-capita clean supply rises to 169 (2024) and eases to 160 (2031) and 118 (2076); it never falls below 100 m³/capita/yr within the 60-year horizon and W stays above 1 throughout. At year 15 the intervention's W is 105% higher than no-intervention (2.53 vs 1.23); at year 60 it is ~3.6× higher (1.96 vs 0.54).

### Outcome Analysis

Answers: (1) Yes, the region becomes markedly less susceptible to water scarcity — the intervention keeps the availability index roughly 2.5 instead of letting it decay toward 1, and keeps per-capita clean supply at ~160 instead of ~78 m³/capita/yr over the 15-year horizon. (2) Yes, water will become a critical issue in the future, and the model dates it: without intervention the region crosses into hard deficit (W<1, demand exceeding clean supply) in 2043, about 27 years after the 2016 baseline, with per-capita clean supply already below the 100 m³/capita/yr 'critical' level by 2019 and falling to ~58 m³/capita/yr by 2046. With the intervention, the W<1 crossing is not reached within the 60-year horizon and per-capita clean supply stays above 100 m³/capita/yr throughout, so the intervention defers the critical point beyond the modeled future. Caveats and the model's own limit (from the Exchange-3 counterexample): the post-intervention W is high in part because the augmentation term A dominates the renewable base, and the expert identified that an A-dominated supply with a nonzero-but-low φ can read 'adequate' while the renewable stock is being depleted — so the 2076 W≈1.96 should be read as 'the plan is working on the metrics it was built to move,' not as proof the region is permanently secure. The robust conclusions are the direction and the deferral: the intervention more than doubles the margin and pushes the critical crossing beyond 60 years, but it does not eliminate susceptibility, it converts a physical-scarcity problem into a managed, energy- and cost-dependent one, and it would need to be sustained and re-optimized as the climate decline deepens and the renewable base keeps shrinking. Bias summary for the report: the index's main biases are the single aggregate φ factor, the fixed per-capita sectoral rates, the deterministic annual resolution (no wet/dry-year risk), and the uncapped ratio's tendency to overstate adequacy under augmentation dominance; the model is best read as a driver-attribution and scenario-comparison tool for the region, not as a precise national water-balance forecast.
