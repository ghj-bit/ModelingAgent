## Subtask 1: Part 1. Using the NFLIS drug-identification data (2010-2017, counties of Ohio, Kentucky, West Virginia, and Virginia; Te

### Problem

Part 1. Using the NFLIS drug-identification data (2010-2017, counties of Ohio, Kentucky, West Virginia, and Virginia; Tennessee is named in the problem but contains no rows in the supplied file), build a model describing the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid and heroin cases in and between the states and counties over time. Identify possible locations where specific opioid use might have started in each state, the drug-identification threshold levels at which concern should trigger, and where and when the model predicts those thresholds will be crossed in the future.

### Analysis

ASSUMPTIONS. (1) The supplied NFLIS file contains data for OH, KY, WV, and VA only; Tennessee has zero rows, so the five-state scope is analyzed as four states and the absence is reported as a data limitation. (2) Per the expert's structural ruling (Exchange 1), a county-class-year count is a censored observation of a detection process, not a snapshot of use: count = f(true use intensity, detection probability), where the detection probability is state/lab/year-specific and unobserved. Zero is absence of detection, not of use. Consequently only within-state, within-year relative county comparisons are defensible; the state-year total is used as the detection normalizer. (3) Per the expert (Exchange 2, the proposed latent adjacency structure was rejected; Exchange 3), the fallback is a detection-normalized epidemiological-curve model with no county adjacency graph and no unobserved latent layer. (4) The substance list is partitioned into three classes: Heroin; Fentanyl-class (all 29 fentanyl names plus the U-47700/U-48800/U-49900/U-51754/MT-45 research analogs); and Other (all remaining narcotic analgesics: oxycodone, methadone, morphine, hydrocodone, codeine, etc.). This partition is structural, matching the problem's 'synthetic and heroin' framing while keeping the prescription base separate. METHOD. State-level annual totals per class are fit with a shifted log-logistic curve Y_t = B + A/(1+exp(-k(t-t50))), and where the curve has not reached its inflection by 2017 (the fentanyl class) with a pure exponential Y_t = B + A exp(g(t-2010)); the two are selected by BIC. County-level detection-normalized counts Z = Y/d (d = state-class-year total) and growth rates G = log(Z_t/Z_{t-1}) are computed, with zero-to-positive transitions excluded as detection onset per the expert's Exchange 3 ruling. 'Origin' is the first-reported-here signal, not a claim about where true use began.

### Modeling Process

State curve: Y_{s,c,t} = B + A/(1+exp(-k(t - t50))) (logistic) or Y = B + A exp(g(t-2010)) (exponential), fit by nonlinear least squares (scipy curve_fit) to the 8 observed state-year totals, chosen by BIC; forecast 2018-2020 by evaluation of the fitted curve. County level: Z_{s,c,i,t} = Y_{s,c,i,t}/d_{s,c,t} with d the state-class-year total; G_{s,c,i,t} = log(Z_t/Z_{t-1}) defined only when both years are nonzero; 'stable' counties have nonzero Z in >=5 of 8 years. Origin signal: for each state-class, the earliest year in which a stable county's Z exceeds 3x the state-year median Z and its G is in that year's top decile; the county named is 'first reported here.' Threshold: G* = 75th percentile of the empirical G distribution over stable counties; a county-class is flagged when G > G* for 2 consecutive years. CAGR per state-class computed as (Y_2017/Y_2010)^(1/7)-1 and (Y_2017/Y_2013)^(1/4)-1.

### Outcome Analysis

RESULTS. (a) Curves. Fentanyl is the dominant and still-accelerating class: OH Fentanyl grew at a 2013-2017 CAGR of +255% (logistic, t50=2016.1, R2=1.000), forecast to 22,481 in 2020 (2017: 17,707). KY Fentanyl is exponential (underdetermined, no plateau in window), CAGR +222%, forecast 33,189 in 2020 (2017: 2,701) - a 12x extrapolation that should be read as a lower bound on an inflection not yet reached. WV Fentanyl logistic t50=2015.2 (R2=0.992), forecast 326 in 2020. VA Fentanyl logistic t50=2016.4 (R2=0.997), forecast 2,793 in 2020. Heroin is peaking or declining in three states: OH peaked 2015 (23,347), 2013-2017 CAGR -4.8%, forecast 19,945 in 2020; KY peaked 2014 (4,362), CAGR -6.2%, forecast 3,906; WV peaked 2013 (1,857), CAGR -20.3%, forecast 1,155. VA Heroin is the exception - still rising (2013-2017 CAGR +7.7% to peak, exponential fit, R2=0.528), forecast 6,166 in 2020. Other (prescription base) is flat-to-declining in all states (KY and WV negative CAGR), confirming the illicit classes, not the prescription base, are the growing signal. (b) Origin signals (first-reported-here, detection-caveated). Fentanyl: OH 2011 (Stark, Defiance); KY 2011 (Jefferson); WV 2011 (Berkeley); VA 2013 (Fairfax). Heroin: OH 2011 (Licking); KY 2011 (Boyd, Pendleton); WV 2011 (Cabell); VA 2011 (Caroline, New Kent). Other: OH 2012 (Cuyahoga); KY 2012 (Whitley, Scott); WV 2012; VA 2012. These are the earliest counties to clear 3x the state-year median with top-decile growth; per the expert they identify where the class was first *reported*, not where use truly began, because a zero in an earlier year may be absence of detection. (c) Threshold levels. With G* at the 75th percentile of the stable-county G distribution, the trigger fires on county-classes whose detection-normalized growth exceeds G* for two consecutive years; the first flags appear in 2011 (heroin, KY) and 2014 (fentanyl, KY - Campbell). Because the problem text commits to no threshold reading, the threshold is a property of the detection-normalized rate of change, not the raw count or a per-capita share; the 75th percentile is a data-driven calibration, and a sweep over quantiles (0.5, 0.75, 1.0) is reported in the code output. (d) Where/when in the future. The model's concrete future-prediction is the 2018-2020 state-class curve forecasts above: the fentanyl class continues to rise in all four states, with Kentucky's exponential forecast the most aggressive (and least certain) because its curve had not inflected by 2017. LIMITATIONS AND BIAS. The fentanyl exponential forecast for KY is an extrapolation past the inflection and is a lower bound. The state-level curve fit for WV and VA heroin has low R2 (0.001, 0.528) and is unstable. Detection heterogeneity across states (lab capacity, submission policy) is normalized only by the state total, so cross-state level comparisons are not valid - only within-state trends and the detection-normalized county growth are. The 'origin' signal is confounded by late-entry detection: a county that first reports a class in year t is flagged even if use predates t, which is precisely the failure mode the expert identified in Exchange 3 and which the stable-county + zero-exclusion rule mitigates but does not eliminate. The data end in 2017, so the 2018-2020 forecast is out-of-sample and carries the full uncertainty of the curve form.

### Problem

Part 2. Using the U.S. Census Bureau (ACS DP02) socio-economic data for the same counties, 2010-2016, test whether opioid use or trends-in-use are associated with the provided socio-economic variables, and modify the Part 1 model to include any important factors.

### Analysis

ASSUMPTIONS. The ACS DP02 files contain a fixed set of socio-economic variables: household composition, educational attainment (25+), 65+ household share, and related demographic tables. They do NOT contain poverty rate, median income, or unemployment - the variables the external HHS/ASPE literature (Item 2) associates with opioid outcomes. The modelable covariate set is therefore: pct_less_hs (% of 25+ adults with less than a high-school diploma), pct_bach_plus (% with a bachelor's or higher degree), pct_65plus_hh (% of households with someone 65+), pct_family, pct_nonfamily, and log(total households) as a size proxy. The dependent variable is the Part 1 detection-normalized county growth rate G = log(Z_t/Z_{t-1}), defined only for stable counties with both years nonzero (zero-to-positive excluded as detection onset per Exchange 3). METHOD. A fixed-effects (within) panel regression of G on the standardized ACS covariates, with county x class and year fixed effects removed by within-transformation, and ridge regularization (lambda=1.0) to handle near-collinearity among the covariates. The a priori hypothesis from the literature is that economic/educational hardship (lower education, smaller households, older household structure) is associated with higher growth; the coefficients' signs are the testable claim, their magnitudes estimated.

### Modeling Process

Panel: county i, class c, year t in 2011-2016 (ACS available 2010-2016; G for year t uses the year-t ACS snapshot). Rows with missing covariates dropped (2,553 observations, 277 counties). Dependent: G_{s,c,i,t} = log((Y_{t}/d_{s,c,t})/(Y_{t-1}/d_{s,c,t-1})). Covariates x_{k} standardized (zero mean, unit variance). Model: G = alpha_i + gamma_t + sum_k beta_k x_k + eps, with alpha_i (county x class) and gamma_t (year) fixed effects. Estimation: within-transform demean by county x class then by year, then ridge OLS beta = (X'X + lambda I)^-1 X'y with lambda=1.0. Standard errors from (sigma^2 (X'X + lambda I)^-1); t-tests via the normal distribution.

### Outcome Analysis

RESULTS. The within-county association between the available ACS socio-economic covariates and the detection-normalized growth rate is statistically indistinguishable from zero. R2_within = 0.001. Coefficients (standardized): pct_less_hs +0.011 (t=0.21, p=0.83); pct_bach_plus -0.036 (t=-0.48, p=0.63); pct_65plus_hh -0.038 (t=-0.81, p=0.42); pct_family +0.0004 (t=0.00, p=1.00); pct_nonfamily -0.0004 (t=-0.00, p=1.00); logHH -0.020 (t=-0.43, p=0.67). None is significant at any conventional level, and none has the sign-magnitude the HHS/ASPE priors would suggest for a one-standard-deviation shift. INTERPRETATION. This is a genuine null result, not a failure to find an effect: within a county, year-to-year changes in the detection-normalized growth rate are not explained by the county's own cross-sectional socio-economic profile from DP02. The likely reasons are (i) DP02 lacks the poverty, income, and unemployment variables that the external literature identifies as the operative drivers, so the modelable covariate set is the wrong set; (ii) 5-year ACS estimates are too coarse to track year-to-year growth, introducing measurement error that biases coefficients toward zero; and (iii) the detection-normalized growth is dominated by the substance-class transition (the fentanyl wave) rather than by stable local socio-economics. The Part 1 model is therefore NOT modified by Part 2 covariates: there is no coefficient large enough and significant enough to add to the growth term. The honest conclusion is that, with the data provided, the socio-economic profile of a county does not predict its year-to-year change in reported opioid-identification growth; the external HHS/ASPE association (hardship -> higher opioid measures) is a cross-sectional, national-scale relationship that this county-level, within-county, detection-normalized test does not reproduce, and the absence of poverty/income/unemployment from the supplied files is the binding constraint.

### Problem

Part 3. Combining the Part 1 and Part 2 results, identify a possible strategy for countering the opioid crisis, test its effectiveness with the model, and identify the significant parameter bounds on which success or failure depends. Also summarize the significant insights for a 1-2 page memo to the Chief Administrator, DEA/NFLIS.

### Analysis

ASSUMPTIONS. The Part 2 null means the strategy cannot lean on a county socio-economic lever that the data support; the operative mechanism is the spread/growth of the illicit classes identified in Part 1. The strategy is therefore (i) a reduction in the intrinsic growth rate k_{s,c} of the fentanyl (and still-growing heroin) curves - i.e., slowing the pace of the wave at the state level, the direct target of interdiction, diversion control, and detection-deterrence - and (ii) a reduction in the county detection-normalized growth rate G via the socioeconomic channel, applied at a conservative magnitude because Part 2 found that channel's within-county effect statistically zero. SUCCESS CRITERION. A state-class is 'controlled' if its predicted 2020 total does not exceed its 2017 total by more than a data-driven ceiling, ceiling = max(one in-sample standard error of the curve fit, 10% of the 2017 total). PARAMETER BOUNDS. The critical reduction delta_k in the growth rate (with the socioeconomic lever at zero) below which each state-class is controlled, found by bisection on delta_k in [0, 0.999].

### Modeling Process

Counterfactual: for each state-class curve, re-anchor at the 2017 observed value and scale the growth. For a logistic (B,A,k,t50): replace k by k*(1-delta_k), re-solve A so the curve passes through the 2017 value, evaluate at 2020. For an exponential (B,A,g): forecast Y_2017 * exp(g*(1-delta_k)*3). The combined strategy multiplier is (1-delta_k)*(1-0.5*delta_G), where the 0.5 factor is a conservative translation of the county-level G effect (found ~0 in Part 2) to the state aggregate. Success if Y_2020 <= Y_2017 + ceiling. Critical delta_k per state-class by bisection.

### Outcome Analysis

RESULTS. The parameter bounds are stark and they are the central finding. For the fentanyl class, the strategy must reduce the intrinsic growth rate by 93-96% to hold the 2020 total within the ceiling: OH Fentanyl delta_k* = 0.949; KY Fentanyl 0.930; WV Fentanyl 0.947; VA Fentanyl 0.957. In other words, the fentanyl wave is growing so fast (2013-2017 CAGR +218% to +255%) that a 95% reduction in its growth rate is the bound on which containment succeeds - any smaller reduction and the 2020 total breaches the ceiling. For heroin the bounds are far more attainable because the wave is already peaking or declining: OH, KY, and WV heroin require delta_k* = 0.000 (no intervention needed - the 2020 forecast is already within the ceiling of the 2017 level); VA heroin, the one state where heroin is still rising, requires delta_k* = 0.463, a ~46% growth-rate reduction. INTERPRETATION. The crisis is bimodal. The heroin component is past its inflection in three of four states and is self-moderating on the reported-identification measure; the fentanyl component is the active, accelerating front and is the only one on which a counter-strategy must concentrate, and it faces a near-total (>=93%) growth-rate-reduction requirement. The socioeconomic lever contributes little in the model because Part 2 found no significant within-county association; the binding constraint is the growth rate of the fentanyl class itself. SIGNIFICANT INSIGHTS FOR THE MEMO. (1) The fentanyl class is the dominant and still-accelerating signal in all four states; its 2013-2017 CAGR exceeds +200% in OH and KY, and the model forecasts continued growth through 2020 in every state. (2) Heroin is peaking or declining in OH, KY, and WV (each has passed its 2013-2015 peak) but is still rising in VA, which should be a priority for heroin-specific response. (3) The 'origin' (first-reported-here) counties for fentanyl are Stark/Defiance (OH, 2011), Jefferson (KY, 2011), Berkeley (WV, 2011), and Fairfax (VA, 2013); these are where the class was first reported at above-median intensity, subject to the detection caveat that an earlier zero may be absence of detection. (4) The drug-identification threshold that should trigger concern is a detection-normalized year-over-year growth rate exceeding the 75th percentile of the stable-county distribution for two consecutive years; the first such fentanyl flags appear in 2014 (KY, Campbell). (5) The supplied ACS DP02 files do not contain the poverty, income, or unemployment variables that national literature links to opioid outcomes, so no county socio-economic profile in the provided data predicts year-to-year growth; the model's counter-strategy is bounded by the fentanyl growth rate, not by a socio-economic lever. (6) The critical growth-rate reductions for fentanyl containment are 93-96% - a bound that implies interdiction and diversion-control measures must be near-maximally effective to hold the 2020 level, and that any materially smaller effect will see the fentanyl total breach the ceiling. LIMITATIONS. The fentanyl exponential forecast for KY is a lower-bound extrapolation; the 2018-2020 window is out-of-sample; detection heterogeneity is normalized only by state total, so cross-state level comparisons are invalid; and the success ceiling is a data-driven but arbitrary choice (max(in-sample SE, 10% of 2017)). The bounds are therefore directionally robust (fentanyl requires a far larger growth-rate cut than heroin) but their exact values depend on the ceiling and the curve form.
