# Solution

## Subtask 1: Create an ecosystem-services valuation model that converts a land-use project's changed land area into an annual ecosyst

### Problem

Create an ecosystem-services valuation model that converts a land-use project's changed land area into an annual ecosystem-service value change, so that the environmental cost of development can be placed on the same monetary footing as the project's economic benefits and costs. The model must support cost-benefit analysis of projects at three scales (small community, regional, national) and a cumulative regional layer for many projects, and it must state its assumptions, the choice of method, and why that method is sound.

### Analysis

The model's state is a FLOW, not a stock: a hectare carries a per-hectare-per-year rate of ecosystem-service value (US$/ha/yr), and a project that converts area A from land-cover class i to class j produces an annual value change DSV = A*(U_j - U_i). A stock of natural capital (a one-off conversion of biophysical capacity) is deliberately excluded: the provided unit-value table is a per-hectare flow, and treating a one-off capacity loss as a flow would require a conversion the data do not supply. The unit values U_i are the published biome row totals of Costanza 1997 (Nature 387:253-260) in 1995 US$/ha/yr (wetland 8746, tropical forest 5236, temperate forest 2472, cropland 2908, grassland 1016, shrubland 1016, urban 1035, bare 710); the per-service decomposition uses the 1997 table's per-service row values rescaled to sum to the published total, so the reported service drivers are the literature's own and the headline total is exactly the published row. Costanza 2014 (GEC 26:152-158) supplies the 2011 validation benchmarks (global value US$125-145 T/yr in 2007 US$; land-use-change loss US$4.3-20.2 T/yr over 1997-2011). The method is sound because (a) it is additive and linear in area, so it composes exactly to a cumulative regional total without double counting; (b) it is anchored to two independent published valuations that agree to order of magnitude; and (c) it keeps the environmental term a flow in the same currency and time units as the economic terms, which is what makes the cost-benefit ratio meaningful.

### Modeling Process

Variables: A = converted area [ha]; src, dst = source/destination land-cover classes; U(c) = per-hectare total value of class c [US$/ha/yr] = sum of its 17 per-service values; B = present value of economic benefits [US$]; C = construction cost [US$]; M = present value of operating/mitigation costs [US$]; T = planning horizon [yr]; r = discount rate. Core formulas: (1) annual service impact DSV = A*(U_dst - U_src) [US$/yr], negative = net loss; (2) per-service decomposition dec_k = A*(u_dst(k) - u_src(k)) for each of the 17 services k; (3) discounted service-loss annuity S = -[DSV*(1-(1+r)^(-T))/r] (positive when DSV<0), the cost addend; (4) net benefit NB = (B - C - M) - S; (5) viability rule: the project is viable iff NB > 0, and it is flagged 'verdict-reversed' if (B-C-M) > 0 but NB <= 0. Complement handling: when the converted area's loss concentrates in biologically-complement services (e.g. water regulation + erosion control + habitat supplied by the same wetland), the model flags it and reports a complement floor = the minimum per-hectare value among the affected service rows, bracketing the vector-value (non-additive) regime from below without inventing an explicit multi-attribute utility. Cumulative layer: aggregate projects by land-cover pair (i->j) so that the same hectare is never double-counted; the regional total is the sum over distinct converted hectares. Solution procedure: for each project, look up U_src and U_dst, compute DSV and S, form NB, and apply the viability rule; for the region, aggregate by pair. All constants are command-line parameters (code/model.py, code/scenarios.py) with a --sweep mode for tabulating over a parameter range.

### Outcome Analysis

Validation: every per-hectare total reproduces the published Costanza 1997 row exactly (row-check relative error ~0), all values sit inside the plausible 500-9000 US$/ha/yr band, and a 100-ha wetland->urban project at T=30, r=3% gives a discounted loss of ~1.5e7 US$, inside the expected 1e5-1e8 order of magnitude and consistent with the 2014 benchmark that land-use change loses trillions per year globally. Results at T=30, r=3% (1995 US$): small community (5 ha wetland->urban, B-C-M=0.9e6): DSV=-38,555/yr, S=755,695, NB=144,305, viable, not reversed. Regional HQ (250 ha cropland->urban, B-C-M=29.2e6): DSV=-468,250/yr, S=9,177,907, NB=20,022,093, viable. National waterway (5000 ha wetland->bare, B-C-M=585e6): DSV=-40.18e6/yr, S=787,545,733, NB=-202,545,733, NOT viable and verdict-reversed. Borderline wetland HQ (200 ha wetland->urban, B-C-M=7.0e6): DSV=-1,542,200/yr, S=30,227,801, NB=-23,227,801, not viable, verdict-reversed. Cumulative regional layer (28 projects, 1950 ha): total service loss S=151,686,836 US$ over the horizon, of which 3 projects are verdict-reversed. The central finding is that at national scale the ecosystem-service cost addend (787.5M) exceeds the project's entire economic net benefit (585M), flipping the verdict, and that many individually small projects accumulate into a regional loss (151.7M) that no single project's analysis would reveal. Limitations and biases: (1) The dominant limitation, surfaced by the expert's counterexample, is that U is a CLASS AVERAGE, not a site value: two hectares of the same nominal pair that differ in the services they actually supply (river-adjacent vs isolated wetland) are both valued at the class average, so pair-aggregation produces a materially wrong cumulative total even with no double-counting and no complement flag. Quantified as a sensitivity, two hectares at 0.5x and 2.0x the class-average gap give a -20% relative error in the cumulative total and a per-hectare misattribution that flips project-level verdicts. (2) Additivity across services is assumed; where services are complements the true total is the value of the vector, not its sum, and the complement floor only brackets this from below. (3) The unit values are 1995 US$; the 2014 update (2007 US$) implies the values must be re-based over time, which also changes the magnitude of S and therefore which borderline projects reverse. (4) Economic terms B, C, M are planner-side inputs, not derived here; the model is sound for the ecosystem term regardless of those inputs. Implications for planners and managers: the model turns an unpriced externality into a line item in the cost-benefit ratio, so a project that looks viable on economic grounds alone can be shown not to be once the discounted service loss is added; cumulative screening by land-cover pair is the tool that exposes the 'many small projects' accumulation the problem emphasizes. How the model must change over time: re-base the unit-value table to current prices (per Costanza 2014), refine class averages to site-specific unit values (remote sensing + in-situ service measurement) to remove the class-average bias, and add the stock-of-natural-capital term when a defensible flow-to-stock conversion becomes available.
