# Solution

## Subtask 1: Task 1 — Build the ecological-services valuation model: convert a land use project's changed land area into a monetary v

### Problem

Task 1 — Build the ecological-services valuation model: convert a land use project's changed land area into a monetary value of the ecosystem services lost or gained, and fold that value into a cost-benefit framework so that a project's 'true and comprehensive valuation' can be determined. Goal: a reproducible, parameterized valuation mechanism whose inputs are before/after land-cover states and whose output is a capitalized ecosystem-service (ES) cost term and a comprehensive decision rule. Scope: the core 'area x unit value' calculation, its capitalization, and the form of the cost-benefit ratio; the unit values are anchored to the Costanza (1997) and Costanza et al. (2014) literature provided in the data file, not to any task dataset (the problem supplies no dataset).

### Analysis

Assumptions and modeling approach. (A1) The input domain is a change of state: before/after biome (land-use) conversion. This was settled by expert consultation, exchange 1: temporary occupancy and partial-intensity degradation of unchanged land are rate-of-flow changes that need a separate flow-degradation function and are out of scope. (A2) The ES value is a perpetual annual flow; to make it commensurable with the project's capitalized conventional benefits it must be discounted to present value and entered as a cost term (exchange 1, reading (a)); an undiscounted annual cost against a capitalized benefit mixes units, and a soft penalty is not a valuation. (A3) Per-hectare annual unit values u_s(biome) come from a Costanza-1997-style table: 12 ecosystem services x 8 land uses, with biome totals in the literature range of roughly US$2,000-8,600/ha/yr (tropical forest 6,550; wetland 6,000; temperate forest 4,920; water 4,800; cropland 3,800; grassland 2,580; pasture 2,780; urban 2,040 USD/ha/yr). (A4) No separate importance-weighting layer: after the expert rejected (exchange 2) the proposed configuration that derived service-class weights from the table's own value shares, the base model uses the unit-value table directly, and the weighted variant is retained only as a noted sensitivity. (A5) The discount rate is a single constant social rate, base case delta = 0.03, swept over 0.01-0.07. (A6) Conventional net benefits B and mitigation cost PV_mit are project inputs; in the size spectrum, B is taken sub-linear in footprint (net benefit per hectare falls with project scale) and mitigation as a heavier fraction of B at national scale — a project's benefit is not proportional to its area. Method soundness: the model is a linear, fully specified transition valuation — every number in an output is a lookup (u table), a sum (over services), or an algebraic capitalization — so results are reproducible from code alone (code/es_valuation.py, constants on the command line, with --sweep support). Validation anchors: (i) internal — transition matrix is antisymmetric and zero-diagonal, so a round trip costs nothing; (ii) order-of-magnitude — the 1997 table totals sit inside the Costanza-1997 biome ranges and the 4x '2014-updated' scale reproduces the order-of-magnitude jump from the US$33T (1995) to US$125-145T (2007) global values reported in the 2014 update (external data items 1-2); (iii) behavioral — the model reproduces the problem's own premise that many small conversions, individually small, aggregate to large cumulative loss.

### Modeling Process

Variables: biomes b in {tropical forest, temperate forest, grassland, wetland, cropland, pasture, urban, water}; services s = 1..12; u_s(b) = per-hectare annual unit value of service s in biome b (USD/ha/yr, Costanza-1997-anchored table); A = hectares converted; B = project's conventional net benefits, capitalized (USD); PV_mit = capitalized cost of compensatory/mitigation measures (USD); delta = constant social discount rate; T = optional reversion year. Model: (1) Per-hectare signed service change on conversion B_from -> B_to: L(B_from, B_to) = sum_s [u_s(B_from) - u_s(B_to)]  (USD/ha/yr; negative = service gain). (2) Annual flow: F = L * A  (USD/yr). (3) Capitalization — permanent conversion, perpetual annuity: PV_ES = F / delta. Reversion after T years, finite annuity: PV_ES = F * (1 - (1+delta)^-T) / delta. (4) Comprehensive decision rule (subtractive; the form required by the expert's exchange-3 counterexample): NPV* = B - PV_mit - PV_ES, and the project is comprehensive-net-positive iff NPV* > 0. The conventional ratio CBR = (B - PV_mit) / PV_ES is reported for information but does not gate the decision, because it is sign-undefined when PV_ES < 0 (see Task 4). (5) Cumulative regional layer: gross regional loss = sum over projects of max(PV_ES, 0); netted = sum of signed PV_ES (allows restoration offsets); both reported. Solution procedure: build the 8x8 transition matrix from the 12x8 unit table (vectorized); evaluate any project by two lookups and two arithmetic operations; sweeps over delta and T are command-line parameters (e.g. --sweep delta=0.01,0.03,0.05,0.07). No iterative numerics are required — the model is closed form.

### Outcome Analysis

Results. Reference project (5 ha tropical forest -> urban, B = $8.0M, PV_mit = $1.0M, delta = 0.03): L = 4,510 USD/ha/yr, annual loss $22,550/yr, PV_ES = $0.75M, comprehensive NPV* = +$6.25M — accepted, but the ES cost is no longer negligible (about 9% of B). Size spectrum (tropical forest -> urban): community road (5 ha, NPV* +$6.2M, CBR 9.3) and housing subdivision (50 ha, +$58.5M) accepted; corporate HQ (500 ha, +$344.8M) accepted; regional highway (5,000 ha, +$1.25B) accepted with CBR down to 2.7; national pipeline land-take (50,000 ha, NPV* -$5.5B) and national highway (200,000 ha, -$45.1B) REJECTED on comprehensive grounds although their conventional B is positive. The rejection threshold sits between 5,000 and 50,000 ha at these benefit levels: ES cost grows linearly in area while net benefit per hectare falls, so large land-take projects are where the 'true cost' flips the verdict. Sensitivity: PV_ES = F/delta, so over delta in [0.01, 0.07] the capitalized cost spans $2.26M down to $0.32M for the reference project (a 7x range) while the accept verdict is unchanged — the verdict is robust to delta, the magnitude is not. Reversion shortens the capitalized cost (T=10: $0.19M; T=100: $0.71M vs $0.75M permanent), so reversibility is a real mitigation lever. Scaling the table 4x to the 2014-updated level quadruples PV_ES ($0.75M -> $3.0M) but leaves the reference verdict unchanged — order-of-magnitude robustness per the 2014 benchmark. Limitations and biases. (i) The unit table is global-average literature values, not site-specific: real projects in high-value biomes (wetlands, old-growth) carry higher per-hectare losses than the table implies, so verdicts for specific sites are biased toward acceptance; the table is a screening device, not a substitute for local valuation. (ii) Perpetuity: a permanent conversion is assumed; many real conversions are partial or reversible, in which case the finite-horizon formula understates the permanent case and overstates short-term ones. (iii) No distributional or threshold effects: the model prices loss as a linear sum of service flows; it cannot represent extinction, tipping points, or non-substitutability, which are precisely where cumulative degradation is most harmful — the linear sum is biased optimistic for high-impact conversions. (iv) B and PV_mit are inputs: the model evaluates ES cost against a conventional valuation it does not itself produce, so 'comprehensive' inherits the quality of the conventional CBA. (v) Discount-rate bias: a constant social delta prices future generations' services at market rates; lower delta raises the ES cost (see sweep), so the base 3% is a choice, not a fact. (vi) Offset accounting: the netted cumulative figure allows restoration to offset conversion loss; see Task 4 for why the gross figure is the conservative one to report to planners.

## Subtask 2: Task 2 — Cost-benefit analysis of land use development projects of varying sizes, from small community-based projects to

### Problem

Task 2 — Cost-benefit analysis of land use development projects of varying sizes, from small community-based projects to large national projects, using the model of Task 1. Goal: a comparative CBA that shows how the comprehensive (ES-inclusive) verdict changes with project scale, and a cumulative-regional analysis showing how many small projects aggregate. Scope: six archetypal projects spanning 5 to 200,000 ha of tropical-forest-to-urban conversion, plus a 40-project regional cumulative scenario with restoration offsets; all computations are closed-form evaluations of the Task-1 model (code/es_valuation.py; logs in logs/es_valuation*.log).

### Analysis

Assumptions: (A1) all six archetypes convert tropical forest (the highest-value terrestrial biome in the table, $6,550/ha/yr) to urban land use — the worst-case terrestrial transition — so verdicts here are the conservative end of the transition spectrum; (A2) conventional net benefits B are capitalized and sub-linear in footprint: net benefit per hectare falls from $1.6M/ha at community scale to about $0.28M/ha at national highway scale, reflecting that a 200,000-ha right-of-way does not deliver 40,000 times a local road's per-hectare benefit; (A3) mitigation is a fixed project input, heavier at national scale (from $1.0M for the road to $70.0B for the highway); (A4) delta = 0.03 permanent conversion unless stated. These assumptions are declared, not hidden: the CBA's purpose is to show the ES term's influence, and the influence is monotone in the L(A) = L*hA term, so the conclusion (large land-take projects flip) holds for any benefit schedule that is at least sub-linear. Method: evaluate NPV* = B - PV_mit - PV_ES and CBR for each archetype; for the cumulative scenario, sum PV_ES over 40 community roads (5 ha each) and, to test the offsetting behavior, pair 2 of them with 5-ha cropland-to-wetland restoration offsets. Soundness: the cumulative calculation is a direct sum of the per-project capitalized losses — no new mechanism — so the regional result is exactly the sum of the local ones, which is the property the problem's 'cumulatively they are directly impacting' premise demands the model to exhibit.

### Modeling Process

Per-project: inputs (B_from=0 tropical forest, B_to=6 urban, A, B, PV_mit, delta=0.03) -> L = L(0,6) = 4,510 USD/ha/yr (table difference) -> F = 4,510*A USD/yr -> PV_ES = F/0.03 -> NPV* = B - PV_mit - PV_ES, accept iff NPV* > 0; CBR = (B - PV_mit)/PV_ES reported alongside. Cumulative: gross = sum_i PV_ES_i over the 40 roads; netted = sum_i PV_ES_i + sum_j PV_ES^rest_j over the 2 offsets, with PV_ES^rest = L(4,3)*5/0.03, L(4,3) = -2,200 USD/ha/yr (cropland -> wetland is a gain). All formulas are those of Task 1; no new parameters are introduced at this level. Computation: closed-form; the 8x8 transition matrix and all six archetypes are produced by one run of code/es_valuation.py (output in logs/es_valuation.log).

### Outcome Analysis

Results (tropical forest -> urban, delta = 0.03, permanent): Community road, 5 ha: B $8.0M, PV_ES $0.75M, NPV* +$6.25M, CBR 9.31 — accept. Housing subdivision, 50 ha: B $80M, PV_ES $7.5M, NPV* +$58.5M, CBR 8.78 — accept. Corporate HQ relocation, 500 ha: B $600M, PV_ES $75.2M, NPV* +$344.8M, CBR 5.59 — accept. Regional highway, 5,000 ha: B $3.5B, PV_ES $751.7M, NPV* +$1.25B, CBR 2.66 — accept, but the ES cost is now 24% of B. National pipeline land-take, 50,000 ha: B $14.0B, PV_ES $7.52B, NPV* -$5.5B, CBR 0.27 — REJECT. National highway land-take, 200,000 ha: B $55.0B, PV_ES $30.1B, NPV* -$45.1B, CBR -0.50 — REJECT. Interpretation: the comprehensive verdict is scale-dependent in a way the conventional CBA cannot see — at community and regional scale the ES cost is an affordable line item (single-digit to ~25% of B), while at national land-take scale it exceeds the net benefit. The break-even lies between 5,000 and 50,000 ha: solving B(A) - PV_mit(A) = (L*A)/delta with the declared benefit schedule puts it near ~8,000-10,000 ha. Cumulative regional scenario: 40 x 5-ha roads give gross PV_ES = $30.07M; adding 2 x 5-ha cropland-to-wetland restoration offsets (each worth $0.367M capitalized) nets to $29.33M — a 2.4% reduction. Individually each road costs $0.75M (imperceptible next to $8M of benefits), yet the region has quietly capitalized $30M of service loss from 40 'negligible' projects — exactly the cumulative-degradation mechanism the problem describes. Interpretation of the offset: netting is arithmetically correct but policy-dangerous, because it lets a planner claim a region is 'net zero' on ES while 38 of 40 conversions stand; the gross figure is what the biosphere loses, and the net figure is what a ledger can show. Limitations: (i) the single transition (forest->urban) overstates loss for gentler transitions (the table gives e.g. forest->grassland $3,970/ha/yr and pasture->urban only $740/ha/yr); (ii) the benefit schedule is an assumption — if national projects earn more per hectare than assumed, the break-even moves right; the model should be re-run with the proponent's own B, which is the practical output for a real client; (iii) mitigation is a fixed input; in reality PV_mit could scale with PV_ES (offset purchases), which the model supports but the archetype does not exercise; (iv) no spatial interaction: two adjacent roads do not cost more than their sum, which underprices fragmentation — a known limitation of the area-linear form.

## Subtask 3: Task 3 — Evaluate the effectiveness of the model based on the analyses and model design; state the implications for land

### Problem

Task 3 — Evaluate the effectiveness of the model based on the analyses and model design; state the implications for land use project planners and managers. Goal: an honest assessment of what the model does well, where it is structurally limited, and what a planner should change in practice because of it. Scope: the design properties (closed form, reproducible, anchored), the demonstrated failure mode and its correction, the sensitivity results, and the operational guidance for planning practice.

### Analysis

Effectiveness is assessed on four criteria: (1) does it answer the question the problem poses — can the environmental cost of a land use project be put into its cost-benefit ratio? Yes, by construction: PV_ES is a USD number that enters the same ratio as any other cost; (2) is it reproducible? Yes — closed-form, one script, command-line constants, no hidden state; (3) is it anchored? Unit values are literature-anchored (Costanza 1997/2014) and the 4x scaling sanity-check reproduces the documented global-value jump; (4) does it expose its own failure modes? Yes — the counterexample consultation (exchange 3) produced a concrete, numerically demonstrated sign-flip failure in the ratio form, and the decision rule was changed to the subtractive NPV* because of it. The model's design strength is that the ES term is separable: planners can insert it into any existing CBA without rebuilding the conventional side. Its design weakness is linearity: value is proportional to area and to the table's averages, which is precisely the structure that biases verdicts toward acceptance for high-impact sites and hides tipping points. The most consequential design decision, fixed by consultation, is that the decision variable is the single project's NPV and the input domain is before/after state change — this keeps the model honest (no temporary-occupancy fudging) at the cost of scope (flow-degradation projects are out).

### Modeling Process

No new equations; this task applies the Task-1 model and its sensitivity sweeps. Effectiveness checks performed: (i) antisymmetry/zero-diagonal of the transition matrix (structural consistency); (ii) delta sweep 0.01/0.03/0.05/0.07 on the reference project — PV_ES from $2.255M to $0.322M, accept verdict invariant; (iii) T sweep 10/25/50/100 — PV_ES from $0.192M to $0.713M, verdict invariant; (iv) table-scale sweep 1x/4x — PV_ES from $0.75M to $3.0M, verdict invariant for the reference project; (v) the restoration counterexample (cropland -> wetland, 50 ha, B $3.0M, PV_mit $0.5M): PV_ES = -$3.67M; subtractive NPV* = +$6.17M (ACCEPT — a service gain should help the project), while the multiplicative CBR = (B-PV_mit)/PV_ES = -0.68 would REJECT it. This is the demonstrated failure: dividing by a quantity that can be negative flips the accept/reject sign. The corrected rule NPV* = B - PV_mit - PV_ES has no such singularity and is used throughout.

### Outcome Analysis

Effectiveness verdict: the model is effective as a screening and negotiation instrument and structurally limited as a site-specific price. It is effective because: the ES cost is a single, auditable USD term; the verdict is robust to the discount rate (7x PV_ES range, same verdict) and to the 1997->2014 unit-value revision (4x, same verdict for the reference project); and it demonstrably changes real verdicts at the scale where the problem says they should change (national land-take projects flip to reject). It is limited because: linear-area pricing cannot represent thresholds, extinction, fragmentation, or non-substitutability; global-average unit values bias site verdicts toward acceptance; perpetuity and a constant 3% social discount rate are debatable (lower delta raises ES cost, so the base case is the cheaper-ES end of the plausible range); and the model inherits the quality of the conventional B it is added to. Implications for planners and managers: (1) require an ES line item in every CBA above a small footprint threshold — the break-even analysis says land-take above roughly 10,000 ha of high-value biome should be expected to flip, and projects between 500 and 5,000 ha should expect the ES term to be a material line (10-25% of B); (2) route large land-take projects through alternatives analysis — rerouting a pipeline to skip wetland or forest biomes is worth the table's per-hectare difference ($6,000 vs $2,780 for pasture), which at national area is worth billions capitalized; (3) price reversibility: a 50-year reversion cuts the reference project's capitalized ES cost by ~23% (T=50: $0.58M vs $0.75M), so temporary-occupency designs are cheap ES mitigation; (4) report gross cumulative ES loss to the public and netted to the ledger — the 2.4% offset example shows how small a restoration program can make a region look 'net zero' while 95% of the conversions stand; (5) use the model as a negotiation floor: the table values are conservative global averages, so a proponent who disputes them must produce site-specific valuation, not just a lower number. How the model must change over time: re-anchor the unit table at each revision of the global valuation literature (1997 -> 2014 was ~4x; the next revision will move it again, and the --scale parameter makes this a one-line update); replace the constant delta with a declining rate to reflect intergenerational equity; add a flow-degradation function for partial-intensity changes (the out-of-scope domain of exchange 1); add spatial interaction terms (fragmentation, adjacency) once landscape data are available; and move from biome averages to site-specific valuation where the project is large enough to justify it. The structural change the consultation forced is already in place: the subtractive decision rule, which no future table revision can break.

## Subtask 4: Task 4 — Record and analyze the model's demonstrated failure mode and the expert-consultation that exposed it, and state

### Problem

Task 4 — Record and analyze the model's demonstrated failure mode and the expert-consultation that exposed it, and state the model's limitations and biases in the form a judge or planner can use. Goal: a named, numerically demonstrated limitation (the ratio-form sign-flip on service-gain projects and the cumulative offsetting problem) with its correction, plus the full bias inventory. Scope: the exchange-3 counterexample, its numerical reproduction, the corrected decision rule, and the gross-vs-netted cumulative reporting.

### Analysis

The consultation followed the prescribed three-exchange structure. Exchange 1 (open-ended) settled the two structural questions the problem leaves open: the ES flow enters as a capitalized present-value cost term (not an undiscounted annual cost, not a soft penalty), the decision variable is the single project's NPV, and the input domain is before/after biome conversion (temporary/partial degradation excluded as a separate flow-degradation problem). Exchange 2 (judgment) proposed the fuller configuration — signed transition table, table-derived service-class weights, perpetual-annuity capitalization, the ratio CBR = (B - PV_mit)/PV_ES as the decision gate, descriptive regional layer — and the expert rejected it; the rejected items were treated as untested rather than true, and the base model dropped the weighting layer and (as exchange 3 showed was necessary) the ratio gate. Exchange 3 (counterexample) asked, against the fallback structure, for the case that breaks it; the expert named the restoration/reversion case: when B_to is richer than B_from, L is negative, PV_ES is negative, and the ratio form sign-flips. The analysis in this task reproduces that case numerically and states the correction. The method is sound because the failure was found by adversarial questioning before any number was trusted, not after, and the correction is structural (subtract instead of divide) rather than a patch.

### Modeling Process

Failure case (expert exch. 3): project = cropland -> wetland restoration, A = 50 ha, B = $3.0M, PV_mit = $0.5M, delta = 0.03. L(4,3) = sum_s [u_s(cropland) - u_s(wetland)] = -2,200 USD/ha/yr (a service gain). F = -110,000 USD/yr. PV_ES = F/delta = -$3.667M. Under the multiplicative rule CBR = (B - PV_mit)/PV_ES = 2.5M / (-3.667M) = -0.682 < 1 -> REJECT, which is logically wrong: a project that both has positive conventional net benefit and creates ecosystem-service value is rejected by the very mechanism meant to reward service value. Under the subtractive rule NPV* = B - PV_mit - PV_ES = 3.0M - 0.5M + 3.667M = +$6.167M > 0 -> ACCEPT, which is correct: the service gain adds value. The same negative PV_ES, summed into a regional ledger, nets against conversion losses: in the 40-road cumulative scenario, 2 x 5-ha restoration offsets (each PV_ES = -2,200*5/0.03 = -$0.367M) reduce the reported regional loss from $30.067M to $29.333M (-2.4%) — correct arithmetic, but it lets a planner report a smaller cumulative loss by pairing conversions with restorations elsewhere, while the biosphere has lost the 38 un-offset conversions. Correction: (i) decision gate is NPV* = B - PV_mit - PV_ES (no division, no sign singularity); (ii) the ratio CBR is reported only for PV_ES > 0 cases, as a conventional-information statistic; (iii) cumulative reporting carries both gross (sum of positive PV_ES) and netted (sum of signed PV_ES) figures, with gross as the headline. All of this is computed by code/es_valuation.py (the 'FAILURE MODE' and 'CUMULATIVE REGIONAL LAYER' sections of logs/es_valuation.log) and stored in results/model_numbers.json.

### Outcome Analysis

The named limitation, in one sentence: a cost-benefit ratio that divides by the ecosystem-service loss is undefined-by-sign for any project that creates service value, and a cumulative ledger that nets gains against losses underreports cumulative degradation; the model corrects the first by switching to the subtractive NPV* gate and the second by dual gross/netted reporting. Numerical proof: the restoration case gives CBR = -0.68 (would reject) vs NPV* = +$6.17M (accepts) for the same project; the cumulative case shows a 2.4% reduction from a small offset program. Why this matters beyond the example: restoration, reversion, and rewilding projects are a growing category of land use 'projects', and any valuation instrument that rejects them by construction will be ignored by the practitioners who need it — the failure mode is not a corner case but a whole class of decision. Full bias inventory: (1) site bias toward acceptance — global-average unit values underprice high-value sites (wetlands, old-growth, carbon-dense forest); (2) linearity bias — no thresholds, tipping points, extinction, fragmentation, or non-substitutability; the model is systematically optimistic exactly where cumulative degradation is most harmful; (3) scale bias from sub-linear B — the break-even at ~10,000 ha moves with the assumed benefit schedule and must be re-computed per project; (4) discount-rate bias — constant 3% is the cheap-ES end of the plausible range (delta = 0.01 triples the reference project's ES cost, verdict unchanged, but for marginal projects the verdict would move); (5) table-revision bias — the 1997 table is ~4x lower than the 2014 update; verdicts computed on stale tables are biased toward acceptance, and the scale parameter makes the update a one-line change; (6) scope bias — temporary occupancy and partial-intensity degradation are out of domain by the exchange-1 ruling, so projects that degrade without converting are unpriced by this model and need the flow-degradation extension; (7) offset bias — netted cumulative figures understate regional loss; gross is the conservative headline. None of these biases is fixable by re-estimating a constant; all but (6) are parameter or reporting choices, and (6) is a declared scope boundary. The consultation's net effect on the deliverable: the capitalized cost term and the state-change domain came from exchange 1; the drop of the weighting layer and the ratio gate came from exchanges 2-3; the subtractive rule and the dual cumulative reporting are the model's load-bearing decisions and are the ones most likely to survive future table revisions.
