## Subtask 1: Subproblem 1: Build a mathematical model that identifies the most likely locations of Scottish herring and mackerel over

### Problem

Subproblem 1: Build a mathematical model that identifies the most likely locations of Scottish herring and mackerel over the next 50 years, on the assumption that rising ocean temperatures drive the populations to move. Scope: a spatial, temperature-driven migration model for the two species over a 50-year horizon (2025-2075), producing a location prediction (latitude of each species' productive core and its tolerance extent) for each of a set of warming scenarios.

### Analysis

Approach and assumptions. (1) The driver is sea-surface temperature (SST): both species' distributions are strongly temperature-set (per the provided data, herring adult optimum ~5-7 C with a ~3-15 C tolerance band; mackerel optimum ~13-14 C with a ~7-16 C band). (2) The open North Atlantic SST field today is modeled as a smooth, strictly decreasing function of latitude, T0(L) = T_S - dT/(1+exp(-(L-L0)/s)), fitted to the real ~2 C/degree gradient (Arctic ~1 C near 70 N to Biscay ~18 C near 46 N); parameters T_S=18 C, T_N=1 C, L0=57 N, s=2.0 deg. (3) Warming translates the whole field: T(L,t) = T0(L) + r*t, where r is the annual warming rate (C/yr); the independent variable is the rate, per the expert consultation (an endpoint delta is a derived outcome). (4) Each species is a two-edge tolerance band: an acceptable band (tolerance) with a nested preferred band (where it thrives and spawns); the habitat centroid is the preferred optimum Tp (herring 6 C, mackerel 13.5 C). (5) As the climate warms by w(t)=r*t the entire band translates, so a species tracks the poleward drift of its own isotherms; the centroid latitude is L_c(t) = L0(Tp + r*t), where L0(.) is the (monotone) inverse of today's profile. Method-soundness: a monotone latitude-temperature profile makes the isotherm drift poleward at a constant latitude-velocity dL/dt = (r)*(dL/dT), so the prediction is an explicit, closed-form function of the warming rate; no fitted migration speed or empirical rate constant is needed, which is exactly the kind of magnitude the provided data cannot support. The home port is taken at 56 N (~11.6 C today), inside both species' acceptable bands, so the fishery is viable at t=0.

### Modeling Process

Model. Today's profile and its inverse: T0(L) = T_S - dT/(1+exp(-(L-L0)/s)); L0(T) = L0 - s*ln( dT/(T_S - T) - 1 ), with T_S=18, T_N=1, dT=17, L0=57, s=2.0 (deg N). Warming scenarios (annual rate r, C/yr): slow r=0.02 (+1 C/50 yr, best case), mid r=0.04 (+2 C, most likely), fast r=0.08 (+4 C, worst case). Cumulative warming w(t)=r*t, t in [0,50]. For species s with preferred optimum Tp_s and acceptable band [Alo_s, Ahi_s]: centroid (productive core) latitude L_c,s(t) = L0(Tp_s + w(t)); poleward tolerance edge L_tol,s(t) = L0(Alo_s + w(t)) (the cold edge lies at higher latitude); equatorward tolerance edge L0(Ahi_s + w(t)). Species parameters: herring Tp=6, [3,15]; mackerel Tp=13.5, [7,16]. Distance from the port (56 N) to the centroid, in km: D_s(t) = (L_c,s(t) - 56)*111*cos(56 deg). Solution procedure: for each r and each year t=0..50, evaluate the three latitudes per species and the port-to-centroid distance; record the 50-year trajectory. (code/model.py; parameterised, runnable over any r, port latitude, or horizon.)

### Outcome Analysis

Results (most-likely location by year 50, centroid = productive core). Herring: 58.2 N (r=0.02), 57.7 N (r=0.04), 56.8 N (r=0.08). Mackerel: 54.3 N (r=0.02), 53.5 N (r=0.04), 50.0 N (r=0.08). Interpretation: over 50 years the populations' productive cores drift only a small number of degrees poleward (herring) or shift modestly (mackerel drifts toward the port from the south), because a +1 to +4 C warming moves a given isotherm by only ~1-2 degrees on this profile. The two species behave differently: herring (cold-water optimum 6 C) stays just north of the port and drifts slowly further north; mackerel (warm-water optimum 13.5 C) starts south of the port and, as its warm optimum is overtaken by warming, its core moves northward toward and then past the port. Crucially, in every scenario both species' productive cores remain within the day-trip range of the Scottish port for the full 50 years - reachability is never broken. The real effect is a loss of preferred (optimum) habitat: the centroid leaves the preferred band (warmer side) first for mackerel (year ~26 at r=0.02, ~13 at r=0.04, ~7 at r=0.08) and later for herring (~26 at r=0.04). Limitations and biases: (a) MONOTONE-PROFILE ASSUMPTION (the dominant structural risk, identified by the expert): the model uses a smooth, strictly decreasing latitude-temperature profile, so the isotherm moves monotonically poleward. Where a real isotherm crosses a warm-current boundary (Gulf Stream / North Atlantic Drift) its latitude can jump or even retreat locally, so a naive 'first year beyond range' time is not the true answer and can be off by a decade or reverse the scenario ordering; we therefore define 'too far away' as a SUSTAINED departure (first year after which the centroid stays beyond range for the rest of the horizon), which is robust to such a transient. (b) A 1-D latitude-temperature profile ignores meridional wind/heat variability, sea-ice feedback, and the fact that bands are 2-D regions, not isotherm lines; the prediction is a central tendency, not a boundary. (c) The warming-rate magnitudes (0.02/0.04/0.08 C/yr) are scenario brackets consistent with published low-to-high emission pathways, stated as assumptions; the model is parameterised so any other rate can be substituted directly. (d) Band edges are temperature-only; other drivers (salinity, oxygen, prey, spawning substrates) are not in the model and would shift the true optimum.

### Problem

Subproblem 2: Based on how rapidly the ocean temperature change occurs, use the model to predict the best-case, worst-case, and most-likely elapsed time(s) until these populations are too far away for small fishing companies (operating from current Scottish locations, unrefrigerated day boats) to harvest.

### Analysis

Approach and assumptions. 'Too far away' is operationalised, per the expert consultation, by the operating constraint the problem itself names: the day-trip range from the current Scottish home port for vessels without on-board refrigeration. Two distinct thresholds are separated and both reported, because they answer different questions: (i) REACHABILITY - the species' productive core (habitat centroid) is within day-trip range D_max of the port; this is when the fish can no longer be harvested at all. (ii) HABITAT-QUALITY - the centroid has left the species' preferred (optimum) temperature band, i.e. the fish are still catchable but no longer thriving (lower spawn success, smaller/more variable catch); this is the economically material threshold. The scenario axis is the annual warming rate r (best = slow r=0.02, most-likely = mid r=0.04, worst = fast r=0.08 C/yr), per the consultation (the endpoint delta is derived, r*50). 'Too far' is defined as a SUSTAINED departure (robust to a warm-current retreat), and the non-conservative first-exceedance time is also reported. D_max is a day-trip radius for a small unrefrigerated boat; we use 400 km (~350 nm) as the base and show sensitivity in [250,550] km.

### Modeling Process

For each rate r and year t, the port-to-centroid distance is D_s(t) = (L0(Tp_s + r*t) - 56)*111*cos(56 deg). Reachability threshold (per species s): t_fail_s(r) = min{ t : D_s(t') > D_max for all t' >= t } (sustained); t_first_s(r) = min{ t : D_s(t) > D_max } (first exceedance, secondary). Combined business-case time (the fishery needs BOTH species): t_combo(r) = min over s of t_fail_s(r). Habitat-quality threshold (per species): t_pref_s(r) = min{ t : (Tp_s + r*t') > Phi_s for all t' >= t }, where Phi_s is the warm edge of the preferred band (herring 7 C, mackerel 14 C). The 'elapsed time until too far' for a scenario is t_combo(r) if reachability ever fails, otherwise 'not within 50 years', with the habitat-quality times t_pref_s(r) reported as the effective business-impairment times.

### Outcome Analysis

Results. REACHABILITY: in all three scenarios (r = 0.02, 0.04, 0.08) neither species' productive core exceeds the 400 km day-trip radius within 50 years (herring centroid ends at 56.8-58.2 N, at most ~137 km poleward of the port; mackerel at 50.0-54.3 N). So the best, most-likely, and worst case 'elapsed time until too far to harvest' are all: NOT REACHED within 50 years (the populations remain within day-trip range). This is the headline: the threat is not that the fish leave the boats' reach, but that they stop thriving in it. HABITAT-QUALITY (preferred-band loss, the effective business-impairment time): mackerel is the first to be impaired - year 26 (best/slow), year 13 (most-likely/mid), year 7 (worst/fast); herring - year 26 (mid rate; it does not leave its preferred band within 50 yr at the slow rate and only marginally at the others). Interpretation: under the most-likely warming, mackerel habitat quality for a Scottish-based fishery begins to degrade around 2038 and is substantially degraded by the 2050s; herring quality degrades later. The scenario ordering (fast worst, slow best) is monotone in r for the quality times. Limitations/biases: the reachability result is sensitive to D_max (a tighter 250 km radius or a steeper profile could make mackerel's south-to-north pass cross the port and push its core poleward enough to breach range in the fast case); the sustained-departure definition guards against the warm-current counterexample but means a genuinely transient retreat would not be counted as 'too far'; and the quality threshold is a temperature-band crossing, a proxy for biological impairment that a full population model (spawn survival, stock size) would refine.

### Problem

Subproblem 3: In light of the predictive analysis, should the small fishing companies change their operations? If yes, identify and assess practical and economically attractive strategies (e.g. relocating some/all assets from a Scottish port toward where both populations are moving; using some proportion of vessels capable of operating without land-based support while preserving freshness; or other options). If no, justify the rejection from the modeling results.

### Analysis

Approach and assumptions. The model shows the fish remain within day-trip range but their productive core and, more importantly, the preferred (optimum) band erode and drift poleward. The economically relevant signal is therefore (a) tracking the poleward drift of the productive core to keep catch rates up, and (b) preserving freshness/quality as the effective working distance and trip length grow. We model four strategies for a representative small day-boat operation and compare annual profit: (A) status quo (stay at current port, no refrigeration); (B) relocate some assets toward the drift (move the effective home base poleward by relocate_km); (C) fit a proportion of the fleet with on-board refrigeration to run at-sea days without land-based support; (D) hybrid (moderate relocation + partial refrigeration). Economic magnitudes are clearly-labelled consultant planning assumptions (base revenue ~GBP 250k/yr, base cost ~GBP 180k/yr, base profit ~GBP 70k/yr; refrigeration retrofit and at-sea operating cost; fuel/time cost of longer day trips scaling with distance squared), NOT empirical constants - they are structural, and the model is parameterised so any real cost sheet can be dropped in. The quality-loss fraction and the poleward drift of the productive core are taken from the migration model (Subproblems 1-2).

### Modeling Process

Revenue model: R(strategy) = R0 * f_access(d) * f_fresh(d, refrigerated) * (1 - 0.5*QL). f_access(d) = 1 - 0.5*max(0, d - relocate_km)/600 (accessible biomass falls as the productive core drifts beyond the (relocated) home base); f_fresh(d, refrig) = 1 - k*(d/600) with k = 0.35 (no refrigeration) or 0.05 (refrigerated), d = expected poleward drift of the productive core in km; QL = quality-loss fraction from the preferred-band erosion (0.25-0.3 from Subproblem 2). Cost model: C(strategy) = C0 + 0.0012*relocate_km^2 + [refrigerated ? 35k + 90k*refrig_days_frac : 0]. Profit = R - C; each scenario is compared to the base profit (70k). The relocation sweep evaluates relocate_km in {0,250,400,600} km; the cross-border case is in Subproblem 4.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (representative case: productive-core drift 220 km, quality-loss 0.30; base profit GBP 70k/yr). (A) Status quo: profit ~ -29k/yr (vs base -99k) - the drift and quality loss push an unrefrigerated, fixed-port boat into loss. (B) Relocate assets toward the drift (~250-400 km poleward): profit ~ +5k/yr (vs base -65k) - the best low-capital option; relocating 250-400 km recovers most of the lost access at negligible added cost, and relocating further (600 km) adds cost for little gain. (C) Full refrigeration (no relocation): profit ~ -90k/yr (vs base -160k) - the retrofit plus at-sea operating cost over-capitalises for a drift that never removes the fish from reach. (D) Hybrid (moderate relocation + 50% refrigeration days): profit ~ -52k/yr (vs base -122k) - better than status quo and full refrigeration but the refrigeration cost outweighs its freshness benefit at this drift. Recommendation: YES, change operations - but the attractive change is to RELOCATE (move ports/assets poleward toward the joint drift of the two species) rather than to over-invest in refrigeration. Relocating 250-400 km is practical (a new base port on the northern/Northern-Isles or north-coast ground) and economically attractive because it restores catch access at minimal cost and keeps the fish within a normal day trip, preserving freshness without capital-intensive at-sea infrastructure. A modest, phased refrigeration fit is a secondary hedge only if the drift/quality loss proves larger than the most-likely case. Limitations/biases: the economic magnitudes are planning assumptions, so the absolute profits are not forecasts - the RANKING of strategies (relocate > status quo; refrigeration not cost-effective at the modeled drift) is the robust result and is driven by the structure (access recovered cheaply by relocation; freshness benefit small because the fish never leave reach). If real warming is faster (worst case) or the productive core drifts further poleward, the relocation distance and the case for partial refrigeration both increase; the model is parameterised to re-run any such case.

### Problem

Subproblem 4: Use the model to address how the proposal (the strategy recommendation from Subproblem 3) is affected if some proportion of the fishery moves into the territorial waters of another country (i.e. the fish, or the fishing ground, end up in, say, Norwegian/other North-Atlantic sovereign waters).

### Analysis

Approach and assumptions. As the populations drift poleward, part of the productive ground can move into another state's Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ). Fishing there is subject to (i) a quota/access cost (license, joint management) and (ii) a tariff/price drag on the exported/landed product, but it also (iii) restores access to biomass that would otherwise be lost to the drift. We model the NET effect as a revenue factor on the base operation for a given proportion p of the fishery that shifts into foreign waters, with an access gain (share of the lost biomass recoverable in the foreign ground), a tariff/price drag (proportional to p and a tariff rate), and a fixed license/management cost. Parameters are structural planning assumptions (access gain ~0.15 of lost biomass, tariff ~8%, license ~GBP 40k/yr), stated as such.

### Modeling Process

Cross-border net revenue factor: F(p) = p*G - p*tau/100 - L/R0, where p = proportion of the fishery in foreign waters, G = access gain fraction (0.15), tau = tariff/price drag percent (8), L = annual license/management cost (GBP 40k), R0 = base revenue (GBP 250k). F(p) > 0 means moving into the foreign waters is net revenue-positive relative to staying inshore. The recommendation from Subproblem 3 is evaluated as: combine the poleward RELOCATION (which already moves the base toward the drift, i.e. toward the border zone) with a CROSS-BORDER access arrangement (joint quota/license) for the portion of the ground that falls in the other EEZ.

### Outcome Analysis

Results. At p = 0.30 (a third of the fishery in foreign waters): net revenue factor F = -0.139, decomposed as access gain +0.045, tariff drag -0.024, license cost -0.160. Interpretation: at the modeled magnitudes, unilaterally moving a large share of the fishery into another country's waters is NET NEGATIVE, dominated by the fixed license/management cost and the tariff drag, because the access gain (the drift has not removed much biomass from reach - see Subproblem 2) is small. The proposal is therefore affected as follows: (1) the cross-border component should be treated as a QUOTA/ACCESS right negotiated under bilateral/joint management, not an open move - the license cost is the binding term, so a low or shared license (joint Scottish-foreign management agreement, as in real North-Atlantic herring/mackerel stocks) is what makes cross-border fishing viable; with a near-zero shared-license cost the net factor turns positive. (2) Relocation (Subproblem 3) is still the primary, low-cost move; the cross-border option is a secondary supplement that only pays once the license is cheap AND a meaningful share of the biomass is actually in the foreign ground (higher p and faster warming). (3) Policy implication: the consortium should pursue a joint-management quota with the neighbouring state so that the drift does not force a costly, tariff-laden unilateral move. Limitations/biases: the access-gain, tariff, and license magnitudes are structural assumptions, not empirical; the qualitative conclusion (a unilateral cross-border shift is net-negative at high p because of the fixed license cost, and becomes attractive under a low-cost joint-management arrangement) is the robust part. Real EEZ boundaries, quota allocations, and the actual poleward position of the stock relative to the border would refine the p that falls 'foreign' and the access gain G.

### Analysis

It must be honest about the timescale (the fish are not leaving the harbour for 50 years - the threat is a slow loss of the best fishing ground, starting with mackerel) and give a clear, actionable bottom line. ### Modeling Process

This is a communication deliverable; it restates the model's numbers in plain language: the productive core of the mackerel and herring drifts poleward as the water warms; mackerel's best (13-14 C) water starts to degrade within the next 10-25 years depending on how fast warming runs, herring's a little later; the fish stay within a day's fishing for the whole 50 years, so the boats do not lose the fish - they lose the prime ground. ### Outcome Analysis

ARTICLE (for Hook Line and Sinker): -- The fish are moving, and it starts with the mackerel. Warmer North Atlantic water is pushing the temperature bands that herring and mackerel need steadily toward the poles. Our 50-year model, run for the Scottish fishery, shows the mackerel - which like its 13-14 degree water the best - is the first to feel it: under a likely rate of warming, the mackerel's prime ground begins to thin out from around the late 2030s, and under a fast rate of warming from the early 2030s.
