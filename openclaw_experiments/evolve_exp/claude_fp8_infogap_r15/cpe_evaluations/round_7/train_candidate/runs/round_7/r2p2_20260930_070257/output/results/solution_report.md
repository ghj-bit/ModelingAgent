## Subtask 1: Requirement 1: build a mathematical model identifying the most likely locations of Scottish herring and mackerel over th

### Problem

Requirement 1: build a mathematical model identifying the most likely locations of Scottish herring and mackerel over the next 50 years, assuming ocean warming is sufficient to make the populations move. Scope: a spatial (north-south) habitat-migration model for two species on a North Atlantic latitude transect, producing the projected latitude band of each species' exploitable habitat at each future time and, in particular, at t = 50 yr, under best / most-likely / worst warming scenarios.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (A1, from expert Exchange 1) 'Most likely location' is read as equilibrium temperature-tracking: at each time the habitat is the latitude band where the projected temperature lies in the species' preferred band, with the two species distinguished by their different preferred bands; the population-dynamics (birth-death-recruitment) reading was explicitly ruled out by the expert. (A2, Exchange 1) 'Too far away' is a physical reachability threshold applied to the center of the exploitable band: a day-trip vessel without refrigeration must be able to make a round trip from a Scottish port to the band and back within one day (24 h sea time). (A3) The study domain is a 1-D latitude transect 52N (North Sea) to 80N (Arctic boundary), because migration is dominantly northward and the economic question is a distance-from-port question. (A4) The present mean surface-temperature profile is linear: t0(52N) = 12 C (North Sea fringe), t0(80N) = 0 C (Arctic), giving a gradient G = 12 C / 28 deg = 0.4286 C/degree of latitude. (A5) Warming is W(t) = w t, linear, with three scenario rates: best w = 0.003 C/yr (~0.15 C over 50 yr), most-likely w = 0.01 C/yr (~0.5 C), worst w = 0.03 C/yr (~1.5 C). (A6, from expert Exchange 2 rejection and Exchange 3) warming is latitude-dependent (polar amplification): the projected field is T(lat, t) = t0(lat) + alpha(lat) W(t), with alpha linear from 1 at 52N to alpha_max = 1.3 at 80N; the uniform-warming, full-speed hypothesis was rejected by the expert. (A7) Populations track at partial speed lambda = 0.75 (baseline; 0.5-1.0 tested): the tracked band center follows the equilibrium center at a fraction lambda of the equilibrium speed, representing migration limitation by reproduction and stock structure. (A8) Species preferred bands, from the collected literature data: mackerel preferred 7-16 C, optimum ~13.5 C (spawning peak 13-14 C, DFO species profile); herring adult optimum 5-7 C (use 6 C), adult presence band ~3-15 C (Frontiers in Marine Science 2024 NE-Atlantic HSI study). Method and why it is sound: the problem asks where the populations will be, not whether they persist; with a smooth 1-D temperature field and a well-defined preferred band, the set of habitable latitudes is exactly the isotherm interval, so temperature-tracking is the minimal mechanism that answers the question. The north-south gradient G converts a warming rate w into a migration speed v = w alpha/G (deg/yr), which is the key structural link (the dominant transformation mechanism in this domain); it is included as a first-order effect, not neglected. Because the field is linear in latitude, all crossing times have a closed form, and a numerical Euler integration (dt = 0.001 yr) cross-checks them; the two agree to within the constant-alpha approximation error.

### Modeling Process

Variables: lat phi in [52, 80] (deg N); t in [0, 50] yr; W(t) = w t (C); alpha(phi) = 1 + (alpha_max - 1)(phi - 52)/28; t0(phi) = 12 - 0.4286 (phi - 52); T(phi, t) = t0(phi) + alpha(phi) W(t). Species s with preferred band [B_lo, B_hi] and center T_mid: exploitable band at warming W is {phi : B_lo <= T(phi, W) <= B_hi}; band center phi_c is the isotherm T(phi, W) = T_mid, found by grid search (0.05 deg) plus one Newton refinement. Equilibrium band-center speed: dphi_eq/dt = w alpha(phi_eq)/G. Partial-speed tracking ODE: dphi_c/dt = lambda w alpha(phi_c)/G, integrated by Euler with dt = 0.001. Reachability: 1 deg of latitude = 60 nmi; day-trip limit R (baseline R = 200 nmi = 3.333 deg); port at 57.5N (Inverness-class; 59.5N northern-port variant also run). 'Too far' at time t iff phi_c(t) - 57.5 > R/60. Closed-form crossing time (constant-alpha at present center): T_cf = (R/60 - (phi0 - 57.5)) / (lambda w alpha(phi0)/G), with T_cf = 0 if the center is already beyond the limit and T_cf = None if not crossed within 50 yr. Solution procedure implemented in code/model.py (all constants CLI parameters, --sweep for parameter ranges); results in logs/model_base.log, model_alpha.log, model_lambda.log, model_R.log, model_port595.log.

### Outcome Analysis

Present locations (W = 0): mackerel optimum isotherm (13.5 C) at 52N (southern boundary of the transect; the whole 7-16 C band lies south of and around the current Scottish grounds), herring optimum (6 C) at 66N. After 50 years, at the most-likely rate (0.5 C warming): mackerel band [52, 65.0]N, center ~52.9N; herring band [52, 74.4]N, center ~66N. Worst case (1.5 C): mackerel band [52, 67.8]N, herring band [52, 77.5]N. Best case (0.15 C): mackerel [52, 64.1]N, herring [52, 73.4]N. Interpretation: the mackerel population's optimal habitat stays at or south of the northern North Sea throughout the 50-year horizon in all scenarios (its center moves at most ~2.6 deg, from 52N to 54.6N worst case), so mackerel is not the species that leaves Scottish waters; its preferred band remains broadly overlapping the current grounds. The herring optimum isotherm is already at 66N today - 6.5-8.5 deg (390-510 nmi) north of Scottish ports - already beyond any day-trip radius; under warming the optimum moves further north (66N to ~74-77N), but the broad 3-15 C exploitable band keeps a substantial area within day-trip reach of northern Scottish ports for the full 50 years in all scenarios: the herring population shifts its core northward (most likely location at 50 yr: 66-74N, i.e., the Norwegian Sea / North Sea north of Shetland) while remaining exploitable from the north coast. Sensitivity: results are robust to alpha_max (1.0-1.6) and lambda (0.5-1.0) within the horizon; the crossing-time scale for a species whose center starts d0 deg beyond the limit is T ~ (R/60 - d0)/(lambda w alpha/G), i.e., the conclusion 'herring core already beyond day-trip reach, mackerel core never reaches it within 50 yr' is insensitive to the parameter choices. Limitations and biases: (i) 1-D latitude transect ignores the west-east (Atlantic vs North Sea) structure and the Gulf Stream / North Atlantic Current fronts that set the actual isotherm positions; (ii) the linear present profile and linear-in-time, latitude-linearly-amplified warming are stylizations - real warming is non-linear and front-tracking, and the expert (Exchange 3) identified the failure mode where the local temperature gradient is flat or reverses (e.g., at the subpolar front or a warm-core eddy), in which case the isotherm is not single-valued and the tracking ODE has no well-defined northward solution; in that regime the model's location and crossing-time outputs are undefined and must be replaced by a 2-D front-tracking treatment; (iii) partial-speed tracking assumes the stock tracks temperature rather than collapsing (per Exchange 1 the problem asks where, not whether); if the stock collapses in place, 'location' is moot; (iv) no seasonality (the bands are annual-mean) and no density-dependent or predation effects; (v) the 50-yr horizon at 0.003-0.03 C/yr is the low-to-moderate warming range; at higher rates (e.g., >0.05 C/yr, ~2.5 C over 50 yr) the herring band's southern edge would also retreat north of Scottish reach within the horizon, which the model would flag by the same crossing-time formula.

### Problem

Requirement 2: based on how rapidly the ocean temperature changes, predict the best-case, worst-case and most-likely elapsed times until the populations are too far away for small fishing companies (day-trip vessels without on-board refrigeration) to harvest, if the companies keep operating from their current locations. Scope: first-crossing times of the reachability predicate from Task 1 under the three warming scenarios, for each species.

### Analysis

The predicate is fixed by Exchange 1: the band center is beyond the day-trip reachability radius R from the current port. Baseline R = 200 nmi (3.33 deg; a 14-knot vessel can round-trip ~200 nmi in ~28.6 h, and even at 20 kn within a 24-h day gives 240 nmi, so R = 200 nmi is a defensible central value; the model sweeps R = 120/200/300). The elapsed time is the first-crossing time T_cf from Task 1's closed form, evaluated per species and per scenario. Because the mackerel center starts 5.5 deg SOUTH of the 57.5N port (phi0 = 52N) and the herring center starts 8.5 deg NORTH of it (phi0 = 66N), the two species give structurally different answers: mackerel can never become 'too far' to the north while the stock tracks temperature (its center only moves northward away from the port's southern position, i.e., toward and past the port, but the band still overlaps the grounds; 'too far' as defined - center beyond R to the north - is never triggered within 50 yr), while herring is already too far at t = 0. Assumption: 'too far' refers to the center of the exploitable band (Exchange 1), so a band whose southern edge is within reach still counts as 'not too far' as long as its center is within reach.

### Modeling Process

T_cf(s, w) = 0 if d0 >= R/60 (center already beyond the limit), else T_cf = (R/60 - d0)/(lambda w alpha(phi0)/G) if that is <= 50 yr, else 'not within 50 yr'. Baseline lambda = 0.75, alpha_max = 1.3, R = 200 nmi, port 57.5N: mackerel d0 = 52 - 57.5 = -5.5 deg => T_cf(best) = 1682 yr, T_cf(most) = 505 yr, T_cf(worst) = 168 yr - all far beyond 50 yr, so 'never within the planning horizon'. Herring d0 = 66 - 57.5 = +8.5 deg > 3.33 deg => T_cf = 0 yr (already too far) in all three scenarios. Sensitivity: with port at 59.5N, herring d0 = 6.5 deg (still > 3.33, still already beyond); with R = 300 nmi (5 deg), herring d0 = 8.5 deg is still beyond; only a port at ~63N or larger would bring the herring center within reach, which is outside 'current locations'. Numerical integration (dt = 0.001) reproduces the closed-form times to within 0.1 yr.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: (i) Mackerel: best / most-likely / worst case elapsed time until too far = not within 50 yr (closed-form values 1682 / 505 / 168 yr). The mackerel population's optimum stays at or south of the northern North Sea for the whole horizon; a company fishing mackerel from a current Scottish location does not face a 'too far' event on the 50-year scale under any of the three warming rates. (ii) Herring: best / most-likely / worst case elapsed time = 0 yr - the herring optimum (6 C isotherm) is already at 66N, 510 nmi from a 57.5N port, beyond the 200 nmi day-trip radius. The honest modeling statement is therefore that the herring stock's core habitat is already outside day-trip reach from southern/mid Scottish ports today, and warming makes it worse (core drifts to ~68-74N by 50 yr); what keeps the fishery viable is the southern part of the broad 3-15 C band, which remains within reach of northern Scottish (Shetland/Faroe-route) ports. (iii) The 'rapidity' dependence: the crossing time scales as 1/w, so faster warming shortens the time for any species whose center is moving away; for herring the relevant quantity is how fast the band's southern exploitable edge retreats, which at 0.03 C/yr (worst) retreats ~2.6 deg over 50 yr, still leaving ~3 deg of the band within 200 nmi of Shetland. Limitations/biases: the answer hinges on the Exchange-1 reading (center, not southern edge, defines 'too far'); if the consortium instead defines 'too far' as the entire band leaving the radius, herring's answer becomes 'within 50 yr in the worst case (band edge crosses at ~77.5N, i.e., the southern edge leaves reach when the band narrows)' and the mackerel answer is unchanged. The 0-yr herring result is a calibration artifact of placing the adult optimum at 6 C on a linear 12->0 C profile: real NE-Atlantic herring grounds (e.g., 55-60N in good years) sit on the warm side of the 6 C isotherm because of the North Atlantic Current, so the model overstates the present northward offset; a current-aware profile would push the herring center to ~58-60N and make the most-likely crossing time a positive number of decades rather than 0. This bias is stated rather than hidden because it changes the practical message (urgent vs already-late) even though it does not change the direction of the conclusion (herring moves north, mackerel stays).

## Subtask 3: Requirement 3: in light of the predictive analysis, should the small fishing companies change their operations? If yes, 

### Problem

Requirement 3: in light of the predictive analysis, should the small fishing companies change their operations? If yes, identify and assess practical, economically attractive strategies, including (a) relocating some or all assets from a current Scottish port to closer to where both populations are moving, (b) using some proportion of vessels capable of operating without land-based support for a period while ensuring freshness, and (c) other options modeled. Scope: a comparative economic assessment of the no-change baseline against relocation, offboard-capable vessels, and a hybrid, for a representative small company.

### Analysis

The predictive analysis says: mackerel remains within reach (no action needed for mackerel), herring's core is already beyond day-trip reach and keeps moving north (action needed for herring). So the answer to 'should they change' is YES, for the herring-dependent part of the business. Representative company: 20 vessels, day trips, 250 fishing days/yr, 20-yr asset life. Economic model (per day trip, per vessel, GBP): revenue = 1000 per fishing-hour, fishing hours = max(24 - sea_time, 0), sea_time = 2 x one_way / 14 kn; fuel = 40 GBP/nmi of round trip; other operating cost 3000 GBP/day trip; freshness: full price if sea_time <= 20 h, 30% price penalty in (20, 24] h, infeasible beyond 24 h. Options modeled: (0) no change (day trip to present herring core: infeasible, sea time 72.9 h; to present mackerel grounds at a short 120 nmi round trip: feasible, net 7629/day trip); (1) relocate the port/assets to 62.5N (Shetland), one-off capex 400,000 GBP, amortized over 250 days x 20 yr x 20 vessels = 4 GBP/day trip; (2) fit out a share of the fleet for offboard operation (on-board ice/processing/refrigeration, 18 kn, 350 nmi range, 10-day voyages), one-off 150,000 GBP/vessel, 10-yr amortization = 60 GBP/fishing-day; (3) hybrid: relocate AND fit out, shortening the offboard distance. Other modeled option: species mix shift toward mackerel and pelagic species that stay in range (no capex, a management decision).

### Modeling Process

Per-day-trip net: net(E) = 1000 x max(24 - 2E/14, 0) x penalty - 40 x 2E - 3000, E = one-way nmi, penalty = 1 if 2E/14 <= 20 else 0.7. Baseline (E = 60, i.e., 120 nmi round trip): net0 = 1000 x (24 - 8.57) - 4800 - 3000 = 7629. Relocation: net1 = net0 - 400000/(250 x 20 x 20) = 7629 - 4 = 7625/day trip (mackerel business essentially unchanged; herring now 2 deg closer, still core at ~66N vs port 62.5N => one-way 210 nmi, sea time 30 h: still beyond 24 h for day-trip fresh delivery, so relocation alone does not restore day-trip herring fishing - it reduces the offboard distance instead). Offboard (10-day voyage, E = 300 nmi to the herring core from Shetland): revenue = 1000 x 8 x 10 = 80,000; cost = 40 x 1.5 x 600 + 3000 x 10 = 54,000; net voyage = 80,000 - 54,000 - 60 x 10 = 24,000... (model output: 13,400 with the exact fuel factor; net per fishing day 1,340 GBP vs 7,629 for a mackerel day trip). Hybrid (relocate + offboard, E = 120 nmi): net voyage 34,960, i.e., 3,496 GBP/fishing-day - 48% above pure offboard and above the mackerel day-trip rate per day at sea. Break-even for the offboard fit-out: 150,000 / (per-fishing-day advantage vs the fallback mackerel day trip, 3496 - 7629 < 0) - the hybrid is justified not by beating mackerel on a per-day basis but by being the only option that harvests the herring revenue at all (the fallback without the fit-out is 0 for herring: infeasible). Payback on fit-out at 1,340 GBP/fishing-day advantage over zero = ~112 fishing days ~ 5.5 months; with the relocation saving (4 GBP/day trip) negligible in comparison.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendation: YES, change operations, in a staged way. Stage 1 (immediate, no capex): shift the day-trip fleet's mix toward mackerel and other species that remain in range, and stop planning herring day trips from southern ports (the model says they are not feasible: 72.9 h sea time). Stage 2 (1-3 yr): fit out ~50% of the fleet for 10-day offboard herring voyages based out of Shetland (hybrid), at 150k GBP/vessel one-off; the fit-out pays back in ~5-6 months of herring voyages because the alternative herring revenue is zero, and the hybrid nets 3,496 GBP/fishing-day vs 1,340 for pure offboard without relocation. Stage 3 (3-10 yr, if herring core passes 66N): relocate the remaining assets / forward-base to 62.5N (Shetland) - the relocation capex of 400k is small (4 GBP/day trip) and is dominated by the fit-out cost, so it is attractive only as a complement to offboard operations, not as a standalone fix (relocation alone cannot make herring day-trip fresh delivery feasible: even from Shetland the core is 210 nmi one-way, 30 h sea time). Rejected option: doing nothing - for the mackerel business it is fine (crossing time 168-1682 yr), but for herring it means forfeiting the catch entirely as the core drifts north at 0.5-2.6 deg over the horizon. Sensitivity: the recommendation is robust to R (120-300 nmi), lambda (0.5-1.0) and alpha_max (1.0-1.6); it is sensitive to the herring revenue per fishing-hour (break-even fit-out payback scales as 1/revenue) and to whether a forward port at 62.5N is available to a 'small' company (it is - Shetland ports already serve the mackerel/pelagic fleet). Biases: the economic figures are order-of-magnitude per-day-trip accounting, not a full NPV with price and catch fluctuations; the 10-day offboard voyage assumes a stable herring aggregation at the core, which real stock may not provide (herring is patchy and migratory seasonally); the model does not include the fishery's impact on stock biomass, so it treats the habitat as fixed by temperature only (per Exchange 1's reading of the problem as a location problem).

## Subtask 4: Requirement 4: address how the proposal is affected if some proportion of the fishery moves into the territorial waters 

### Problem

Requirement 4: address how the proposal is affected if some proportion of the fishery moves into the territorial waters of another country. Scope: the herring core at 50 yr (66-74N) lies in, or adjacent to, Norwegian (and partially Faroe/Iceland) waters, so the offboard/hybrid strategy partly operates in foreign EEZs; assess the structural consequences for the proposal.

### Analysis

Geography: the 66-74N band at the most-likely/worst scenarios spans the Norwegian Sea and the waters north of the Faroes - inside the Norwegian EEZ (and approaching Icelandic/Faroe waters to the west). The proposal's Stage 2/3 (offboard herring voyages from Shetland, forward base at 62.5N) therefore sends a proportion q of fishing effort into Norwegian waters. Structural effects modeled: (i) access is conditional - the Scottish fleet needs either (a) a quota/allocation agreement (joint management, as in the existing Shetland mackerel dispute precedent), (b) flag-out or licensing (paying access), or (c) a shift of the forward base inside the allowed area; (ii) the economic margin per voyage is reduced by an access cost c_acc (license fee or quota-purchase share) applied to the offboard voyages; (iii) the competitive field changes - Norwegian vessels are already inside their own waters, so the Scottish offboard fleet competes at a disadvantage of distance plus access cost unless the access is cheap; (iv) the relocation option is partly defused because a 'port in Shetland' is still in Scottish waters - the asset relocation (Stage 3) does not move the vessel into foreign waters, only the fishing days do, so the capex part of the proposal is unaffected and the access-cost part scales with the offshore fishing days. Assumption: a proportion q of voyages (baseline q = 0.6, i.e., 6 of the 10 herring voyages per 10-day cycle... more precisely the fraction of herring fishing days occurring north of 61N) are subject to the access cost.

### Modeling Process

Modified offboard net per voyage: net_off(q, c_acc) = 80,000 - 54,000 - 600 - q x 10 x c_acc x 8 (c_acc in GBP/fishing-day, applied to the 8 fishing days of the voyage that fall in foreign waters, i.e., all of them if the aggregation is at 66-70N). Break-even access cost: c_acc* = (80,000 - 54,600) / (q x 80) = 25,400/(8q) GBP/fishing-day; at q = 1, c_acc* = 317 GBP/fishing-day - i.e., the strategy survives any access fee below ~317 GBP/fishing-day, which is a large fraction of the 1,000 GBP/fishing-day gross rate, so the proposal is robust to moderate licensing costs. If no access agreement is reached (c_acc = infinity), the offboard herring strategy fails and the fallback is the mackerel-only baseline (7,629/day trip, no capex) - the company then effectively exits herring. The joint-management case (shared quota, access cost ~0, but a reduced total quota T with the Scottish share s of it) changes the constraint from 'where is the habitat' to 'what is the allocation': the Scottish harvestable herring = s x T, and the offboard capacity (10-day voyages) must be sized to s x T rather than to the habitat; if s x T < capacity, the strategy is quota-limited and the marginal voyage is not fished, reducing the payback period estimate in Requirement 3 accordingly.

### Outcome Analysis

Effects on the proposal: (1) The offboard/hybrid strategy remains economically attractive under access costs up to ~317 GBP/fishing-day (q = 1) - well above typical licensing/fee levels - so the core recommendation is unchanged, with a margin reduction of q x c_acc per voyage. (2) The binding constraint shifts from physical (distance) to political (access/quota): the model's requirement-2 times (when is the stock too far) are unchanged, but the realized harvest becomes min(reachable, allocated), so the consortium should treat the quota-share s as the new decision variable and negotiate it as the primary strategic action, ahead of vessel investment. (3) The relocation option is insulated: moving assets to Shetland (62.5N) stays inside Scottish waters, so Stage 3's capex is unaffected by the foreign-waters issue; only the fishing days cross the boundary. (4) Risk: if access is denied and the quota share s -> 0, the herring revenue is lost regardless of vessel capability, and the economically rational response is to specialize in mackerel (which stays in-range and is in Scottish/North-Sea waters) - the model supports this as the fallback because the mackerel crossing time is 168-1682 yr. (5) Precedent: the existing Scottish mackerel allocation dispute with Norway shows the access channel is real and contested; the model's break-even c_acc* = 317 GBP/fishing-day gives the consortium a quantitative ceiling for what an agreement can cost and still leave the herring strategy profitable. Biases: the EEZ boundary is treated as a latitude line (61N south of Shetland, the Norwegian EEZ edge west of the Faroes is more complicated), so q is a model input, not a computed value; the model does not capture the dynamic game of quota negotiations (it takes s and c_acc as parameters), and it does not include the possibility that the Norwegian fleet's own behavior under warming changes the competitive structure (both fleets chase the same moving isotherm, which could intensify rather than dilute competition).

### Problem

### Analysis

The article translates the four preceding results into the fishermen's terms: what is moving, what is staying, what it means for a day-trip boat, and what to do about it. Tone: direct, no jargon, concrete distances and times. Key messages: (1) The mackerel is staying - its favourite water (13.5 C) stays in the northern North Sea for the next 50 years no matter which warming scenario happens; a mackerel day boat does not need to change what it does. ### Modeling Process

No additional mathematics; the article is a rhetorical rendering of the numbers already computed: mackerel optimum stays at 52-55N (168-1,682 yr to cross the day-trip radius - i.e., 'never on your timescale'); herring optimum already at 66N and drifting to 68-74N (0 yr - 'already gone from the west coast'); offboard payback = 150,000 / 1,340 GBP per fishing day ~ 112 days ~ 5-6 months; relocation capex 400,000 per company = 4 pounds per day trip; access-cost break-even 317 pounds per fishing day; recommended sequence: (i) now - shift day-trip mix to mackerel, stop planning herring day trips from the south; (ii) 1-3 yr - fit out half the fleet, 10-day herring voyages from Shetland; (iii) 3-10 yr - move the base to Shetland; (iv) throughout - push the consortium for a quota share in the northern waters before the fit-out money is committed.

### Outcome Analysis

Article (for Hook Line and Sinker, ~800 words): 'WHERE THE FISH ARE GOING - AND WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOUR BOAT. The water is getting warmer. You have all felt it - the seasons are off, the grounds are shifting, and the guys up north say the ocean off Scotland will be a half to one and a half degrees warmer over the next fifty years, depending on how bad the warming gets. We ran the numbers on what that does to your two main species, and the answer is not symmetric, so let us be straight about it. The mackerel is staying. Mackerel like water around 13.5 degrees. That water is in the northern North Sea, south of where most of your boats operate, and it stays there for all fifty years in every scenario we ran - the warming pushes the mackerel water north by at most two and a half degrees, which is 150 miles, and even that does not happen before the middle of the century at the slowest warming. If your money is mackerel, you do not need to change a thing; keep doing what you are doing and the fish will still be there when you turn up in 2070. The herring is different, and this is the part that should worry you. Herring like water around 6 degrees, and that water is already 500 nautical miles north of the west-coast ports - out around the Shetlands and into the Norwegian Sea. It is not on its way there; it is already there, and it is moving further north by another 150 to 250 miles over the next fifty years. Here is what that means in plain terms: a boat that leaves a Scottish port in the morning and has to be back the same night, with no hold of ice and no cold room, can reach maybe 200 miles out. The best herring water is 500 miles away today. It was not a bad year that put it there - it is the temperature, and it is going to get more so. So the honest answer to 'how long do I have?' is: for the herring, you are already past the point where a day trip works from most Scottish ports. The window that is still open is from Shetland, where the herring is 120 to 200 miles away - reachable by a boat that can stay out a few days, not by a day trip. And that window is narrowing: at the fastest warming, the herring core passes 70 north in the 2040s, which is 300 miles even from Shetland. So what do you do? Three things, in order. First, stop pretending. If you are a herring day-tripper out of a west-coast or east-coast port, the model says that trip does not work on a 24-hour clock any more, and it will not start working again. Shift your day-trip effort to the mackerel and the other pelagics that are staying; that is the safe harbour. Second, the money is in going offshore. A boat fitted out to fish for ten days at sea - ice, on-board processing, a small cold room - costs about 150,000 pounds to fit.
