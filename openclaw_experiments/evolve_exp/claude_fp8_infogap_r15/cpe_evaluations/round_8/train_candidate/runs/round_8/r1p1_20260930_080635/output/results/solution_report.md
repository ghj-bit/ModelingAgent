## Subtask 1: Subtask 1 — Define the charitable return-on-investment (ROI) and the meaning of 'student performance' for the Goodgrant 

### Problem

Subtask 1 — Define the charitable return-on-investment (ROI) and the meaning of 'student performance' for the Goodgrant Foundation. The problem asks for an ROI 'defined in a manner appropriate for a charitable organization' and a strategy to maximize the likelihood of a strong positive effect on student performance, but the data file contains only outcome levels (10-year median earnings, share earning >$25k, retention, completion, debt), not treatment effects. Goal: settle the estimand, the valid domain of 'performance', the logical form of ROI, and the structural domain of the investment duration. Scope: the conceptual definitions that all subsequent ranking and allocation depend on.

### Analysis

Assumptions, fixed by expert consultation (Exchange 1, Operator 1, open-ended) rather than invented from the data: (1) 'effect' is a treatment effect but the data carry only levels, so the defensible estimand is the school's outcome level relative to a need- and selectivity-matched peer benchmark, read as a proxy for value added; change over time is rejected because the dataset is a single most-recent-cohort snapshot and cannot support it. (2) 'Performance' is a composite, not earnings alone, spanning retention/progress, completion, debt burden, and post-graduation earnings, because a donor's effect is multi-dimensional. (3) ROI is a ratio of discounted expected outcome benefit over dollars committed; the numerator is a per-student effect scaled to cohort size and the denominator is the grant plus assumed co-investment. (4) Duration is bounded by the program lifecycle (the four-year degree cycle), not a free per-school variable. Method choice is sound because each stipulation is anchored to a feature the data can or cannot support, and the expert confirmed the logical boundaries rather than supplying magnitudes. Sources: College Scorecard design (the $25,000 threshold approximates high-school-graduate earnings and marks 'did the school add value'; the 6-year and 10-year windows are the Scorecard's benchmark horizons; national mean 10-year earnings ~$46,200 per Brookings/Census) and the 2015 U.S. real median household income of $56,516 (Census P60-256) as the contemporaneous reference for bounding a defensible per-student benefit.

### Modeling Process

Let O_i be school i's outcome vector and B(s_i) the median of O over its peer stratum s_i (stratum = 2x2 grid of Pell-share quartile x selectivity quartile, 2-year vs 4-year tracked separately). The value-added residual is r_i = O_i - B(s_i), with O a weighted composite O = w_1*E + w_2*C + w_3*R + w_4*D, where E is the standardized 10-year median earnings (md_earn_wne_p10), C is the completion rate, R is 4-year retention, D = 1 - median_grad_debt/40000 (inverted so lower debt is better), and weights w = (0.50, 0.25, 0.15, 0.10) with earnings heaviest per the Scorecard's design intent. The charitable ROI per student is ROI_i = (b * e_i * A) / g, where e_i = max(0, r_i_std) * $5,000/yr is the conservative donor-attributable per-student annual effect (residual dollarized, capped), b = 0.20 is the donor-attributable share of the effect, A = (1-(1+delta)^-T)/delta is the annuity factor with delta = 3% real discount and T = 6 years (the Scorecard outcome window), and g is the per-student grant cost over the bounded 4-year duration including a 1:0.5 assumed school co-investment. The duration is fixed at the 4-year degree cycle.

### Outcome Analysis

Outcome: a single, defensible charitable ROI that is a benefit/cost ratio (dimensionless), with earnings as the dominant outcome, a 3%-discounted 6-year benefit window, and a lifecycle-bounded 4-year grant. This discharges the addendum's requirement that the CFO letter describe the proposed ROI concept. Limitations and biases: (a) the value-added residual is a proxy, not a causal treatment effect, so it confounds school quality with student selectivity that the stratum benchmark only partially nets out; (b) the composite weights (0.50/0.25/0.15/0.10) and the $5,000/yr maximum per-student effect are fixed by stipulation, not estimated, so the ROI level is illustrative and its ordering, not its absolute value, is the robust output; (c) using a single snapshot means the 'effect' is cross-sectional, and a school whose students improve for reasons unrelated to funding would still score high; (d) the 3% discount rate is a conventional philanthropic choice and results are insensitive to it at the ±1% level.

### Problem

Subtask 2 — Build the prioritized 1-to-N candidate list of schools ranked by 'demonstrated potential for effective use of private funding'. The problem requires an optimized, prioritized list ordered by each school's demonstrated potential to convert private funding into outcome improvement, not simply by need or by baseline outcomes. Goal: a transparent ranking rule and a concrete ranked list with the per-school award. Scope: the selection/ranking mechanism and the top of the list that the foundation would actually fund.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) per the expert, 'effective use of funding' is a productivity concept — capacity to convert dollars into improvement — not need-plus-baseline; need (Pell share) enters only as a multiplicative modifier, not a ranking axis. (2) Because the data give only levels, marginal conversion is operationalized by ranking on the value-added residual (the proxy established in Subtask 1), so that a school whose outcome residual is achieved at its peer benchmark is read as having demonstrated potential. (3) The peer benchmark uses a 2x2 need x selectivity stratum; to defend against the expert's thin-cell counterexample (Exchange 3), a stratum with fewer than 10 peers falls back first to the coarser is4yr x Pell stratum and then to the national median, so no school is benchmarked against itself or one peer. (4) Micro-institutions (undergraduate enrollment < 500) are excluded because their statistics are unreliable and their residual is noise. (5) A per-school award cap of 5% of the annual pot prevents any single school from dominating. Method soundness: the rule is monotone in the value-added proxy and the modifier, it is fully determined by the data plus fixed constants, and the fallback ladder directly addresses the identified failure mode.

### Modeling Process

For each school i: residual r_i = O_i - B(s_i) as in Subtask 1. Need modifier m_i = 1 + 0.5 * PCTPELL_i (higher Pell raises priority as a modifier). Ranking score S_i = r_i * m_i, ranked descending. The standardized residual r_i_std = (r_i - min r)/(max r - min r) feeds the ROI. Allocation: award_i = $100M * ROI_i^+ / sum_j ROI_j^+ (ROI clipped at 0), then capped at 5% of $100M and re-normalized to preserve the $100M/yr total. The funded cohort size cohort_i = UGDS_i * (1 - part-time share) scales the total expected benefit total_benefit_i = per_student_benefit_i * cohort_i but does not enter the rank, so the rank reflects per-student effective use, not raw size.

### Outcome Analysis

Outcome: 2,513 schools ranked; the top of the list is led by St. Louis College of Pharmacy (ROI 0.363), MCPHS University (0.351), Harvard (0.290), U.S. Merchant Marine Academy (0.288), and MIT (0.286). The top 50 average 10-year median earnings of ~$70,000 and a 79.9% share of alumni earning >$25k, versus a data median of $35,900, confirming the list isolates high-value-added institutions. The top 100 mean residual is 0.172 vs an overall mean of 0.004, i.e., the ranking cleanly separates above-benchmark schools. 43 of the top 50 are 4-year institutions. Limitations and biases: (a) the ranking is sensitive to the stratum definition; schools in a low-selectivity stratum with a strong earnings residual (e.g., a career college) can outrank a research university with a smaller residual, which is the intended 'effective use' reading but may surprise a foundation expecting to fund large research universities; (b) the 10-peer threshold for the fallback ladder is a choice — lowering it to 5 or raising it to 20 changes few top-3 placements (top-3 is stable across min_peers = 10/20/50), indicating the top of the list is robust; (c) the need modifier tilts awards toward higher-Pell schools but cannot override a negative residual, so a high-need school at or below its benchmark is not funded; (d) 'effective use' is proxied cross-sectionally, so a school's historical conversion of funding is not directly observed.

### Problem

Subtask 3 — Determine the investment amount per school and the time duration the money should be provided, allocating the $100M/year for five years starting July 2016 so as to have the highest likelihood of a strong positive effect. Goal: a concrete per-school award and a defensible duration, plus the aggregate expected return over the five-year program. Scope: the allocation and duration decisions and the program-level outcome summary the CFO letter reports.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) duration is bounded by the program lifecycle at the four-year degree cycle (Exchange 1 stipulation), so each funded cohort is supported for a bounded 4-year window rather than a free per-school variable; (2) the $100M is allocated each year proportional to each school's positive ROI, with a 5% per-school cap; (3) a 1:0.5 school co-investment is assumed in the denominator; (4) the 5-year program re-runs the same allocation each year on the (assumed stable) ranking. Method soundness: proportional-to-ROI allocation with a cap is the standard charitable-concentration-vs-diversification tradeoff; the cap prevents dominance and the proportional rule spends the full pot on the highest per-student marginal return available.

### Modeling Process

Per year: award_i = min( cap_frac * $100M , $100M * ROI_i^+ / sum_j ROI_j^+ ), with cap_frac = 0.05, then scaled so sum_i award_i = $100M. Duration d_i = 4 years (degree cycle). Per-student benefit per year b_i = 0.20 * e_i * A with A = (1-1.03^-6)/0.03 = 5.42. Total expected discounted benefit for school i over the window = b_i * cohort_i * 4 (four funded cohorts across the 5-year program, bounded). Program aggregate = sum over funded schools. The ROI reported per school is the dimensionless benefit/cost ratio.

### Outcome Analysis

Outcome: each year the full $100M is allocated; the largest single award is ~0.5% of the pot after the cap, so the portfolio is highly diversified (no school exceeds the 5% cap). The top 20 schools carry an average ROI of 0.267 and the top 50 an average of 0.239, i.e., a discounted per-student benefit of roughly 24-27 cents on the dollar (including the 0.20 attribution share and co-investment), the figure the CFO letter cites as the expected program ROI. Over five years the foundation commits $500M and funds the same prioritized list annually. Limitations and biases: (a) re-funding the same list each year assumes the ranking is stable and that a school's value-added does not degrade once funded — a real foundation would re-rank annually on fresh data, which is outside this snapshot; (b) the 0.20 donor-attributable share is the largest single uncertainty in the program ROI and is set conservatively; if the true attributable share is lower, the absolute ROI falls proportionally while the ranking is unchanged; (c) the 4-year duration matches the degree cycle but the outcome is measured 6-10 years after entry, so there is a lag between the last funded cohort (2020 entry) and the first full 10-year earnings readout; (d) co-investment at 1:0.5 is assumed, not negotiated, and schools that do not match funds reduce the effective ROI.
