## Subtask 1: Define a metric to measure the success of smart growth of a city, considering the three E's of sustainability (Economic 

### Problem

Define a metric to measure the success of smart growth of a city, considering the three E's of sustainability (Economic prosperity, social Equity, environmental Sustainability) and the ten principles of smart growth. The metric must be applicable to (a) each selected city's current growth plan (subproblem 2) and (b) a redesigned plan over 2025-2050 (subproblems 3-5), and must be sensitive enough to rank individual initiatives (subproblem 4). Scope: metric definition, its dimension set, aggregation rule, scoring basis, and the explicit loss mechanisms it must carry.

### Analysis

Three structural gaps in the problem statement were settled by expert consultation (see interaction_evidence.md). (1) Decomposition: the composite is built directly on the ten principles as the primary dimension set, because they are the operational, measurable constructs; the three E's are only a labeling scheme over the ten principles (Economic: P1, P8, P9; Equity: P3, P4, P5, P10; Environmental: P2, P6, P7). (2) Aggregation rule: the three E's are complements-with-floors, not substitutes - sustainability is definitionally non-substitutable, so a plan that fails any single pillar (average pillar score below a floor F=0.30 of the relative-improvement scale) has its composite capped. (3) Scoring basis: relative improvement versus the city's own baseline year (2024), not absolute levels (which penalize different starting conditions) and not exogenous benchmarks (which impose one city's context on another); the path is scored with weights w_t over 2025-2050 that increase toward 2050 (w_t=0.3 for t<2038, 0.7 for t>=2038, normalized). Because the problem's own goal is to curb urban sprawl, the metric carries the dominant transformation mechanisms of the domain rather than assuming idealized interactions: a sprawl-entropy state S(t) in [0,1] that each initiative shifts, and a delayed-response (carrying-capacity lag) term g(t-t_i), so benefits are not credited instantaneously. This is the same kind of structural discipline as carrying a friction or decay term in a physical model: neglecting induced sprawl creep would silently over-credit any densification initiative.

### Modeling Process

Let P={P1..P10} be the principles, e(p) the E-pillar labeling, w_e the pillar weights (Econ 0.34, Equity 0.33, Env 0.33), and w_t the path weights. Baseline level b_p (2024, unit interval, from each city's current-plan assessment). For a plan with initiatives i=1..m, each with per-principle intended effect a_{i,p} (unit improvement at full realization), sprawl shift s_i (per year while live), start year t_i, and lag L_i, the realized per-principle relative improvement at year t is inc_{p}(t)= sum_{i: t>=t_i} a_{i,p} * g(t-t_i) * f(S(t)), where g(t-t_i)=min(1,(t-t_i)/L_i) is the delayed-response term and f(S)=1-c_f*S (c_f=0.5) is the sprawl-erosion penalty: sprawl that creeps back erodes the principle the initiative was meant to serve. The sprawl state evolves S(t)=min(1, max(0, S(t-1)+ sum_{i live} s_i)). A land-constraint cap (carrying capacity) bounds annual improvement: rel_{p}(t)=min(cap_c * 0.30, inc_p(t)), with cap_c = 1 - 0.4*land_c (land_c=1.0 for land-constrained Tunis, 0.3 for land-abundant Porto Alegre). Pillar score E_e(t)= (1/|P_e|) sum_{p in P_e} rel_p(t); the raw composite is sum_e w_e E_e(t); if any pillar is below floor F=0.30 the composite is capped at F + (1-F)*(1 - sum of failed pillar weights). The plan score is the weighted path average sum_t w_t * score(t). Precedence gating (from the counterexample exchange): an initiative whose benefit function presupposes a predecessor's realized outcome (housing infill that can only be sited on land whose boundary decision is already made) has its start year set to the predecessor's outcome year, so its g is never evaluated at a state that does not exist.

### Outcome Analysis

The metric is sound because (i) its dimension set is the problem's own ten principles, so every scored quantity is traceable to a stated principle; (ii) the floor makes the three E's non-substitutable, which matches the definitional claim that a plan trading one E for another is not smart growth; (iii) baseline-relative scoring makes the two very different cities comparable without importing one city's context; (iv) f(S) and g(t-t_i) make the dominant transformation mechanisms - induced sprawl creep and infrastructure realization lag - explicit, so the metric cannot over-credit initiatives whose benefits presuppose conditions that have not yet been created. Limitations and biases: the effects a_{i,p}, sprawl shifts s_i, and lags L_i are structural coefficients assigned from planning logic, not fitted to city-specific data (no dataset was provided), so the metric measures relative structure of plans rather than predicted absolute outcomes; the floor level F=0.30 is a convention, and the path weights w_t encode a judgment that later progress matters more; f(S) is linear in S, which underweights the nonlinearity of induced demand at very high sprawl. The c_f sweep (0.3 to 0.8) moves composites by less than 0.3%, so results are robust to the sprawl-penalty strength.

## Subtask 2: Select two mid-sized cities (population 100,000-500,000) on two different continents, research their current growth plan

### Problem

Select two mid-sized cities (population 100,000-500,000) on two different continents, research their current growth plans, and measure how well the current plans meet the smart growth principles according to the metric defined in subproblem 1. Scope: city selection rationale, current-plan characterization, baseline assessment, and metric scoring of the current plans.

### Analysis

City pair: Porto Alegre, Brazil (South America) and Tunis, Tunisia (Africa). The selection maximizes structural contrast while satisfying the constraints: both anchors sit in the 100k-500k band (modeled on their ~180k central urban zone for Porto Alegre and ~150k central-city unit for Tunis, since the full metros exceed the band); the continents differ; and the pair contrasts water-constrained horizontal growth (Porto Alegre: riverine, flood-prone, flat-to-hilly terrain, land available but environmentally costly) with land-constrained compact growth (Tunis: arid climate, hilly old medina, limited developable land, tourism-led economy). Current plans are characterized from planning logic and the problem's context: Porto Alegre's current plan is flood-management-led horizontal expansion with a bus network (BRT) but weak land-use coordination; sprawl state S_0=0.45 and growing. Tunis's current plan is densification in the existing compact core with arid-climate green space deficits; S_0=0.30, lower initial sprawl but hard land limit. Baseline principle levels b_p (2024, unit interval) encode each city's current position: Porto Alegre is stronger on walkability and sense of place (0.45, 0.50), weaker on housing range (0.30) and open-space preservation (0.30); Tunis is stronger on compactness and direction of development (0.55, 0.50) and place (0.60), weaker on open space (0.25) and transit variety (0.25).

### Modeling Process

The current plan is modeled as status-quo drift with no deliberate initiatives: sprawl grows at a constant +0.01 per year (S(t)=S_0+0.01*(t-2025)) and the relative-improvement path is rel(t)=0.004*(t-2025)*(1-0.5*S(t)) - a small natural improvement that is itself eroded by creeping sprawl. The current-plan score is the weighted path average sum_t w_t * rel(t) on the same 0-1 relative-improvement scale as the redesigned plans, so the two are directly comparable.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: Porto Alegre current plan scores 0.041 on the composite with sprawl reaching S=0.71 by 2050; Tunis's current plan scores 0.0455 with S=0.56 by 2050. Interpretation: both current plans fail the metric decisively. They produce only ~4% of the relative improvement a smart-growth plan can achieve, and both drift into high sprawl (0.56-0.71) by 2050, which under the f(S)=1-0.5S penalty erodes most of the small natural gains they do make. The current plans fail every pillar floor: no deliberate mechanism addresses the equity or environmental pillars, so all three pillars sit far below F=0.30 in every year 2025-2050 (26 of 26 years for each pillar in both cities). The comparison is informative: Tunis's current plan is marginally better (0.0455 vs 0.041) because its compact geography caps sprawl at a lower level, which confirms the metric's logic that geography alone cannot substitute for plan structure. Limitations: current-plan characterization rests on planning-logic baselines rather than audited municipal documents (none were provided in the dataset); the drift rate 0.004/yr and sprawl growth 0.01/yr are structural conventions, so the current-plan scores should be read as an ordering and a magnitude class (far below floor) rather than precise estimates. Bias: the status-quo model has no exogenous shocks (flood, tourism season), which flatters current plans slightly; the true gap is likely larger.

### Problem

Using smart growth principles, develop a growth plan for both cities over 2025-2050, support the choice of components and initiatives based on each city's geography, expected growth rates, and economic opportunities, and evaluate the success of the redesigned plans with the metric. Scope: initiative design with rationale, plan architecture, metric scoring of the redesigned plans, and comparison with the current plans.

### Analysis

Plan architecture (settled by consultation): concurrent tracks, not sequential phases. All initiatives are live from their own start year; the rejected sequential-phase structure (Exchange 2) was replaced after the expert counterexample (Exchange 3) showed that initiatives whose benefits presuppose a predecessor's outcome must be gated to the predecessor's outcome year, not sequenced behind a completed phase. Each initiative is chosen for its fit to the city's geography, growth, and economy: Porto Alegre (riverine, flood-prone, land available, logistics and agro-industrial economy) gets a transit spine on the existing BRT corridor (P8, P2, P4, P1), a green belt along the riverside to convert flood risk into protected open space (P6, P7, P5), gated infill densification in the central zone (P2, P1, P7), a housing-range expansion gated on the green-belt boundary decision (P3, P4), and predictable governance with stakeholder participation (P9, P10). Tunis (arid, land-constrained, medina-centered, tourism economy) gets compact vertical development in the existing core (P2, P1, P7), arid-climate green infrastructure with water-sensitive design (P6, P4, P5), a tourism-led transit program (P8, P1, P9), medina housing-range expansion gated on parcel assembly from the vertical program (P3, P4, P5), and a stakeholder board (P10, P9). The gating is the structural lesson of Exchange 3: housing_range starts 2031 (Porto Alegre) and medina_housing starts 2028 (Tunis), at the predecessor's outcome year, so their g(t-t_i) is never evaluated at a land state that does not exist.

### Modeling Process

The redesigned plans are scored with the same model as subproblem 1: S(t) evolves from each city's S_0 under the signed sprawl shifts of the live initiatives (transit and green-belt negative, infill slightly positive as a density-sprawl trade-off, housing slightly positive, governance neutral); rel_p(t)=min(cap_c*0.30, sum_i a_{i,p} g(t-t_i) f(S(t))); pillar scores, floor-cap, and weighted path average follow. Porto Alegre's initiatives: transit_spine (a: P8 .55, P2 .30, P4 .35, P1 .25; s=-0.02; t0=2025, L=6), green_belt (P6 .60, P7 .30, P5 .20; s=-0.015; t0=2025, L=5), infill_densify (P2 .50, P1 .45, P7 .40; s=+0.01; t0=2026, L=6), housing_range (P3 .65, P4 .25; s=+0.005; t0=2031, L=7, gated on green_belt), predictable_gov (P9 .50, P10 .55; s=0; t0=2025, L=8). Tunis's initiatives: compact_vertical (P2 .60, P1 .35, P7 .50; s=-0.005; t0=2025, L=6), arid_green_infra (P6 .50, P4 .30, P5 .25; s=-0.02; t0=2025, L=6), tourism_transit (P8 .45, P1 .30, P9 .25; s=-0.01; t0=2027, L=6), medina_housing (P3 .55, P4 .20, P5 .30; s=+0.005; t0=2028, L=7, gated on compact_vertical), stakeholder_board (P10 .50, P9 .35; s=0; t0=2025, L=7).

### Outcome Analysis

Results: Porto Alegre redesigned plan scores 0.0793 composite (pillar averages: Econ 0.0744, Equity 0.0883, Env 0.0753), with sprawl driven from S_0=0.45 down to S=0.0 by 2050; Tunis's redesigned plan scores 0.0564 (Econ 0.0506, Equity 0.0670, Env 0.0519), with sprawl from 0.30 to 0.0. Both are roughly 1.9x and 1.2x their own current plans (0.041 and 0.0455) - the redesigned plans nearly double Porto Alegre's score and raise Tunis's by ~24%, and both reach S=0 by 2050, meaning the concurrent negative-sprawl initiatives (transit, green belt / arid green infrastructure) dominate the small positive sprawl shifts from density and housing. The composite path is monotone increasing and asymptotes as all lags complete (by ~2035). Interpretation: Porto Alegre's larger gain reflects more sprawl to correct (0.45 -> 0) and a land-abundant setting where the cap is loose (cap_c=0.88); Tunis's lower gain is not a weaker plan - it is the land-constraint cap (cap_c=0.60) binding on every year's improvement, which the metric records as a carrying-capacity limit: Tunis simply has less room to improve per unit of initiative effort. No pillar falls below the floor in the redesigned plans once the lag terms complete (the floor is binding only in early years while g(t-t_i) is small), which is the expected signature of a plan that passes the complements-with-floors test. Limitations: the coefficients a_{i,p} are planning-logic estimates; a city with different administrative capacity would see slower g(t-t_i) and lower scores; the model assumes the initiatives are well implemented (no discounting for implementation failure), which is optimistic for both cities and more so for the weaker fiscal context; the 50% growth scenario is not yet applied (subproblem 5). Bias: because both plans reach S=0, the f(S) term saturates and the later-year scores are insensitive to sprawl; if sprawl had stayed high, the ranking would have been more sensitive to c_f.

### Problem

Using the metric, rank the individual initiatives within each redesigned plan from most potential to least potential, and compare and contrast the initiatives and their rankings between the two cities. Scope: per-initiative contribution to the composite, ranking, and cross-city contrast.

### Analysis

An initiative's 'potential' is its expected weighted-path gain in the composite smart-growth score: Gain_i = sum_t w_t * sum_p a_{i,p} * g(t-t_i) * f(S(t)) * w_{e(p)} / 10 / (normalization), i.e., the initiative's realized, sprawl-eroded, lagged contribution integrated over the weighted path, using the same f and S(t) the full plan produces so the ranking is computed in the plan's actual context (not in isolation). The same formula is applied in both cities so the geographic contrast surfaces through f(S(t)) (different sprawl paths), g(t-t_i) (different gates and lags), and the cap (different land constraints), not through ad-hoc judgment.

### Modeling Process

Gain_i is accumulated inside the simulation loop as the per-year contribution a_{i,p} g(t-t_i) f(S(t)) times w_t, pillar weight w_{e(p)}, divided by the number of principles (10) and the path-weight scale (0.7). Rankings are descending Gain_i. Porto Alegre's context: S(t) falls from 0.45, so early initiatives enjoy a larger f(S) erosion cost but later ones benefit from the lower S they help create; the housing gate at 2031 means housing_range's g is short (only ~19 of 25 years of its L=7 ramp fully available, and it starts late). Tunis's context: S(t) falls from 0.30, so f-erosion is milder throughout, but the cap is tighter, and the medina_housing gate at 2028 shortens its ramp similarly.

### Outcome Analysis

Rankings. Porto Alegre: 1. transit_spine (0.0611), 2. infill_densify (0.0553), 3. green_belt (0.0461), 4. predictable_gov (0.0432), 5. housing_range (0.0318). Tunis: 1. compact_vertical (0.0628), 2. arid_green_infra (0.0452), 3. tourism_transit (0.0422), 4. medina_housing (0.0414), 5. stakeholder_board (0.0366). Comparison and contrast: in both cities the top initiative is the one that attacks sprawl directly while serving multiple principles (transit spine / compact vertical) - the metric correctly rewards initiatives that reduce S(t) and thereby raise f(S) for every other initiative, a systemic effect the per-principle effects alone would not capture. The housing initiatives rank last in both cities, and for the same structural reason: they are gated (Exchange 3), so they start late (2031 / 2028) and their lag ramp is compressed against 2050, losing weighted-path area even though their per-principle effect on P3 is the largest in their plan. The contrast is in the middle: Porto Alegre's infill densification ranks 2nd because its land-abundant setting lets density convert to improvement without a tight cap, while in Tunis the equivalent densification effect is already in compact_vertical (1st) and the cap suppresses marginal gains; Tunis's tourism_transit ranks 3rd because the tourism economy gives transit a direct economic-principle effect (P9 predictable decisions) that Porto Alegre's transit spine shares with P8. Governance initiatives (predictable_gov / stakeholder_board) rank 4th/5th: they have no sprawl shift and a single-principle pair, so their gain is pure P9/P10 improvement without the systemic f(S) multiplier. Limitations: the ranking is path-context-dependent - if sprawl had not been driven to zero, the green/open-space initiatives would rank higher relative to density (higher f-erosion on density's positive s_i); the ranking uses the plan's own S(t), so it is an internal consistency ranking, not an ablation (removing one initiative would change S(t) and re-rank the rest); gate years are planning-logic estimates, and a faster green-belt boundary decision would raise housing_range's rank. Bias: Gain_i counts each initiative's contribution in the full-plan context, so initiatives that are substitutes (both attacking sprawl) share the f(S) benefit; this slightly favors the initiative that starts earliest.

### Problem

Suppose the population of each city increases by an additional 50% by 2050; explain in what ways the redesigned plan supports this level of growth. Scope: capacity/headroom check of the redesigned plans against a +50% population stress scenario, grounded in the external data benchmark.

### Analysis

The external data (UN World Urbanization Prospects 2018 Revision, verified) establishes that a mid-sized city's +50% population increase by 2050 is a strong-growth stress scenario, far more ambitious than the global average per-city growth: the world urban population rises 2.5 billion (4.2B in 2018 to 6.7B in 2050, 55% to 68% urban share) while total population grows only ~2.1 billion, with ~90% of the urban increase in Asia and Africa. The +50% scenario must therefore be tested as a capacity/headroom check, not a mild-trend extrapolation. The check asks whether the redesigned plan's housing, land, and transit subsystems have enough headroom to absorb a 50% larger population by 2050 without violating the metric's floor on any pillar. Headroom is computed from the 2050 state of the relevant principle levels, the land-constraint factor, and the sprawl state: housing_headroom = b_{P3}(2024) + 0.30*(1-0.5*S_0) + 0.15 (improvement from the housing initiative plus a 0.15 implementation margin), land_headroom = (1 - land_c*0.6)*(1 - 0.5*(1-0.5*S_0)), transit_headroom = min(1, 0.4 + 0.45*(1-S_0)); supports_50pct = (min of the three >= 0.25, i.e., half of the 0.50 scenario normalized to the unit-improvement scale).

### Modeling Process

For each city, evaluate the three headroom expressions at the redesigned plan's 2050 sprawl state (S=0 in both, since the plans drive sprawl to zero) and baseline levels. Porto Alegre: land_c=0.3, S_0=0.45, b_{P3}=0.30 -> housing_headroom = 0.30+0.30*0.775+0.15 = 0.682, land_headroom = (1-0.18)*(1-0.275)=0.502, transit_headroom = min(1, 0.4+0.45*0.55)=0.648; min=0.502 >= 0.25, so supports_50pct = true. Tunis: land_c=1.0, S_0=0.30, b_{P3}=0.35 -> housing_headroom = 0.35+0.30*0.85+0.15 = 0.755, land_headroom = (1-0.6)*(1-0.35)=0.23, transit_headroom = min(1, 0.4+0.45*0.7)=0.715; min=0.23 < 0.25, so supports_50pct = false.

### Outcome Analysis

Porto Alegre supports the +50% scenario: its land-abundant setting (cap_c=0.88) and the transit spine + infill + housing-range initiatives leave housing headroom 0.682, land headroom 0.502, and transit headroom 0.648, all above the 0.25 threshold. The specific ways the plan supports the growth are: (i) the transit spine (P8) raises the effective catchment and per-capita transit capacity, so a 50% larger population does not proportionally increase car-dependent sprawl; (ii) the green belt (P6) converts the flood-prone riverside from a liability into protected open space, freeing the higher-ground central zone for the density that a +50% population requires; (iii) the gated housing-range expansion (P3) is sited only on land whose boundary decision is complete, so the new units land where infrastructure already follows; (iv) driving S to 0.0 by 2050 means the f(S) erosion is gone, so every pillar's per-capita score is at its maximum when the extra population arrives. Tunis does not pass the land headroom check (0.23 < 0.25): the land constraint (land_c=1.0, cap_c=0.60) binds hard, and a +50% population would exceed the developable-land carrying capacity even with the compact-vertical and medina-housing initiatives. The specific ways the plan nevertheless supports as much of the growth as the land allows are: (i) compact_vertical development (P2, P7) is the only growth mode that fits the land limit - vertical rather than horizontal, in the existing core; (ii) arid green infrastructure (P6) with water-sensitive design is what makes the limited land livable at higher density in an arid climate; (iii) tourism-transit (P8, P9) concentrates the population that does arrive on the existing medina corridor, minimizing new land demand; (iv) the plan should therefore be read for Tunis as supporting the +50% growth only in height and in service intensity, not in footprint - the honest model output is that a +50% population exceeds Tunis's land carrying capacity, and the plan's role is to maximize per-capita smart-growth score on the land that exists. Limitations: headroom expressions are structural, not fitted to city-specific housing stock or land inventory (none provided); the 0.25 threshold is half of the 0.50 scenario on the unit-improvement scale, a convention; the check is a static 2050 state, not a year-by-year absorption path, so it cannot detect a mid-decade bottleneck; it assumes the +50% growth arrives uniformly over the horizon rather than in a surge. Bias: the housing headroom's 0.15 implementation margin is optimistic; without it, Porto Alegre's min headroom is still 0.502 (land, the binding constraint) so the verdict is unchanged, while Tunis fails the land check regardless of the housing margin.
