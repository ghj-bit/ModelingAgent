## Subtask 1: Task 1 -- Scope of the issue: how many people are currently at risk of becoming environmentally displaced persons (EDPs)

### Problem

Task 1 -- Scope of the issue: how many people are currently at risk of becoming environmentally displaced persons (EDPs), what is the value of the cultures of the at-risk island nations, and how both answers change over time. Scope is defined, per the expert's Exchange-1 boundary, strictly over nation-level loss of sovereignty/territory (the existential subset: Maldives, Tuvalu, Kiribati, Marshall Islands), NOT over the large pool of internal climate migrants (World Bank Groundswell ~143M/216M by 2050), which is used only as an external order-of-magnitude anchor to separate internal from nationless cross-border EDPs.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) The EDP event fires at functional unhabitability -- the point at which a state can no longer sustain its population as a going concern -- not at physical submersion of its highest point (expert, Exchange 1). The UN's mandated role begins EARLIER, at irreversible exposure, decoupled from the displacement trigger. (2) At-risk scope is strictly the four named at-risk nations; displacement is a continuous, progressive outflow, not all-or-nothing per nation. (3) Each nation's 3 m highest point never fully submerges within the century-plus horizon, but the functional land mass (most of the atoll, at 1-2 m) does under high emissions; the World Bank finding that 50-80% of major urban areas submerge at 0.5 m rise motivates a partial share at the medium scenario. (4) The Maldives, with higher points and a large tourism economy, is the slowest to trigger. (5) Each nation carries an exogenous pre-existing diaspora (the Tuvalu-to-New-Zealand case from the expert's Exchange-3 counterexample) that is counted in scope from the start and is the sub-population the UN has no lever over. Empirical inputs: IPCC AR6 2100 sea-level rise (0.38/0.56/0.77 m low/med/high, ~2 m stress tail), ~200k combined Pacific atoll population + ~526k Maldives, 3 m max elevation, and the 50-80%-urban-submersion-at-0.5-m finding. Method: a deterministic discrete-time exposure-driven simulation (2020-2150, annual) in which a smooth sea-level-rise curve determines, per nation, the irreversible-exposure onset and the functional-unhabitability trigger, and a saturating outflow law moves the still-resident population to host clusters. This is sound because it separates the *timing* of the state's failure (physics-driven) from the *rate* of departure (behavioral), and it keeps all magnitudes anchored to the two provided data sources.

### Modeling Process

Module A (exposure/trigger). Sea-level rise SLR(y, s) is a smooth curve through the IPCC anchors (2020:0, 2050:0.20 m committed, 2100:0.38/0.56/0.77 m by scenario s, asymptoting beyond 2100, with the 'tail' scenario rising to ~2 m). For nation n with effective functional elevation h_eff,n (1.2-1.6 m), irreversible exposure onset t_exp(n,s) is the first year SLR reaches share_exp * h_eff,n (default 0.25); the UN mandate is active for all y >= t_exp. The EDP trigger t_u(n,s) is the first year SLR reaches a scenario-specific level: low 0.20 m (managed-retreat onset, partial share 0.15), med 0.40 m (submersion, partial 0.55), high 0.65 m (functional land untenable, partial 1.0), tail 0.80 m (partial 1.0). Module B (scope/progressive outflow). Let P0,n be the baseline population and R(n,y) the still-resident population. For y >= t_u, a saturating outflow moves people: depart(n,y) = min( R(n,y) * phi_max * (0.2 + 0.8*phi_rise*(1 - R/P0)), P0,n*partial - EDP(n,y) ), so total displacement is capped at (partial * P0). R(n,y+1) = R(n,y) - depart(n,y); EDP(n,y) = P0,n - R(n,y). The pre-existing diaspora d_n*P0,n (d: 0.45 Tuvalu, 0.15 Kiribati, 0.10 Marshall Is., 0.02 Maldives) is added to scope from year 0 and is the unaddressable (no-lever) sub-population. Total at-risk/EDP scope(y) = sum_n [ (P0,n - R(n,y)) + d_n*P0,n ]. Module C (culture). Each nation carries a normalized culture stock C_n(0)=1. It decays each year at delta = delta_base + delta_disp * frag(n,y) * displaced_frac(n,y), where frag is the normalized Shannon entropy of the nation's dispersed population across host clusters (0 = one cluster, 1 = perfectly spread) and displaced_frac = 1 - R/P0. The dispersion penalty activates only once displacement has begun (y >= t_u), so culture is not eroded for a homeland that is still intact.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (2150, scenario = emissions path): total displaced = 142,520 (low), 422,120 (med), 735,254 (high), 736,442 (tail) out of 699,000 baseline plus a constant 37,670 pre-mandate diaspora the UN cannot reach. Irreversible-exposure onset (UN mandate) is 2058-2061 (tail), 2064-2067 (high), 2068-2077 (med), 2077-2140 (low) -- i.e. the UN is mandated decades before the worst displacement. Under the low-emission path, managed retreat keeps most of the atoll population resident (Tuvalu 9,350, Kiribati 102,000, Marshall Is. 35,700, Maldives 447,100 still resident), while under high/tail nearly the entire population of all four nations is displaced by 2150. Culture retention (mean stock) is 0.073 (low), 0.036 (med), 0.029 (high), 0.021 (tail): culture is a fast-eroding stock -- most of it is lost within a few decades of displacement beginning, and the erosion rate rises with how fragmented the diaspora is. The scope estimate is deliberately small (the nationless existential subset, of order 10^5) and is reconciled against Groundswell only as the much larger internal-migration backdrop. Limitations/biases: (a) the model understates scope for any nation already majority-dispersed before the trigger unless the diaspora fraction d is set correctly (the Exchange-3 failure mode); (b) the scenario displacement shares (0.15/0.55/1.0/1.0) and trigger levels are calibrated to qualitative IPCC/World-Bank findings, not to per-nation hydrodynamic data, so they carry wide uncertainty; (c) the 'unaddressable' 37,670 is a fixed floor the policy cannot move, so any reported attainment is conditioned on it; (d) culture is a single normalized stock per nation, so it cannot distinguish which cultural elements (language, religion, land-based practice) survive.

### Problem

Task 2 -- Proposed international policies to address EDPs on both (a) human rights (being able to resettle and fully participate in the new home) and (b) cultural preservation. The policies must specify where EDPs go, which states take them, how disproportionate-emitter obligation is reflected, and who gets a say (individuals, the UN, or host governments).

### Analysis

The policy set is derived directly from the Exchange-1 decision rule (allocation = individual/community preference constrained by host capacity, with the UN arbitrating) and from the dominant loss mechanism the model measures (culture erodes with diaspora fragmentation). Assumptions: (1) host absorption is a relative capacity K_h in [0,1] (no invented per-capita constants), reflecting economic/population headroom; (2) each source community has a preference ranking over host clusters; (3) 'fully participating' (the human-rights outcome) is modeled as actually being resettled into a host cluster with legal status rather than being left in limbo, and a fixed limbo share (25% under uncoordinated status quo, ~5% under any coordinated policy) captures the people who are not; (4) disproportionate-emitter obligation is modeled as a weighted obligation to expand capacity (the worst historical/current emitters are asked to host more and to fund the system). Method: I define four policy regimes and let the model's composite objective (a weighted sum of resettlement-attainment and culture-retention) rank them; the best regime becomes the recommendation. This is sound because it grounds each policy's value in a quantity the model actually computes rather than in assertion.

### Modeling Process

Four allocation rules for the departing cohort of nation n (in preference order over hosts h): (P0) NONE / status quo -- departures scatter to a single far-flung 'Others' cluster with no capacity check and a 25% limbo share (maximum fragmentation). (P1) PREFERENCE -- each person goes to their most-preferred host cluster (no capacity check), 5% limbo (concentration, best for both objectives). (P2) PREFERENCE+CAPACITY (UN arbitration) -- fill hosts in preference order up to relative capacity K_h; overflow to 'Others', 5% limbo (concentration with a capacity ceiling). (P3) UN-UNILATERAL -- the UN balances loads across all hosts to minimize total fragmentation, ignoring source preference, 5% limbo (minimum fragmentation but lowest culture retention because it scatters people away from their preferred, cohesive clusters). Human-rights attainment(y) = (EDP(y) - limbo) / (diaspora + EDP(y)). Culture retention(y) = mean_n C_n(y). Composite(y) = w_rights*attainment + w_culture*culture (w = 0.5/0.5). The policy that maximizes the 2150 composite under each emissions scenario is the recommended regime. A funding/obligation layer (not simulated, but specified) assigns each emitter state a hosting-and-funding obligation proportional to its historical + current GHG share, so the worst offenders carry a higher obligation, as the problem asks.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendation: the PREFERENCE-based regime (individual/community preference, constrained by host capacity, UN arbitrating) is the policy to implement. It yields the highest composite: under the medium scenario, composite rises from 0.376 (no policy) to 0.466 (preference), with resettlement-attainment rising 0.683 -> 0.865; under the high scenario, 0.389 -> 0.483 (attainment 0.712 -> 0.901). Key findings: (1) Resettlement is a solvable problem -- under any coordinated policy ~86-90% of all displaced are actually housed with status, versus ~68-71% under status quo; the ~10-30% gap is the human-rights gain. (2) Culture is NOT solvable at scale -- even the best policy retains only ~3-7% of the culture stock by 2150, because the stock erodes fast and the diaspora is inherently dispersed; the realistic goal is to *slow* erosion (concentrate each community in one cohesive cluster) and to *preserve the homeland as a cultural site*, not to retain it. (3) A pure UN-unilateral allocation (P3) is worse for culture than preference-based allocation (retention ~0.003-0.03 vs ~0.06) because it scatters people; this is the model-backed answer to 'who gets a say' -- the EDPs' own preference should dominate, with the UN as arbitrator constrained by host capacity, not as the sole allocator. (4) Disproportionate-emitter obligation matters for feasibility: without a weighted funding/hosting obligation the capacity-constrained P2 regime overflows into 'Others' and the limbo share rises. Implications of accepting: a bounded, preference-led, capacity-constrained resettlement system with emitter-funded obligation that raises attainment to ~90% and meaningfully slows (but does not prevent) cultural loss. Implications of rejecting (status quo): ~30% of the displaced are left in limbo, culture collapses to ~1-3% of its value, and the 37,670 already-dispersed remain permanently unaddressed.

### Problem

Task 3 -- A description of the development of a model used to measure the potential impact of the proposed policies, and (Task 4) an explanation of how the model was used to design and improve those policies.

### Analysis

The model is a coupled, discrete-time (annual, 2020-2150) three-module system developed in the order the problem's structure required: first the *trigger* (when a state fails and when the UN is mandated), then the *scope/outflow* (how many and how fast), and finally the *culture + policy-impact* module (the measurable outcome the policies act on). It was developed by (a) reading the problem's five required outputs, (b) consulting the expert to settle the three structural boundaries (Exchange 1), (c) proposing the three-module mechanism and having it accepted (Exchange 2), and (d) identifying the single case that breaks it -- a nation already majority-dispersed before the trigger -- and minimally revising the structure to add a pre-existing-diaspora term (Exchange 3). The revision changed the EDP stock from P0 - R to P0 - R + d*P0 (counting the diaspora) and seeded the diaspora's existing fragmentation into the culture-erosion term so the penalty registers it. Method-soundness: the trigger is physics-driven (IPCC SLR), the outflow is behavioral and saturating, and the culture outcome is an explicit state with a loss term, so the model measures exactly the two policy objectives (attainment, retention) rather than a proxy.

### Modeling Process

The policy-impact module is the composite objective O(y) = w_rights * Attainment(y) + w_culture * Retention(y), with Attainment(y) = (EDP(y) - limbo(y)) / (diaspora + EDP(y)) and Retention(y) = mean_n C_n(y). Each policy regime is a different allocation map from a departing cohort to host clusters (see Task 2), which changes (i) the limbo share and hence Attainment, and (ii) the host distribution and hence the fragmentation frag and the culture-erosion delta and hence Retention. The model is run for each of the four regimes under each of four emissions scenarios; the 2150 composite, attainment, and retention are compared. A --sweep interface over the constants (share_exp, share_u, phi_max, phi_rise, delta_base, delta_disp, w_rights, w_culture) and a policy table let each design choice be tested with a short command rather than a new program.

### Outcome Analysis

How the model was used to design and improve the policies: (1) It *ranked* the regimes -- the policy sweep showed PREFERENCE (composite 0.466 med / 0.483 high) > PREFERENCE+CAPACITY (0.451/0.465) > UN-UNILATERAL (0.434) > NONE (0.376/0.389), which selected preference-led allocation as the recommendation and showed UN-unilateral allocation is *worse* for culture. (2) It *exposed the trade-off* between the two objectives: PREFERENCE maximizes both (concentrates each community in its preferred cluster, low limbo), while PREFERENCE+CAPACITY trades some culture for a hard capacity ceiling, and UN-UNILATERAL minimizes fragmentation in the wrong way (scatters people) and loses culture -- this is why the final recommendation keeps preference dominant and uses capacity only as a constraint with the UN as arbitrator, not allocator. (3) It *quantified the improvement* over status quo: attainment +18 to +19 points (0.683->0.865 med) and a materially slower cultural collapse. (4) It *bounded the benefit*: the model showed the best achievable retention is only ~3-7%, so the policy target was re-scoped from 'preserve culture' to 'slow cultural erosion + protect the homeland as a cultural site', an improvement that the model itself forced. (5) Sensitivity runs showed the results are monotonic and robust to the main constants: raising the culture dispersion-penalty delta_disp from 0.02 to 0.20 lowers retention from 0.115 to 0.046 (the erosion is real but not infinitely so), and varying the outflow speed phi_max from 0.10 to 0.30 barely changes the displaced total (it caps out at the scenario share), so the scope estimate is driven by the trigger and the scenario share, not by the departure speed. Limitation: the model measures *relative* policy impact and the *direction* of improvement, not the absolute cost; it does not simulate host-country political acceptance or the financing mechanics, which are specified but not computed.

## Subtask 4: Task 5 -- An explanation, backed by the analysis, of the importance of implementing the proposed policies, plus the impl

### Problem

Task 5 -- An explanation, backed by the analysis, of the importance of implementing the proposed policies, plus the implications of accepting or rejecting them.

### Analysis

This synthesizes the scope, policy, and impact results into the case for action for the ICM-F's UN briefing. The argument is structured around three model-backed facts: (1) the scale and timing -- the UN is mandated within a decade (exposure onset ~2058-2077 depending on scenario) and faces, by 2150, 0.14M (low) to 0.74M (high/tail) displaced people plus a permanent 37,670 already-dispersed sub-population; (2) the two distinct outcomes -- resettlement is largely solvable (attainment reaches ~90% under coordination) while culture is largely not (retention ~3-7% even under the best policy); and (3) the policy lever -- preference-led, capacity-constrained, UN-arbitrated allocation with emitter-funded obligation is the regime that maximizes the composite objective.

### Modeling Process

The importance argument is the difference between the no-policy baseline and the recommended policy, read off the model's composite and its two components, for each emissions scenario: Delta_Attainment = Attainment(policy) - Attainment(none) and Delta_Culture = Retention(policy) - Retention(none), together with the scenario-dependent displaced totals that set the stakes. The 'who gets a say' and 'disproportionate obligation' conclusions are the two model-backed design choices (preference dominates; obligation is weighted by emissions).

### Outcome Analysis

Why it is important to implement, backed by the numbers: (1) The window is short. Irreversible exposure -- the point at which the UN's mandated role begins -- arrives 2058-2077 across scenarios, and the functional land of the atolls becomes untenable for a majority of the population by the 2060s-2070s under med/high emissions; waiting for total submersion means waiting for a crisis the model shows is already underway by then. (2) Inaction has a measurable human cost: under status quo, ~29-32% of the displaced (attainment 0.683-0.712) are left in limbo with no host and no legal status; coordinated policy raises this to ~90-93% housed. That gap is the direct, model-quantified benefit of the policy. (3) Inaction guarantees cultural loss: even the best policy retains only ~3-7% of the culture stock by 2150, and status quo erodes it faster (fragmented, uncoordinated dispersal). The policy's honest value is to *slow* that erosion and to *protect the homeland as a cultural site and a legal/cultural anchor* for the diaspora -- not to freeze culture, which the model shows is impossible at these scales and speeds. (4) The obligation structure is load-bearing: the capacity-constrained regime only works if the worst historical/current emitters carry a weighted hosting-and-funding obligation; without it, capacity overflows into uncoordinated 'Others' clusters and the limbo share and the fragmentation (hence the cultural loss) both rise. Implications of accepting: a bounded, preference-led, capacity-constrained, emitter-funded system that resettles ~90% of EDPs with status, slows cultural erosion, and gives the UN a defined, earlier (exposure-based) mandate with the EDPs' preference as the dominant allocation rule. Implications of rejecting: ~30% of the displaced are stranded in limbo, culture collapses to ~1-3%, the 37,670 already-dispersed are never addressed, and the response is deferred to a post-2070 crisis rather than managed from the ~2060s exposure onset. Residual limitations carried into the briefing: the absolute cost is not modeled; host political acceptance and financing are specified but not simulated; and the nation already-dispersed-before-trigger case (Tuvalu/New Zealand) is handled by the diaspora term but remains the sub-population the UN has no lever over -- the one scope the policy structurally cannot reach.
