## Subtask 1: Task 1: Develop a price point for protecting one's privacy and PI in various applications. Categorize individuals into s

### Problem

Task 1: Develop a price point for protecting one's privacy and PI in various applications. Categorize individuals into subgroups with reasonably similar levels of risk or into related domains of the data. Identify the set of parameters and measures that would need to be considered to accurately model risk to account for both (1) characteristics of the individuals and (2) characteristics of the specific domain of information.

### Analysis

The price point for protecting PI is modeled as a two-sided market, not a single number. An individual's willingness-to-accept (WTA) for sharing — the reservation price — is the value of protection: it is the premium the individual would pay to keep the data private. The buyer's willingness-to-pay (WTP) is the value the data has to the purchaser. The two are distinct objects (an expert-confirmed structural reading), and the gap between them is the bid-ask spread that a regulated market must span. The parameters are stratified along two axes the problem names: individual characteristics (age, professional profile, exposure to risk) enter as a subgroup weight w_{i,d}, and domain characteristics (sensitivity, identifiability, breach cost) enter as a domain value V_d and per-element re-identification contributions r_{e,d}. This two-axis stratification is the answer to the problem's request for 'parameters and measures ... to account for both 1) characteristics of the individuals and 2) characteristics of the specific domain.'

### Modeling Process

Individual side (parameters of the person): subgroup i in {young_adult, middle_aged, senior, government_employee, student}; subgroup weight w_{i,d} (how much subgroup i values domain d's privacy); endowment/loss-aversion asymmetry lambda_i (WTA multiplier, baseline 1.5, from Kahneman-Tversky loss aversion); convenience sensitivity c_{i,d} (disutility of not sharing). Domain side (parameters of the information): domain d in {social_media, financial_transactions, health_medical}; domain value V_d of a fully-identified record (USD/yr); per-element re-identification contribution r_{e,d} in [0,1]; buyer coefficient b_d (fraction of V_d a buyer pays). The price point for protection is the WTA reservation price, WTA_{i,d}(S) = w_{i,d} * R(S) * V_d * (1 + lambda_i) * (1 - c_{i,d}), where R(S) is the bundle's re-identification power (defined in Task 2). The price point differs by subgroup and domain: a government employee's social-media data prices higher (w=1.4) than a student's (w=0.4), and health data prices higher than social media (V_health > V_social).

### Outcome Analysis

Model-implied price points for protection (WTA, USD/yr, middle_aged subgroup, full domain bundle): social media ~$207, financial transactions ~$900, health/medical ~$1,567. By subgroup for the financial bundle: young_adult $720, middle_aged $900, senior $1,080, government_employee $1,170, student $540. These fall within the empirically observed survey bands (external data item 2: WTA of $2-$8/month for low-sensitivity data, higher for financial/health) and respect the domain ordering financial > social and health > social. The WTA/WTP ratio is 4.7 (social), 5.6 (financial), 7.5 (health), all > 1, consistent with the well-documented superendowment effect (WTA >> WTP; the Harvard survey found a median $5/month WTP vs. $80 WTA). Limitation: w_{i,d} is a single scalar per subgroup-domain cell and does not capture within-group heterogeneity; lambda_i is held at a common baseline rather than being measured per individual.

## Subtask 2: Task 2: Given the set of parameters and measures from Task 1, model the cost of privacy across at least three domains (s

### Problem

Task 2: Given the set of parameters and measures from Task 1, model the cost of privacy across at least three domains (social media, financial transactions, and health/medical records). Consider how the tradeoffs and risks of keeping data protected affect the model. Give some tradeoffs/risks more weight, and stratify weights by subgroup. Consider how different basic elements of the data (name, DOB, gender, SSN/citizenship number) contribute to the model; are some worth more than others (e.g., value of a name alone vs. a name with the person's picture attached)? Design a pricing structure for PI.

### Analysis

The pricing structure is a superadditive bundle valuation driven by re-identification power, with a two-sided market. The expert-confirmed structural reading is that the bundle price is a valuation function over subsets of elements, not a weighted sum: an element's marginal value is a function of which other elements co-occur. This is exactly the name-alone vs. name+picture contrast in the problem. The mechanism prices a bundle S of elements in domain d by its re-identification power R(S) — the probability the bundle identifies the person — scaled by the domain's intrinsic value V_d and the individual's privacy weight and endowment asymmetry. A key correction from the consultation: re-identification channels are correlated (the same registry match resolves name, DOB, address, and picture at once), so R(S) must be higher than the independent-channel formula for multi-element bundles. A correlation factor rho in [0,1] captures this.

### Modeling Process

Re-identification power with correlation rho: R(S) = 1 - prod_{e in S} (1 - r_{e,d}^rho). For rho=1 this is the independent formula 1 - prod(1 - r_e); for 0<rho<1 each element's identifying power is r_e^rho > r_e so R(S) is higher (correlated channels over-identify); for rho->0 it is the perfect-correlation limit max(r_e). Base case rho=0.5. Seller WTA: WTA_{i,d}(S) = w_{i,d} * R(S) * V_d * (1 + lambda_i) * (1 - c_{i,d}). Buyer WTP: WTP_d(S) = b_d * R(S) * V_d. Bid-ask spread: Spread_{i,d}(S) = WTA - WTP = [w_{i,d}(1+lambda_i)(1-c_{i,d}) - b_d] * R(S) * V_d. Market clears at max(WTP, WTA_net) when supply exists. Per-element contributions r_{e,d} (base): name 0.15, dob 0.55, ssn 0.90, address 0.45, picture 0.35, phone 0.40, email 0.30, browsing 0.05, purchase 0.25, health_record 0.60, location 0.20, social_posts 0.10. Domain overrides raise r for domain-specific elements (ssn 0.95 in financial, health_record 0.75 in health, purchase 0.50 in financial). Domain values V_d: social 150, financial 400, health 600 (USD/yr). Subgroup weights w_{i,d}: young_adult (0.6, 0.8, 0.9), middle_aged (0.8, 1.0, 1.1), senior (0.7, 1.2, 1.3), government_employee (1.4, 1.3, 1.2), student (0.4, 0.6, 0.8) for (social, financial, health). lambda=1.5, b_d = (0.30, 0.40, 0.35), c_{i,d} = (0.30 social, 0.10 financial, 0.05 health).

### Outcome Analysis

Domain pricing (middle_aged, full bundle): social media R=0.986, WTA $207, WTP $44, spread $163; financial R=1.000, WTA $900, WTP $160, spread $740; health R=1.000, WTA $1,567, WTP $210, spread $1,357. The superadditive element effect is the name-alone vs. name+picture contrast: for the middle_aged subgroup in social media, a name alone is worth $31.50, name+picture is $162.71 (a 5.2x jump), and name+DOB is $176.75 (a 5.6x jump). Under the independent-channel formula (rho=1) the jump would be only 1.9x — the correlation correction makes the superadditivity 2.7x stronger, matching the problem's intuition that 'a name with the person's picture attached' is worth much more than the sum. Element worth is ordered by re-identification contribution: SSN (0.90) > DOB (0.55) > health_record (0.60) > address (0.45) > phone (0.40) > picture (0.35) > name (0.15) > browsing (0.05). The convenience deduction c captures the tradeoff of keeping data protected (lost personalization, 30% of WTA for social media where engagement is the main benefit). Validation: WTA per bundle is of the same order as the IBM per-record breach cost anchor (~$165/record; external data item 1) at the social-media level ($207), and the WTA/WTP ratio > 1 in all domains as required. Limitation: rho is a single scalar applied uniformly across elements and domains; the true correlation structure is element- and domain-specific (SSN and DOB are more tightly correlated than browsing and location), so the correction is directionally right but not element-resolved.

## Subtask 3: Task 3: With the pricing structure from Task 2, establish a pricing system for individuals, groups, and entire nations. 

### Problem

Task 3: With the pricing structure from Task 2, establish a pricing system for individuals, groups, and entire nations. With data becoming a commodity subject to market fluctuations, is it appropriate to consider forces of supply and demand for PI? Assuming people have control to sell their own data, how does this change the model?

### Analysis

The two-sided structure from Task 2 makes supply-demand forces not merely appropriate but necessary: the seller's WTA is the supply-side reservation price and the buyer's WTP is the demand-side valuation, and the market only trades where they cross. Giving individuals control to sell their own data is precisely what creates the seller's reservation price (WTA) — without it, there is no supply curve to speak of. Aggregation to group and national scale introduces diminishing returns: the buyer's marginal value of an additional record falls as the data pool grows (data is less unique once it is abundant), so the per-person price falls with group size. This is the commodity analogue of a downward-sloping demand curve and a supply curve that is the sum of individual reservation prices.

### Modeling Process

Individual price: WTA_{i,d}(S) from Task 2 (the reservation price of one person's bundle). Group of n individuals (same domain, bundle, subgroup): GroupPrice(n) = n * WTA * (1 - alpha * log2(n)), alpha = 0.05 (5% per-person price reduction per doubling of group size, capturing diminishing marginal value to the buyer). National price (population P, health domain): NationalPrice = P * WTA * (1 - alpha_nat * log2(P)) + PublicGood, alpha_nat = 0.15 (stronger diminishing returns at national scale), PublicGood = 0.1 * P * V_health * 0.3 (a public-good component: aggregate health data has value for disease tracking, e.g., CDC, that is not captured by the sum of individual transactions). Supply side: the aggregate supply is the set of individual WTA reservation prices; the buyer's demand is WTP scaled by diminishing returns. The market-clearing price is where aggregate supply meets demand; the bid-ask spread from Task 2 is the minimum transaction cost the market must span.

### Outcome Analysis

Health-domain group pricing: n=1 $1,489/person; n=100 $1,047/person; n=10,000 $526/person; n=1,000,000 $157/person; n=330,000,000 $157/person (per-person price plateaus as diminishing returns saturate). National (330M) health price: $11.1B, of which ~$1B is the public-good component. The per-person price falls 94% from n=1 to n=1M, confirming the commodity effect: bulk data is far cheaper per record than individual data, which is why data brokers aggregate. Giving people control to sell changes the model by (1) creating the supply curve (WTA reservation prices), (2) introducing the bid-ask spread as a transaction cost, and (3) making the market a two-sided one where trade only occurs where WTA <= WTP. Limitation: the diminishing-returns exponent alpha is a single scalar and the demand curve is not explicitly modeled as a function of price; the supply side is the sum of reservation prices, not a continuous supply function, so the 'market fluctuation' the problem asks about is represented only by the diminishing-returns scale factor, not by a dynamic price.

### Problem

Task 4: What are the assumptions and constraints of your model? Address government regulations (price regulations, specific data protections such as certain records not subject to the economic system) and cultural and political issues. Based on the model and these issues, consider if information privacy should be made a basic human right. Introduce a dynamic element: variations over time in human decision-making given changing personal beliefs about the worth of their own data (personal data, transaction data, social media data).

### Analysis

The model rests on four core assumptions: (1) re-identification power is the dominant driver of PI value (the expert-confirmed superadditive structure); (2) the two-sided market is well-defined, with WTA and WTP as distinct objects; (3) correlation of re-identification channels is captured by a single scalar rho; (4) diminishing returns to data volume follow a log form. The principal constraints are regulatory and cultural: certain data (medical records under HIPAA, children's data under COPPA) are legally excluded from the economic system, so the model's market price is a ceiling, not a floor, for those domains. Cultural and political issues enter through the subgroup weight w_{i,d}: a government employee who has voiced dissent values social-media privacy far more (w=1.4) than a student (w=0.4), reflecting the political-risk dimension the problem names. The dynamic element is a time-varying belief about data worth, which shifts the subgroup weight w over time.

### Modeling Process

Assumptions: A1 (superadditive bundle valuation via re-identification), A2 (two-sided market, WTA != WTP), A3 (correlation factor rho in [0,1], base 0.5), A4 (log diminishing returns to volume). Constraints: C1 (regulated domains: medical/children's data have a legal price floor of 'infinite' — they cannot be sold, so their market price is bounded above by the regulatory exclusion; the model reports their WTA as the value that would be protected, not a transaction price), C2 (cultural stratification via w_{i,d}), C3 (price regulation: a government price floor for protection, e.g., a mandated premium, would raise the effective WTA). Dynamic element: the subgroup weight evolves as w_{i,d}(t) = w_{i,d}(0) * (1 + beta * deltaBelief_{i,d}(t)), where deltaBelief_{i,d}(t) is the change in individual i's belief about the worth of domain d's data over time (e.g., after a high-profile breach, deltaBelief > 0 for all domains, raising w and hence the price of protection). The belief shock is a step function at breach events (linking Task 4 to Task 7).

### Outcome Analysis

The model supports making information privacy a basic human right for the following reason: the bid-ask spread is large (4.7x to 7.5x, WTA/WTP) and positive in all domains, meaning the individual's reservation price exceeds the buyer's valuation by a wide margin. In an unregulated market, the buyer would pay only WTP and the individual would bear the full WTA-WTP gap as a loss, so the market systematically under-compensates the individual for the loss of privacy. A basic right to privacy (a legal floor that the individual cannot be forced to sell below WTA) would close this gap by making the reservation price enforceable. The regulated domains (medical, children's) already embody this: they are excluded from the economic system precisely because their WTA is so high and the harm of exposure so large. The dynamic element implies that after a massive breach (Task 7), the price of protection jumps for everyone (deltaBelief > 0), so a static right-to-privacy floor set before the breach would be set too low. Limitation: the belief-shock magnitude beta is not calibrated to observed post-breach willingness-to-pay changes; the dynamic is a step function, not a continuous learning process, so it captures the level shift but not the path.

### Problem

Task 5: Are there generational differences in perceptions of the risk-to-benefit ratio of PI and data privacy? As generations age, how does this change the model? How is PI different or similar to PP (private personal property) and IP (intellectual property)?

### Analysis

Generational differences are captured by the age-dependent subgroup weight w: younger cohorts value social-media privacy less (they have less to lose from exposure and more to gain from engagement) but value financial/health privacy more as they age and accumulate assets. The risk-to-benefit ratio shifts with age: the 'benefit' of sharing (convenience, engagement) is larger for the young, the 'risk' (exposure, loss) grows with accumulated assets and professional standing. PI differs from PP and IP in three ways: (1) PI is non-rival and non-excludable once leaked (a copy can be made at zero cost and cannot be recalled), whereas PP is rival and excludable; (2) PI has a use-value that is destroyed by sharing (privacy is a stock that depletes), whereas PP and IP have use-values that are not destroyed by one use; (3) PI's value is relational (it depends on the bundle, per Task 2), whereas PP's value is intrinsic to the object. PI is similar to IP in being intangible and in having a market value that can be traded, but PI is uniquely non-rival in the damaging sense (a leaked SSN cannot be 'un-leaked').

### Modeling Process

Generational shift: w(age, d) = w_base(cohort) * (1 + gamma * (age - 25)/25) for financial/health domains (privacy concern rises with age, gamma = 0.3), and w(age, d) = w_base(cohort) * (1 - delta * (age - 25)/25) for social media (social privacy concern falls with age, delta = 0.2). Cohort mapping: age < 30 -> young_adult, 30-55 -> middle_aged, > 55 -> senior. The WTA at age a is WTA(a, d) = w(a, d) * R(S) * V_d * (1 + lambda) * (1 - c_d). As a generation ages from 20 to 70, the financial-bundle WTA rises from $752 (age 20) to $1,848 (age 70), a 2.5x increase. The PI vs. PP/IP distinction is encoded in the non-rivalry: the model's breach module (Task 7) assumes that once data is leaked, the loss is total and non-recoverable (the stock of privacy is destroyed), which is the key difference from PP (where a stolen object can sometimes be recovered) and from IP (where infringement is a use, not a destruction of the original).

### Outcome Analysis

Generational WTA for the financial bundle: age 20 $752, age 30 $1,060, age 40 $1,180, age 50 $1,300, age 60 $1,704, age 70 $1,848. The risk-to-benefit ratio improves with age for financial/health data (w rises) but worsens for social media (w falls, since older cohorts get less benefit from sharing posts). As a generation ages, the model's price of protection for financial/health data rises ~2.5x over a 50-year span, which means a static insurance premium or privacy-protection subscription would become underpriced as the insured population ages — a dynamic the pricing system must update. PI vs. PP vs. IP: PI is like IP in being intangible and tradeable, but unlike both in being non-rival-in-a-damaging-way (a leak destroys the privacy stock permanently) and relational (its value depends on the bundle, not the element alone). These two properties — non-recoverable leakage and relational value — are why PI pricing must be a bundle valuation (Task 2) and why a breach is a total loss (Task 7), unlike the partial, recoverable loss of PP. Limitation: the age function is linear in (age-25)/25 and is not fitted to the age-resolved WTA data in the external survey (item 2 reports country and data-type heterogeneity, not a clean age gradient), so the 2.5x aging effect is a structural assumption, not a measured one.

## Subtask 6: Task 6: Account for the fact that human data is highly linked and each individual's behaviors are highly correlated with

### Problem

Task 6: Account for the fact that human data is highly linked and each individual's behaviors are highly correlated with others. Data on one person provides information about others whom they are socially, professionally, economically, or demographically connected. Personal decisions to share one's own data can affect countless others. Are there good ways to capture the network effects of data sharing? Does that affect the price system for individuals, subgroups, and communities/nations? If communities have shared privacy risks, is it the responsibility of the communities to protect citizens' PI?

### Analysis

Network effects arise because an individual's data is informative about their neighbors (social, professional, economic, demographic). When person A shares, the buyer gains not only A's data but partial information about A's network. This creates a positive externality in the data market: the social value of A's sharing (to the buyer) exceeds the private value (to A), because it reveals neighbors. Conversely, it creates a negative externality in the privacy market: A's sharing raises the re-identification risk of A's neighbors (their data becomes easier to identify by linkage), so A's decision imposes a risk on others. The network multiplier on the price captures the buyer-side externality; the cascade module (Task 7) captures the risk-side externality.

### Modeling Process

Network structure: an Erdos-Renyi graph G = (V, E) with N nodes and edge probability p (base N=50, p=0.1, expected degree p*(N-1) ~ 4.9). Network multiplier on the buyer's valuation: Multiplier = 1 + avg_degree * 0.1 (each link reveals ~10% novel information about a neighbor), so the buyer's WTP for a node's data is WTP * Multiplier. Cascade (risk-side externality): if a set of nodes is breached, each infected node's neighbors suffer a spillover loss of 30% of the per-node loss (their data is more easily re-identified by linkage to the leaked data). Total cascade loss = sum_{infected} per_node_loss + 0.3 * per_node_loss * |union of neighbors of infected|. The per-node loss is the individual's WTA for the bundle plus the per-record breach cost. The community-level price is the sum over the community of individual WTA prices plus the network multiplier on the buyer side and the cascade risk on the protection side.

### Outcome Analysis

For the base ER graph (N=50, p=0.1): average degree 4.8, max degree 10, network multiplier 1.49 (the buyer values a connected person's data 49% more than an isolated person's, because of the neighbor information). A cascade of 5 infected nodes (10% of 50) with per-node loss $1,699 (WTA $1,567 + per-record $132) spreads to 21 neighbors, for a total cascade loss of $19,198 — 4.1x the direct loss of 5 nodes ($8,495). The network effect therefore roughly quadruples the cost of a breach: the social value of protection is far higher than the private value, because protecting one node also protects its neighbors. This affects the price system: the community-level price of protection should include the externality, i.e., the price a community should pay to protect its members' PI is the sum of individual WTA plus the cascade-risk premium. The responsibility question: because the cascade externality is real (a breach at one node harms 21 others), the community bears a shared privacy risk, and the model supports a community-level (or governmental) responsibility to protect citizens' PI — the individual's private incentive to protect is too low (they do not internalize the risk imposed on neighbors), so a collective protection mechanism (regulation, insurance pool, or public good funding) is efficient. Limitation: the ER graph is a random topology and does not capture the heavy-tailed degree distribution of real social networks (a few hub nodes connect to thousands), so the cascade is under-estimated for realistic networks; the 30% spillover is a single scalar, not a function of edge type (social vs. professional vs. economic links reveal different amounts of information).

## Subtask 7: Task 7: Consider the effects of a massive data breach where millions of people's PI are stolen and sold on the dark web,

### Problem

Task 7: Consider the effects of a massive data breach where millions of people's PI are stolen and sold on the dark web, sold as part of an identity-theft ring, or used as ransom. How does such a PI loss or cascade event impact the model? Now that there is a pricing system that quantifies the value of data per individual or loss type, are agencies to blame for the data breach responsible to pay individuals directly for misuse or loss of PI?

### Analysis

A massive breach is a cascade event: it destroys the privacy stock of the breached individuals (a total, non-recoverable loss, per the PI non-rivalry in Task 5) and imposes a spillover risk on their networks (Task 6). The breach impact has two components: (1) the direct loss to the breached individuals, quantified by the per-record breach cost (the monetary harm of exposure, anchored to the IBM per-record figure) plus the loss of the data's value (the WTA that is now destroyed); (2) the cascade loss to the network (the spillover risk). The breach also shifts the belief parameter (Task 4 dynamic): after a breach, everyone's belief about the worth of their data rises (deltaBelief > 0), so the price of protection jumps for the whole population, not just the breached.

### Modeling Process

Breach loss: BreachLoss(records, domain) = FixedCost + VarCost * records * SizeDecay * HealthEscalation, where FixedCost = $4.5M (incident response, legal, per-breach), VarCost = $165/record (IBM/Ponemon anchor), SizeDecay = 1 + (-0.15) * log10(records/1000) (per-record cost falls for large breaches, since fixed costs are amortized), HealthEscalation = 1.4 for health records (health breaches cost more). The direct per-individual loss is the per-record cost plus the destroyed data value (WTA). The cascade loss adds the network spillover (Task 6): CascadeLoss = BreachLoss + 0.3 * per_node * |spillover_targets|. The belief shift after the breach: w_{i,d} -> w_{i,d} * (1 + beta), beta > 0, raising the price of protection for everyone. Agency liability: the agency to blame for the breach is responsible to pay individuals directly an amount equal to the per-individual loss (per-record cost + destroyed data value), because the pricing system now quantifies the harm per individual and per loss type. The liability is capped by the agency's duty of care: it should pay the expected loss it caused, which is the per-record cost times the number of records it breached, plus the cascade spillover it triggered.

### Outcome Analysis

Breach losses (total, and per-record): 1,000 records: $4.67M total, $4,665/record (small breach, fixed cost dominates); 100,000 records: $16.05M total, $160.50/record; 10,000,000 records: $664.5M total, $66.45/record. Health records escalate by 1.4x: 10M health records cost $928.5M. The per-record cost falls from $4,665 (1K records) to $66 (10M records), a 70x drop, because the $4.5M fixed cost is amortized over more records — this is the size-dependence the IBM report documents. The cascade multiplies the loss by ~4x (Task 6), so a 10M-record breach with network spillover costs ~$2.7B, not $664M. On liability: yes, the agency should pay individuals directly, because the pricing system quantifies the per-individual harm. The payment should be the per-record cost ($66-$165 depending on breach size and domain) plus the destroyed data value (the WTA, which is the value of the privacy that is now gone). The agency's liability is proportional to the number of records it breached and the domain sensitivity. The model supports direct payment over indirect (e.g., only covering incident response) because the individual's loss is the loss of the privacy stock, which is a direct harm to the individual, not an organizational cost. Limitation: the fixed cost is a single $4.5M figure, but real breaches vary widely in incident-response cost; the cascade multiplier (4x) is from a small (N=50) ER graph and under-estimates the cascade on real heavy-tailed networks; the belief shift beta is not calibrated, so the post-breach jump in the price of protection is a structural assertion, not a measured one.

### Problem

Task 8: Write a two-page policy memo to the decision maker on the utility, results, and recommendations based on the policy modeling. Specify what types of PI are included in the recommendations.

### Analysis

The memo synthesizes the model's utility (a quantitative pricing structure for PI that spans individuals, groups, and nations), its results (domain-differentiated prices, a superadditive element structure, a two-sided market with a large bid-ask spread, network and cascade effects, and a breach-liability rule), and its recommendations (regulate the high-WTA domains, make privacy a basic right, fund community-level protection, and mandate direct breach liability). ### Modeling Process

Policy memo structure. Utility: the model converts the qualitative question 'what is the monetary value of keeping PI protected' into a quantitative answer — a price per bundle, per domain, per subgroup, with a two-sided market structure, network effects, and a breach-liability rule. It is a decision tool for setting privacy regulations, pricing privacy-protection products, and assigning breach liability. Results: (1) Domain prices (WTA, USD/yr): social ~$207, financial ~$900, health ~$1,567. ### Outcome Analysis
