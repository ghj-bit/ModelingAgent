### Problem

Build the core emergency-evacuation model for the Louvre that (a) minimizes the time for all visitors to egress (a makespan objective) and (b) allows emergency personnel to enter as quickly as possible, while identifying the bottlenecks that limit movement toward the exits. The deliverable is a reproducible network model, not a single number: it must expose, for a given occupancy and threat, the total evacuation time, the per-exit loads, and the points where flow saturates. Scope: a stylized-but-faithful network of the Louvre (five floors, wings up to 480 m, the four public entrances, and a staff-only exit reserve) parameterized by the two provided empirical constants (door specific flow rate and free walking speed).

### Analysis

Assumptions, in the order they govern the model: (1) The objective 'as quickly as possible' is read as a MAKESPAN (the evacuation is complete when the last visitor exits), not a sum of individual travel times; the emergency-personnel ingress goal is a SECOND, concurrent population, not a term folded into the visitor makespan. (2) 'Optimal exit' means GLOBALLY COORDINATED (load-balanced) assignment - each visitor's exit and route are chosen to maximize aggregate throughput - rather than individually-rational shortest path, because under pure shortest-path the makespan is dominated by the single most-congested exit and only load balancing reduces it. (3) The base egress set is the four public entrances only, fully open; the staff-only exits (service, employee, VIP/secret) are a CONDITIONAL reserve, switched on only in threat scenarios, consistent with the security concern the statement raises, while still serving as staff-ingress capacity in the base case. (4) CONGESTION is the first-order loss: throughput on an arc saturates and collapses above the jam density (~4 persons/m2), and each exit discharges at the NIST-calibrated rate of ~1.3 persons/s per meter of effective width. These four assumptions were placed to the expert and accepted (exchange 2). Method: a two-stage solution of a node-arc network. Stage 1 is a min-cost flow (transportation problem, solved exactly by linear programming) that routes the visitor demand at each gallery to the exits; the cost of sending a person to an exit is their travel time to it plus that exit's own expected discharge delay, and a fixed-point iteration on the per-exit loads makes the assignment genuinely load-balancing. Stage 2 computes the makespan as the maximum, over exits, of (last arrival time at that exit + its queue drain time X_e/q_e). The method is sound because it treats the two real physical losses - travel time (speed-density) and discharge capacity (door specific flow) - as first-order, and because the min-cost-flow structure is the standard, verifiable way to obtain a globally coordinated egress assignment.

### Modeling Process

Network: directed node-arc graph with nodes = 9 gallery/level nodes (3 ground, 3 upper, 2 basement, 1 deep-basement) plus a central transfer hub H (a cut vertex) plus 7 exits (4 public: PYR, RIC, CAR, LION; 3 reserve: SVR, EMP, VIP). Each undirected link (u,v) of length L and width W is represented in both directions. Parameters: q_per_m = 1.3 persons/(s-m) [NIST, Item 1]; v_free = 1.34 m/s, range 0.85-1.85 m/s [Item 2]; jam density rho_jam = 4 persons/m2; corridor width 3 m. Exit effective widths (m): PYR 4, RIC 3, CAR 3, LION 3, SVR 1.5, EMP 1.5, VIP 1.0, giving discharge rate q_e = 1.3*w_e persons/s (PYR 5.2, others 3.9 / 1.95 / 1.3). Demand: n visitors split by floor share (ground 40%, upper 30%, basement 25%). Stage 1 - assignment: minimize sum_{g,e} c_ge*x_ge subject to sum_e x_ge = D_g for every gallery g, x_ge >= 0, where c_ge = t_ge + lambda*(X_e/q_e), t_ge is the Dijkstra travel time from gallery g to exit e at free speed, X_e = sum_g x_ge is the exit's load, and lambda=10 weights the discharge delay. A fixed-point loop updates X_e from the solved x and re-solves until the load vector is stable (converges in <40 iterations); this is the globally-coordinated load-balanced assignment. Stage 2 - makespan: for each active exit e, clearance_e = max_{g: x_ge>0}(t_ge) + X_e/q_e; the evacuation makespan T = max_e clearance_e. Congestion enters two ways: (i) the speed-density relation degrades effective arc speed as loading approaches jam density (modeled as a first-order saturation penalty on over-capacity flow), and (ii) the exit discharge rate q_e caps how fast an exit can drain, producing the queue drain term. Threat scenarios are solved by deleting arcs (blocked route segments) or deleting a node and its incident arcs (the cut-vertex case) and re-solving. The model is reproducible via code/louvre_evac_model.py with all constants on the command line (e.g. --sweep N_VISITORS=3000,5000,8000).

### Outcome Analysis

Base case (n=5000, v=1.34 m/s, four public entrances): makespan T ~ 1282 s (~21 min); the min-cost assignment routes the flow to spread it across the entrances (PYR, RIC, CAR, LION loads), and the dominant term at each exit is the queue drain X_e/q_e. Occupancy sensitivity (v=1.34): n=3000 -> 851 s; n=5000 -> 1282 s; n=8000 -> 1928 s; n=12000 -> 2790 s - the makespan grows roughly linearly with occupancy because the exits discharge at a fixed rate and the building's total discharge capacity is the binding constraint, not travel time. Speed sensitivity (n=5000): v=0.85 -> 1401 s; v=1.34 -> 1282 s; v=1.85 -> 1226 s - makespan falls as speed rises but with diminishing returns, confirming that at realistic occupancies the evacuation is discharge-rate-limited, not walk-speed-limited, so the bottleneck is the exits and their approach corridors, not the free-flow speed. Threat - two blocked shortcut route segments (arc removal, reserve on): makespan rises to ~1616 s (+394 s, +31%), showing that losing secondary routes forces flow back through the hub and inflates the congested corridor. Threat - cut-vertex (hub) removal, the expert's counterexample (exchange 3): the model correctly returns INFEASIBLE - once the central transfer node is gone, at least one gallery (G2) can reach no active exit, so no load-balancing over the four entrances can restore feasibility; this is precisely the model boundary the expert identified (min-cost flow assumes fixed topology and only capacities change; losing a cut vertex disconnects part of the population). Limitations and biases: (i) the model is a stylized network, not a full agent-based simulation - it captures aggregate throughput and makespan but not individual counterflow, panic, or the smoke-filled counterflow region the expert flagged, where the speed-density relation itself degrades; (ii) in the base case the conditional reserve provides no makespan reduction, because at the modeled distances the reserve exits are not on any min-cost path - the model therefore shows the reserve is a resilience feature (it is what makes some threat scenarios feasible) rather than a base-case throughput feature, and this is reported as-is rather than forced; (iii) the makespan is upper-bounded by the slowest-discharging exit, so a single under-provisioned exit dominates the answer (a real bias: the model can understate risk if one exit is degraded); (iv) all times assume the occupants are already distributed at the modeled floor shares and begin moving at t=0, so alarm/detection latency is not included; (v) the cut-vertex case shows the model's hard boundary - it cannot re-route across a removed node, so for the Louvre the design implication is that no single transfer node should be the sole connector for any wing, i.e. the plan must provide redundant cross-links (which the network includes as secondary routes, but a fire can remove even those). Conclusions and policy/procedural recommendations implied by the results: (1) keep the four public entrances at full discharge capacity, since the evacuation is discharge-rate-limited - the single most impactful crowd-control measure is protecting exit and approach-corridor throughput; (2) treat the staff-only exits as a pre-authorized conditional reserve - pre-staged, staffed, and opened by an explicit trigger (a blocked public entrance or a degraded hub) rather than ad hoc, so the security concern is managed while the reserve remains usable; (3) provide redundant cross-links so no wing depends on a single transfer node (the direct design answer to the cut-vertex failure); (4) use a real-time entrance app (Affluences-style) to pre-position signage and staff at the exits the model predicts will carry the largest load; (5) keep emergency-personnel ingress routes (the staff-only doors) clear and segregated from visitor egress flow so staff ingress is not blocked; (6) for other large crowded structures the model transfers directly by re-estimating per-node demand, link lengths/widths, and exit widths, then re-solving the same two-stage min-cost/makespan problem.
