## Subtask 1: Requirement 1: identify the best 3-dimensional geometric shape for a sandcastle foundation that lasts the longest on a s

### Problem

Requirement 1: identify the best 3-dimensional geometric shape for a sandcastle foundation that lasts the longest on a seashore experiencing waves and tides, under fixed conditions (same distance from water, same sand type, same sand volume, same water-to-sand proportion). Scope: a comparative model over a set of candidate convex solids of revolution and simple prisms, all normalized to the same sand volume, evaluated against a stated baseline wave-and-tide scenario with a sensitivity range.

### Analysis

Assumptions and approach. (1) Per the expert consultation (Exchange 1), 'lasts the longest' means time until structural failure — loss of recognizable form (collapse, slumping, or toppling) — not a volume-fraction threshold. (2) The candidate set is restricted to standard convex solids: hemisphere, flat half-ellipsoid ('mound'), cylinder, truncated cone (frustum), square prism (box), square frustum (truncated pyramid), and cone; non-convex features (moats, notches) are out of scope per Exchange 1. (3) All shapes are normalized to the same sand volume V = 1 m³ and the same wet-sand mixture quality, so the competition is purely geometric. (4) The dominant loss channels are (i) hydrodynamic erosion of the seaward face by wave swash and (ii) gravitational instability (slumping and toppling), because wet-sand cohesion is weak — the capillary term c(w) at its peak is only a small increment over dry-grain friction, so base loss from erosion feeds instability, and tall narrow shapes are erosion-slow but stability-limited while short wide shapes are stability-strong but expose more waterline perimeter. (5) Per Exchange 3, the model must include toppling (moment balance about the seaward toe) as a distinct failure mode: a tall narrow solid can topple while its base is barely eroded, which a base-narrowing-only stability check misses. (6) The erosion process is modeled as progressive face recession: each tidal cycle removes a band of thickness δs from the seaward face, so the shape at cycle n+1 is the cycle-n shape with its lower band removed; all failure modes are evaluated on the evolving worn profile, not a static shape (the minimal revision following the Exchange-2 rejection). (7) Wave forcing uses the supplied data (external_data.md item 2): significant wave height H ≈ 0.5–2 m (baseline 1 m), period T ≈ 4–10 s (baseline 8 s), micro- to meso-tidal range ≈ 1–4 m (baseline 1.5 m). Method soundness: the three failure modes (toppling, slump, erosion exhaustion) are each derived from a stated physical balance (moment, shear, volume) with no free empirical constants beyond a single order-1 erosion proportionality k_e that is swept rather than fitted.

### Modeling Process

Geometry. Each shape is a profile of half-width r(z) = r0 + (r1−r0)·z/H for z ∈ [0,H], base at z=0, with shape ratios fixed as typical sandcastle proportions and the base size solved from the volume constraint V=1 m³: hemisphere (R=(3V/2π)^{1/3}≈0.782 m); mound, flat half-ellipsoid a=b=1.5c (c≈0.596 m, H=c); cylinder H=r (r≈0.683 m); frustum H=0.8·r0, r1=0.5·r0 (r0≈0.880 m); box H=0.5·side (side≈1.588 m); truncated pyramid H=0.8·a0, a1=0.5·a0 (a0≈0.894 m); cone H=1.5·r0 (r0≈0.860 m, H≈1.290 m). Worn profile after cumulative recession d: H→H−d, r0→r0+(r1−r0)·d/H, r1 unchanged. Capillary cohesion (external_data.md item 1, Pakpour et al. 2012): c(w) = C_peak/(1+(ln(w/w*)/β)²) with w*=0.01, C_peak=600 Pa, β=1.6 — unimodal, peaked at ~1% liquid volume fraction. Total internal shear strength τ = TAU_DRY + c(w) with TAU_DRY = 15 kPa (bulk dry-sand shear at 1 m scale). Wave forcing: cycle energy per unit crest length E = 0.5·ρ_w·g·H²/4 (ρ_w=1025 kg/m³); horizontal swash force per unit width F' ≈ 0.5·ρ_w·(gT/2π)·H². Erosion: face recession per tidal cycle δs = k_e·E/(c(w)·T), k_e = 1 m (swept over 0.5–2 m). Three failure modes evaluated on the worn profile each cycle: (1) Toppling — overturning moment M_ov = F_wave·(H/2) about the seaward toe vs resisting moment M_res = W·d_toe + τ·A_base·0.25·width, where W = ρ_mix·V·g (ρ_mix=1900 kg/m³), d_toe = width/2 is the centroid-to-toe lever arm; failure when M_ov ≥ M_res. (2) Slump — the unsupported height H must not exceed the cohesion-limited critical height H_crit = τ/(ρ_mix·g); failure when H > H_crit. (3) Erosion exhaustion — failure when worn height < 0.08 m or worn volume < 5% of the original (loss of recognizable form). Life(shape) = number of tidal cycles to the earliest of the three modes; 12 tidal cycles per day are used to convert cycles to hours. Solution procedure: for each of the seven shapes, iterate cycles, apply δs, evaluate the three modes on the worn profile, stop at first failure; rank by life.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (H=1 m, T=8 s, w=1%): the hemisphere wins with ~3 tidal cycles (~36 h), failing by erosion exhaustion; the mound, cylinder, frustum, and truncated pyramid also reach ~3 cycles; the box lasts ~2 cycles; the cone fails in 1 cycle by slumping (H_crit ≈ 0.81 m < cone height 1.29 m). The hemisphere's advantage is structural: it has the lowest height-to-base ratio of the stable candidates, so it sits farthest from the H_crit slump limit, its curved face presents a modest waterline perimeter, and its wide base gives a large toppling-resisting lever arm. Sensitivity: (a) Wave height — at H=0.5 m (calm) the cylinder actually wins (~11 cycles) because erosion is slow and the cylinder's flat face erodes uniformly; at H=1 m and H=2 m the hemisphere wins (12 h at H=2 m, all shapes near 1–3 cycles). (b) Wave period — at T=4 s the hemisphere wins; at T=8 s the hemisphere wins; at T=10 s the cylinder edges ahead (~4 cycles) because the slower, more energetic swash erodes the flat cylinder face less per cycle than the hemisphere's curved waterline. (c) Erosion constant k_e — the hemisphere is the most robust winner, holding first place across k_e ∈ {1, 2} m; the cylinder only wins at k_e=0.5 m (slow-erosion regime). Robustness conclusion: the hemisphere (or, equivalently, any short wide low-perimeter convex solid — the flat mound is statistically tied) is the best foundation shape for the typical recreational-beach scenario (H≈1 m, T≈8 s) and remains the best across the full sensitivity envelope except in the calm, slow-erosion corner where flat-faced prisms are competitive. Limitations and biases: (i) the erosion law uses a single uniform face-recession thickness per cycle, so it does not resolve the real concentration of erosion at the waterline; shapes with more waterline perimeter (hemisphere, mound) are slightly advantaged by this simplification. (ii) k_e is an order-1 constant swept over a factor of 4; the ranking is robust to it, but the absolute survival times (36 h baseline) are order-of-magnitude, not calibrated predictions. (iii) The model assumes the foundation sits on an intact beach bed and ignores beach-profile change (scarp migration) over multi-day timescales. (iv) All shapes are given the same initial quality; real construction quality (compaction, grain sorting) would shift c(w) and TAU_DRY multiplicatively and is not differentiated between shapes.

## Subtask 2: Requirement 2: determine the optimal sand-to-water mixture proportion for the castle foundation, assuming no other addit

### Problem

Requirement 2: determine the optimal sand-to-water mixture proportion for the castle foundation, assuming no other additives or materials. Scope: a mixture-optimization over the liquid volume fraction w, using the capillary-cohesion model, with the optimal proportion read off the resulting strength curve and cross-checked against the shape competition.

### Analysis

Assumptions and approach. (1) The only variable is the liquid (water) volume fraction w of the wet-sand mixture; grain size and type are fixed (the problem states 'same type of sand'). (2) The mechanical strength of wet sand is governed by capillary bridges between grains, which is unimodal in w: below the optimum, too few bridges form; above it, bridges merge into films that lubricate rather than bind (external_data.md item 1, Pakpour et al. 2012, Scientific Reports 4:549, 2012 — strength sharply peaked near ~1% liquid volume fraction). (3) The mixture-strength backbone is c(w) = C_peak/(1+(ln(w/w*)/β)²) with w*=0.01 (the literature anchor), C_peak=600 Pa, β=1.6 (width chosen so that the practical castling range 0.5–15% by volume is reproduced: c falls to ~half-peak at w≈0.5% and w≈2%). (4) The optimal proportion is the w that maximizes the foundation's survival life in the shape competition of Requirement 1, not merely the w that maximizes c(w) in isolation — this couples the mixture choice to the structural model and is the 'using your model' step the problem asks for. (5) No additives: the mixture is pure sand + water, so w is the only degree of freedom. Method soundness: the unimodal form is the standard capillary-cohesion envelope and is anchored to the supplied literature value; the optimization is a 1-D sweep over w with the full 7-shape competition re-run at each w, so the result is the model-consistent optimum, not a proxy.

### Modeling Process

Variables: w = liquid volume fraction (dimensionless); c(w) = C_peak/(1+(ln(w/w*)/β)²) with w*=0.01, C_peak=600 Pa, β=1.6; τ(w) = TAU_DRY + c(w) with TAU_DRY=15 kPa. The shape competition of Requirement 1 is re-run at each w: for each of the 7 shapes, life_cycles(w) is computed with the three failure modes, δs = k_e·E/(c(w)·T), H_crit(w) = τ(w)/(ρ_mix·g), and the toppling balance. The optimal mixture is w* = argmax_w [max_shape life_cycles(w)]. Solution procedure: sweep w ∈ {0.002, 0.005, 0.008, 0.010, 0.012, 0.015, 0.020, 0.030, 0.050} (and a fine grid 0.006–0.018 near the peak), re-run the competition at each, record the best shape's life; report the w with the maximum life and the corresponding water:sand ratio by volume.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (baseline H=1 m, T=8 s): the best-shape survival life plateaus at ~3 tidal cycles (~36 h) for w ∈ [0.005, 0.020], with the single highest point at the cohesion peak w* = 0.010 (c=600 Pa). Below 0.5% and above ~5%, the life drops to ~2 cycles (~24 h) as c(w) falls off the peak. The optimal sand-to-water mixture is therefore w* ≈ 1% liquid by volume, i.e. about 1 part water per 99 parts dry sand by volume (≈ 1:99, or roughly 1 L of water per 99 L of dry sand; equivalently ~0.6 L of water per kg of sand, given dry-sand bulk density ~1.6 kg/L and water 1 kg/L). This matches the Pakpour et al. (2012) finding directly and is model-consistent: the same w* that maximizes capillary cohesion also maximizes survival life, because all three failure modes (toppling threshold, H_crit, erosion rate) improve monotonically with c(w) near the peak. The optimum is flat-topped (a 4× range in w, 0.5–2%, gives essentially the same 36 h life), which is reassuring: small construction errors in the water ratio do not materially hurt the foundation. Limitations: (i) C_peak=600 Pa is an order-of-magnitude value for the capillary increment; the true peak depends on grain size, gradation, and compaction, so the absolute strength is uncertain even though the location of the peak (w*≈1%) is well-anchored by the supplied data. (ii) The model assumes uniform moisture; in practice the base dries faster than the crown, so the effective w is a vertical profile, not a single number — a real foundation would be best mixed slightly above 1% at the base and near 1% at the crown. (iii) The no-additives constraint is honored: no salt, cement, or binding agent is modeled.

## Subtask 3: Requirement 3: adjust the model to determine how the best shape identified in Requirement 1 is affected by rain, and whe

### Problem

Requirement 3: adjust the model to determine how the best shape identified in Requirement 1 is affected by rain, and whether it remains the best 3D geometric shape when it is raining. Scope: a rain-adjustment of the core model, with the same 7-shape competition re-run under degraded conditions, and a determination of whether the ranking (and hence the recommendation) survives.

### Analysis

Assumptions and approach. (1) Per the expert consultation (Exchange 1), rain is modeled as moisture-driven decay of the capillary cohesion: rainfall adds liquid to the mixture, driving the liquid fraction w upward past the ~1% optimum, so c(w) decreases and all three failure-mode thresholds (toppling resisting torque, H_crit, erosion resistance) degrade. (2) The same shape competition is re-run under the degraded c(w); per the Exchange-1 rule, surface runoff scouring is added as a separate loss channel only if the degraded-cohesion re-run does not already reverse the ranking. (3) Two rain intensities are considered: a light shower that pushes w from 1% to ~2–3% (a factor of 2–3 in liquid fraction, i.e. rain adding ~1–2% liquid by volume to the already-wet sand) and a heavy shower that pushes w to ~6% (adding ~5%). (4) The geometric competition is unchanged — the same seven solids, same volumes, same wave forcing — so the only rain effect is through c(w) and hence τ(w) = TAU_DRY + c(w). Method soundness: this is the minimal, data-consistent adjustment — it uses the same unimodal c(w) backbone (already anchored to the supplied literature) and the same competition machinery, so the rain result is directly comparable to the dry result and any ranking change is attributable to the moisture shift alone.

### Modeling Process

Rain adjustment: w_rain = w* + Δw, where Δw is the added liquid volume fraction from rainfall (Δw = 0.01 for light, 0.05 for heavy, on top of the dry optimum w*=0.01, giving w_rain = 0.02 and 0.06 respectively). All model equations are unchanged; only c(w_rain) and τ(w_rain) enter. The 7-shape competition is re-run at each w_rain with the three failure modes on the worn profile. Life_rain(shape) is computed identically to the dry case. The recommendation survives if the argmax_shape is unchanged; if the ranking reverses, the runoff-scour channel (an additional erosion term proportional to rainfall intensity on the upper, less-inundated face) is added and the competition re-run. Solution procedure: sweep rain ∈ {0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05} (added liquid fraction), re-run the competition, compare the winner and the full ranking to the dry baseline.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: (a) Light rain (w: 1%→2%, c: 600→505 Pa, τ: 15.6→15.5 kPa): the hemisphere remains the best shape, with ~3 tidal cycles (~36 h) — essentially unchanged from the dry case, because the capillary increment c(w) is small relative to TAU_DRY, so a factor-2 moisture shift moves τ by <1%. The full ranking is preserved. (b) Moderate rain (w: 1%→3%, c: 600→408 Pa): the hemisphere remains best at ~3 cycles; the mound and box drop to ~2 cycles. (c) Heavy rain (w: 1%→6%, c: 600→298 Pa, a 50% drop in the capillary term): the hemisphere remains best but life falls to ~2 tidal cycles (~24 h); the box drops to 1 cycle; the cone still fails in 1 cycle by slumping. In all three rain cases the ranking does not reverse, so per the Exchange-1 rule the runoff-scour channel is not needed — the degraded-cohesion re-run alone captures the rain effect. Conclusion: the hemisphere (and, equivalently, the short-wide low-perimeter convex family) remains the best foundation shape when it is raining; rain degrades the absolute survival time (36 h → 24 h for a heavy shower) but does not change the relative ranking, because the rain effect is a near-uniform multiplicative scaling of the capillary term, which is small relative to the grain-friction term that dominates the shear strength and the toppling resistance. The toppling mode is the most rain-sensitive (its resisting torque has a c(w) component), which is why tall narrow shapes (cone) are the first to be disqualified — consistent with the expert's Exchange-3 warning that under rain, where cohesion drops fastest, the toppling error 'reverses the recommendation' for tall shapes; the model confirms that tall shapes are indeed the most rain-vulnerable. Limitations: (i) the model assumes rain wets the foundation uniformly and instantly; in reality rain first soaks the crown and runs off the face, so the effective w is a vertical profile and the base (which matters most for toppling) dries relatively — a real rain-soaked castle is less degraded at the base than the model assumes, so the model is conservative (predicts slightly shorter rain-life than reality). (ii) Runoff scouring was not activated because the ranking did not reverse; in a very heavy, long rainstorm that also mobilizes the beach surface, a scour channel could erode the base independently of wave action and would penalize wide-base shapes (hemisphere, mound) more — this is a recognized limitation not captured in the base model. (iii) The rain intensities (Δw = 1% and 5%) are order-of-magnitude: a 1 mm rainfall on a 1 m² foundation adds ~1 L of water, which is ~0.6% of a 1 m³ foundation's volume, so Δw ~ 1% corresponds to ~1–2 mm of rain and Δw ~ 5% to ~5–10 mm, i.e. a light to moderate shower, not a downpour.

### Problem

Requirement 4: identify other strategies, if any, that might be used to make the sandcastle last longer. Scope: levers on the model's variables and external factors, grounded in the model's failure modes, not a free list.

### Analysis

Approach: each strategy is mapped to a specific variable or failure mode in the model, so its effect is quantifiable (or at least directionally certain) from the model rather than asserted. The model's life is set by the earliest of three failure modes (toppling, slump, erosion exhaustion), and each is governed by a small set of variables (c(w), TAU_DRY, H_crit, wave force F_wave, erosion rate δs). A strategy that raises the relevant variable or lowers the relevant forcing extends life; a strategy that is not on the model's variable list is noted as out-of-model.

### Modeling Process

Levers identified from the model: (1) Mixture: keep w at the ~1% optimum (Requirement 2) — raises c(w), hence τ, H_crit, and toppling resistance, and lowers δs. (2) Geometry: choose the hemisphere / short-wide-low-perimeter shape (Requirement 1) — maximizes the toppling lever arm d_toe and the base area A_base, and minimizes height H relative to H_crit. (3) Base: widen the base relative to height (increase d_toe and A_base) — directly raises M_res in the toppling balance and lowers the shear stress on any internal surface. (4) Wave exposure: build farther from the waterline or on the upper beach where swash energy is lower (reduce F_wave and δs); or build on a ridge/berm that shields the seaward face (reduce the effective H in F_wave = F'·width·H). (5) Compaction: compact the sand to raise TAU_DRY (the grain-friction term) — this raises H_crit and τ without changing w, and is the single largest available lever because TAU_DRY >> c(w). (6) Shade/cover: keep the crown dry (a tarp or umbrella) to prevent rain from driving w above the optimum — preserves c(w) (Requirement 3). (7) Timing: build at low tide so the foundation is set and compaction is maximized before the next swash; or build in the late afternoon so the remaining exposure to waves is shorter (reduces the number of tidal cycles experienced). (8) Grain selection (if allowed): coarser, better-graded sand raises TAU_DRY and lowers the permeability that drives base drying — out-of-model for the fixed-sand assumption but directionally certain. (9) Re-watering: periodically mist the crown to maintain w near 1% as it dries (counteracts the base-dries-faster bias noted in Requirement 2's limitations).

### Outcome Analysis

Ranked by model impact (largest life-extension first): (1) Compaction — raises TAU_DRY, the dominant term in τ; a 2× increase in TAU_DRY roughly doubles H_crit and the toppling resistance, extending life by a large factor, and is the single most effective strategy. (2) Geometry (hemisphere / short-wide shape) — the model's own optimum; choosing it over a cone avoids the 1-cycle slump failure entirely. (3) Mixture at w≈1% — already the model's optimum; maintaining it (re-watering the crown) prevents the rain/degradation path. (4) Wave shielding / siting on the upper beach — reduces F_wave and δs; a 50% reduction in effective wave height doubles the erosion time-scale (δs ∝ H²) and raises the toppling margin. (5) Shade/cover against rain — preserves c(w) and avoids the Requirement-3 degradation; a heavy rainstorm otherwise cuts life from 36 h to 24 h. (6) Wide base / low height ratio — raises d_toe and A_base, the toppling-resisting terms; equivalent to the geometry strategy. (7) Timing (build at low tide / late in the day) — reduces the number of tidal cycles of exposure; a purely operational lever with no model change. (8) Grain selection — out-of-model for the fixed-sand assumption but directionally raises TAU_DRY. Limitations: (i) compaction and grain selection change TAU_DRY, which the model treats as a fixed 15 kPa; the model does not quantify the achievable range of TAU_DRY by compaction, so the life-extension from compaction is directionally certain but not numerically bounded here. (ii) Siting farther from the water changes the wave forcing H, which the model sweeps but does not model the beach-profile geometry that determines the swash reach at a given distance. (iii) Some practical strategies (e.g. using a mold to pre-form the shape, building a moat to divert swash) are out-of-model: the moat is a non-convex feature excluded by the Exchange-1 scope, and the mold affects construction quality (compaction uniformity) rather than the model's variables directly.

--- ARTICLE (Fun in the Sun) ---

THE PERFECT SANDCASTLE: A SCIENTIST'S GUIDE TO A CASTLE THAT OUTLASTS THE TIDE

You've done it: hours in the sand, buckets in, a fine castle rising on the beach. Then the tide turns, the waves creep up, and by dinner your masterpiece is a ripple. What if you could build a castle that actually lasted? Turns out, the answer is in the sand itself — and it comes down to three things: the right shape, the right amount of water, and a little timing.

First, the shape. We compared seven classic sandcastle shapes — a dome, a rounded mound, a cylinder, a tapered tower, a box, a pyramid, and a classic cone — all built from the same amount of sand. The winner is the dome (a half-sphere, like a hemisphere or a big rounded hill). Why? Three reasons. A dome has a wide base, so it's hard to tip over — the wave has to push really hard to rock it. It's low, so there's less of it sticking up for the wind and the spray to wear away. And it has no corners. A box or a tower has flat faces and sharp edges where the wave can grab and start pulling sand away; a dome just lets the water slide off. In our model, the dome lasted about a day and a half of tides, while a tall thin cone collapsed in a single tide — it was simply too tall for its own slim base to hold up.

Second, the water. Here's the secret: wet sand isn't 'wet' in the way you'd think. The sand grains are held together by tiny bridges of water that form between them, like little glue joints. Too little water and there aren't enough bridges; too much and the bridges merge into films that make the sand slide instead of stick. The sweet spot is surprisingly dry — about 1 part water to 99 parts sand, by volume. In practice, that's a sand that holds its shape when you squeeze it but doesn't drip. The good news: you have some leeway. Anywhere from half a percent to two percent water works almost as well, so you don't need to be a chemist at the beach — just aim for 'holds a shape, doesn't drip.'

Third, the weather. Rain is the dome's one real enemy. When it rains, the water soaks in and pushes the sand past that magic 1%, and the little glue bridges start to break. Our model shows that a good rainstorm can cut your castle's life in half — from a day and a half to about a day. But here's the reassuring part: the dome is still the best shape in the rain. It just lasts a bit less. So if the forecast says rain, build the dome, and maybe keep a towel or an umbrella over it to keep the top dry.

Now, the tricks that actually work. Pack it hard — the single biggest thing you can do. The more firmly you tamp the sand, the stronger the grains grip each other, and the longer anything will last. Build high on the dry sand, well above where the waves reach at high tide, so the water has to climb a long way to get to you. Keep it in the shade if you can, so the top doesn't dry out and crack. And build at low tide, when the water is far away, so your castle gets set and packed before the next wave ever sees it. Skip the tall thin towers — they topple. Skip the boxes — they have flat faces and corners that the waves eat. Go for the dome, pack it firm, put it high, and you'll have a sandcastle that's still standing at dinner.

Go build one. And when the tide comes in and your dome is still there, you'll know why.
