## Subtask 1: Deliverable 1 — Model the maximum level of single-use/disposable plastic waste that can be safely mitigated without furt

### Problem

Deliverable 1 — Model the maximum level of single-use/disposable plastic waste that can be safely mitigated without further environmental damage, considering waste sources, the extent of the current problem, and the resources available to process waste.

### Analysis

The problem text does not define 'without further environmental damage' as to whether the existing marine stock must shrink or merely stop growing, and supplies no decay rate or harm threshold. Per the expert consultation (Exchange 1), this damage boundary is the governing structural decision. I adopted the expert-accepted structure (Exchange 2): a marine plastic stock M with annual input I (the mismanaged leakage of the short-lived single-use stream) and an annual drawdown D = delta*M, where delta is a single effective removal rate (fragmentation plus export plus retrieval combined). 'No further damage' is defined as zero net growth of the marine stock, I <= D. The safe throughput W* is the generated waste level at which I(W*) = D. Because delta is not in the provided data, W* is reported as a function of delta (a sensitivity band), which is where the parameter bias lives. The expert (Exchange 3) then identified the real failure of the accepted boundary: because the right-hand side delta*M is dominated by the accumulated stock M, a large past stock makes the boundary easy to satisfy, so the accepted structure certifies continued heavy leakage as 'safe' — the safe throughput rises with the very accumulation that is past damage. I therefore also compute a corrected variant, D = min(delta*M, D_cap) with an annual removal capacity D_cap, under which the boundary cannot be satisfied by having already accumulated a large stock. Anchors from the provided data: W0 = 353 Mt waste and 460 Mt production in 2019 (OECD), ~9% recycled, packaging 40% of waste, single-use/disposable band 40-60% (I use 50%); 4.8-12.7 Mt/yr ocean input in 2010 with a regional mismanaged share of 0.5-4.6% (Jambeck). I calibrate the global mismanaged share to the band midpoint (8 Mt/yr), which gives ~4.5% — above the coastal-only band, consistent with the data applying the 0.5-4.6% range to coastal countries while the global rate is higher.

### Modeling Process

Flows and stock: M_{t+1} = M_t + I_t - D_t. Input: I_t = W_t * s * m, where W_t is total single-use-relevant waste, s = 0.5 (single-use/disposable share), m = 0.0453 (global mismanaged share calibrated to the 8 Mt/yr midpoint). Drawdown, accepted: D_t = delta * M_t. Drawdown, corrected: D_t = min(delta * M_t, D_cap) with D_cap = 20 Mt/yr. Safe throughput solves I(W*) = D: W*_accepted = (delta * M0)/(s*m); W*_corrected = min(delta*M0, D_cap)/(s*m). Nominal parameters: delta = 0.01/yr, M0 = 100 Mt, s = 0.5, m = 0.0453, D_cap = 20 Mt/yr. Solution procedure: closed-form for W*, then discrete projection M_{t+1} = M_t + I - D over 40 years to check the stock trajectory. Cross-check: implied 2019 global ocean input I(353) = 353*0.5*0.0453 = 8.0 Mt/yr, inside the Jambeck band 4.8-12.7.

### Outcome Analysis

At the nominal safe-throughput, I(W*) = 1.0 Mt/yr, so the sustainable total waste throughput is W* = 44.2 Mt/yr — an order of magnitude below the current 353 Mt. That is the central finding: the current waste level is far above the level that can be safely mitigated under zero net growth of the marine stock. Sensitivity of W*_accepted to delta (s = 0.5, m = 0.0453, M0 = 100): delta = 0.005 -> 22 Mt; 0.01 -> 44 Mt; 0.02 -> 88 Mt; 0.04 -> 177 Mt. The safe throughput is proportional to the assumed removal rate, so the entire magnitude is parameter-sensitive and should be read as a band, not a point. Bias (the expert-flagged failure): with delta = 0.10 the accepted W* rises with M0 without bound — M0 = 100 -> 441 Mt, M0 = 400 -> 1766 Mt, M0 = 1000 -> 4415 Mt — certifying ever-heavier leakage as safe because the stock is already large. The corrected variant caps this: at M0 = 400, W*_corrected = 883 Mt vs 1766 Mt accepted; at M0 = 1000, 883 Mt vs 4415 Mt. The corrected boundary is the operative one in the report. Limitations: delta and D_cap are declared, not measured; the marine stock M0 = 100 Mt is an order-of-magnitude calibration anchor; a single global delta hides regional dynamics (the expert rated this a resolution artifact, not a boundary failure); the 9% recycling rate anchors current mitigation capacity but the model does not yet couple processing-resource investment to a rising D_cap.

## Subtask 2: Deliverable 2 — Discuss to what extent plastic waste can be reduced to reach an environmentally safe level, considering 

### Problem

Deliverable 2 — Discuss to what extent plastic waste can be reduced to reach an environmentally safe level, considering sources and uses of single-use plastics, availability of alternatives, impacts on citizens' lives, and the effectiveness of regional/city/country/continent policies under regional-specific constraints.

### Analysis

This deliverable asks for the reduction extent, i.e. the gap between the current waste level and the safe level, and the levers that close it. I treat the reduction as acting on the three multiplicative factors of the input I = W*s*m: (a) the total waste W (production and consumption), (b) the single-use share s (design and substitution toward durable or recyclable formats), and (c) the mismanaged share m (collection, recycling, and end-of-waste infrastructure). Regional heterogeneity is built into the core mechanism as the regional mismanaged share, which the data bound at 0.5-4.6% for coastal countries: high-income regions with strong collection sit at the low end, low-income coastal regions at the high end. The same mechanism feeds the equity analysis (Deliverable 4), so the regional differences are emergent from the flow model rather than imposed downstream. Policy effectiveness is read as the ability of a region to move its m toward the low end and to raise its local removal capacity, which is exactly the resource constraint in Deliverable 1.

### Modeling Process

Reduction extent R = (W0 - W_target)/W0, where W_target is the safe throughput. Two decomposition identities: I = W*s*m, so a target input I* can be met by any factorization, e.g. hold s and m and cut W to I*/(s*m), or hold W and s and cut m to I*/(W*s). Regional flow: I_r = W_r*s_r*m_r with m_r in [0.005, 0.046]; the global input is the sum over regions, so the global safe level constrains the weighted sum of regional inputs. Policy levers map to the factors: substitution lowers s; collection/recycling investment lowers m_r and raises local removal capacity; production caps and EPR fees lower W.

### Outcome Analysis

To reach I* = 1.0 Mt/yr from I0 = 8.0 Mt/yr at constant W = 353 and s = 0.5 requires cutting m from 0.0453 to 0.0057 (about a 90% reduction in the mismanaged share) — i.e. near-universal effective collection and processing, which the data's 0.5% low end shows is achievable only in the strongest systems. Alternatively, holding m = 0.0453 and s = 0.5, the waste must fall to 44 Mt (a ~87% cut). The realistic path is a mix: the single-use share s is the largest structural lever because about two-thirds of plastic waste is from short-lived products and packaging is 40% of the stream; moving s from 0.5 toward 0.2-0.3 via substitution and design-for-recycling cuts the input proportionally without requiring every region to reach the 0.5% collection best case. Regional constraints determine which lever is effective where: high-income regions can push m to the low end with infrastructure, whereas low-income coastal regions face the high end and need financing and technology transfer; continent-level policies (bans on specific single-use items, EPR, extended producer responsibility) are most effective where the single-use share is highest. Impact on citizens' lives is the substitution cost and the loss of convenience of disposables, offset by public-health and environmental co-benefits. The extent of feasible reduction is therefore bounded by (i) the availability and cost of alternatives by product class, and (ii) the regional collection/processing capacity, with the largest near-term reductions coming from reducing s in the packaging-heavy, high-income and rapidly-urbanizing regions where both alternatives and infrastructure exist.

### Problem

Deliverable 3 — Using the model and the discussion, set a target for the minimal achievable level of global single-use/disposable plastic waste and discuss the impacts of achieving it (human life, environment, the multi-trillion-dollar plastic industry).

### Analysis

The target is the minimal achievable safe level. I distinguish two levels: the environmentally safe level (zero net growth of the marine stock, Deliverable 1) and the minimal achievable level (the lowest level reachable given substitution and processing constraints by a target horizon). The corrected safe-throughput is the floor for 'no further damage'; the minimal achievable target is set at the corrected safe input, with a timeline for the reduction. Because the expert identified the accepted boundary as ill-defined, the target is anchored to the corrected variant, and I state the parameter band explicitly rather than a single point.

### Modeling Process

Target input I* = 1.0 Mt/yr (the corrected safe input at nominal parameters), corresponding to a total single-use-relevant waste throughput W_target = 44 Mt/yr at s = 0.5, m = 0.0453 — but expressed operationally as a factorization: reduce the single-use share s to ~0.25 and the mismanaged share m to ~0.02, holding total waste at a slowly declining level, so I* = W*s*m holds without requiring an 87% waste cut. Timeline: phase 1 (2025-2030) cut s via substitution and bans on the highest-volume single-use items; phase 2 (2030-2040) cut m via collection and recycling scale-up in the high-mismanaged regions. The marine stock then transitions from net growth to net drawdown once I < D.

### Outcome Analysis

Minimal achievable global single-use/disposable waste target: an annual ocean-relevant input of ~1 Mt/yr (down from the calibrated 8 Mt/yr), reached by 2040, with the marine stock stopping its net growth by ~2030 and beginning a slow drawdown thereafter. Impacts: (i) human life — substitution of disposables with durable or compostable formats changes daily convenience and increases upfront cost for households, but reduces exposure to plastic-borne contaminants and improves air/water quality; the burden falls hardest on low-income regions that must build collection and processing capacity, which is the equity issue addressed next. (ii) environment — holding net ocean input at ~1 Mt/yr and drawing down the marine stock removes the trajectory toward 'more plastic than fish by 2050'; the largest environmental co-benefit is in the coastal low-income regions where the mismanaged share is currently highest. (iii) the plastic industry — a ~87% cut in the single-use throughput (or the equivalent factorization) contracts the disposable-plastics segment sharply while expanding the durable, recycled-content, and chemical-recycling segments; the multi-trillion-dollar industry is not eliminated but restructured toward longer-lived and circular products, with EPR fees internalizing the externalized waste cost. Limitations: the 2040 horizon and the 1 Mt/yr floor are set by the parameter band, not by an independent demand-side estimate; if delta is at the low end (0.005) the safe input is 0.5 Mt/yr and the target is tighter, while at the high end (0.04) it is 4 Mt/yr and looser — the target should be read as the 0.5-4 Mt/yr band with 1 Mt/yr as the planning value.

### Problem

Deliverable 4 — Discuss the equity issues arising from the global crisis and the intended solutions, and how the ICM should address them; then write the two-page memo content (realistic global target minimum, timeline, and circumstances that accelerate or hinder achievement).

### Analysis

Equity enters through the regional mismanaged share and the regional removal capacity. The data bound the coastal mismanaged share at 0.5-4.6%, and the same flow model shows that the regions with the highest m (low-income, coastal, weak collection) both leak the most and have the least processing resource — the double inequity. The ICM's role is to distribute the mitigation burden and the capability so that the safe level is met without concentrating the cost on the regions least responsible for and least able to bear it. The memo consolidates Deliverables 1-3 into a target, a timeline, and the accelerators/hindrances.

### Modeling Process

Equity metric: the per-region contribution to global input I_r = W_r*s_r*m_r vs. the per-region capacity to reduce it (local removal capacity and financing). The burden-sharing rule is that each region's reduction obligation is scaled to its m_r (the mismanaged share it can most cheaply lower) and to its capacity, so that high-m, low-capacity regions receive financing and technology transfer proportional to their gap to the low end (0.5%), while high-capacity regions take the substitution (s) and production (W) reductions. The ICM mechanism: a global fund financed by EPR fees in high-income regions, disbursed to build collection and recycling in high-mismanaged regions, with targets tracked against the regional I_r.

### Outcome Analysis

Equity issues: (i) historical vs. current responsibility — high-income regions generated most of the accumulated marine stock, so the drawdown burden is historically theirs, yet the regions now leaking most are low-income; the fund addresses this by charging the historical emitters. (ii) capability asymmetry — low-income coastal regions cannot reach the 0.5% collection best case without external capacity, so a uniform per-capita target would be unachievable there and would push leakage into informal channels. (iii) trade and price effects — EPR fees and bans raise the cost of disposables, affecting exports from manufacturing regions; the ICM should phase in fees and grandfather existing exporters. How the ICM should address these: (1) a capability-scaled burden rule (reduce m where m is high and cheap to lower; reduce s and W where capacity exists); (2) a global mitigation fund from EPR fees; (3) technology transfer and capacity building for collection and recycling in high-mismanaged regions; (4) a 10-year transition for trade-affected industries. Memo (two-page content): TARGET — a global single-use/disposable plastic ocean-relevant input of ~1 Mt/yr (band 0.5-4 Mt/yr), reached by 2040, equivalent to halving the single-use share and cutting the mismanaged share toward the 0.5-2% range, with the marine stock stopping net growth by ~2030 and entering a slow drawdown. TIMELINE — 2025-2030: bans and substitution on the highest-volume single-use items in high-income and rapidly-urbanizing regions (s from 0.5 to ~0.35); 2030-2035: collection and recycling scale-up funded by the global fund in high-mismanaged coastal regions (m toward ~0.02); 2035-2040: chemical-recycling and recycled-content mandates close the residual gap (m toward ~0.01, s toward ~0.25). ACCELERATORS — falling cost of recycling and alternatives, strong regional bans, EPR fees that internalize waste cost, and public demand for circular products. HINDRANCES — slow build-out of collection in low-income regions, industry resistance to substitution, trade disruption, and parameter uncertainty in the removal rate delta (at the low end the safe input tightens to 0.5 Mt/yr and the timeline stretches; at the high end it loosens to 4 Mt/yr). The target and timeline should be re-based every five years against observed ocean input and regional m_r, and the corrected safe-throughput (not the accepted, ill-defined one) should be the standing planning floor.
