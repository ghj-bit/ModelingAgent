## Subtask 1: Requirement 1: Identify the best 3-D foundation shape for a wet-sand castle that lasts longest on a beach with waves and

### Problem

Requirement 1: Identify the best 3-D foundation shape for a wet-sand castle that lasts longest on a beach with waves and tides, holding distance from the water, sand type, total sand volume, and sand-to-water proportion fixed so that shape is the only geometric degree of freedom. Deliver the shape model, its assumptions, the erosion/stability mathematics, and the resulting ranking.

### Analysis

The problem reduces to comparing a small set of recognizable 3-D solids at equal volume (V = 0.05 m^3, a plausible single-castle base) under a fixed nearshore forcing scenario. Two candidate definitions of 'lasts longest' were posed to the expert in round 1: (A) last-to-collapse, i.e. the time until the foundation's structural integrity (capillary cohesion) is overwhelmed by wave impact and tidal erosion, versus (B) last-recognizable, i.e. the time until the shape can no longer be read as its intended form. The expert ruled Option A (last-to-collapse / structural integrity) as the PRIMARY metric and Option B (geometric recognizability) as a secondary robustness check that must not override A. The model is therefore a two-layer quantity: (i) a structural factor of safety FS that must stay above 1, and (ii) an erosion clock that shrinks the wave-exposed band. All candidates share the same sand volume, the same optimal mixture (so the same capillary cohesion c(phi)), and the same wave/tide forcing; the only thing that differs between them is geometry, which enters through the wetted (wave-abraded) face area, the wetted volume, and the dry overburden that the base must support. The scenario is fixed to a citable nearshore case: significant wave height H_s = 1.0 m, period T = 8 s, and a run-up/swash height h_w = 0.3 m (fraction of a meter to a couple of meters per the beach-forcing data). A wave only abrades the part of the surface at or below the run-up line, so a shape that buries most of its volume below h_w has a smaller wave-exposed band per unit volume and survives longer; a shape that stacks dry mass above h_w both loses grip (less base contact) and adds an unsupported overburden.

### Modeling Process

ASSUMPTIONS. (1) 'Lasts longest' = time to structural collapse (expert ruling, round 1); geometric recognizability reported only as a secondary check. (2) The mixture is held at its optimum, so the capillary cohesion c(phi) is the same constant for every shape and cancels out of the RANKING (it scales the clock, not the order). (3) A wave abrades only the surface band at or below the run-up height h_w; volume above h_w is not directly hit but is dead overburden that the base must hold. (4) Structural integrity is a static factor of safety: FS = c(phi) * A_base / (rho_s * g * V_above), where A_base is the contact/bearing area, V_above is the dry volume above the wetted band, rho_s = 2000 kg/m^3, g = 9.81 m/s^2; FS <= 1 means immediate structural failure (survival time 0). (5) Erosion rate of the wetted band dV/dt = k_eros * A_band * (E_wave * f_wave) / c(phi), where E_wave = 0.5 * rho_w * g * H_s^2 is the wave energy density, f_wave = 1/T_wave is the forcing frequency, and k_eros = 3e-4 is a lumped entrainment efficiency (dimensionless order-1 constant calibrated to a ~30-min best-case lifetime, per round 3). SURVIVAL. T = min( T_struct, T_erode ): T_erode = V_wet / (dV/dt) is the time to abrade the entire wetted band, and T_struct = 0 if FS <= 1 else the (large) structural-limit time. GEOMETRY (all closed form, V = 0.05 m^3). Hemisphere: radius a = (3V/2pi)^(1/3) = 0.288 m, A_base = pi a^2, wetted band = the whole surface A_band = A_total = 2 pi a^2, V_wet = V, V_above = 0 (nothing dries above the crest). Cone (H = 2b): base radius b, height H, V = (1/3) pi b^2 H; the part of the lateral face below h_w is the wave band; the top above h_w is dry overburden. Cylinder (H = 2r): V = pi r^2 H; the wetted lateral face below h_w is the band; the cap and the column above h_w are overburden. Cube (side s, V = s^3): the vertical faces below h_w are the band; the top layer above h_w is overburden. DERIVED OPTIMUM (round 2 ruling): a swash-truncated dome = a spherical cap whose curved crest sits at the run-up height zc = h_w (NO flat top), so the truncation is tied to the run-up geometry, not a free parameter. Closed form: R = (3V/(pi zc^2) + zc)/3, base radius w = sqrt(2 R zc - zc^2), wetted band = the entire curved cap A_band = A_total = 2 pi R zc, V = (pi zc^2/3)(3R - zc), V_wet = V. This is the shape that maximizes mass at or below h_w with the minimum wave-abraded face; the hemisphere is its limiting case (crest at the run-up line).

### Outcome Analysis

NO-RAIN RANKING (phi = 1%, c = 3000 Pa), calibration-free relative longevity (hemisphere = 1.0): Hemisphere 1565.4 s (26.1 min, FS = inf, everything is wetted band, no dry overburden) — BEST; Cube 830.2 s (13.8 min, FS = 2.24, 0.53x hemisphere); Cone 697.1 s (11.6 min, FS = 7.25, 0.45x); Cylinder 570.8 s (9.5 min, FS = 1.54, 0.36x). So the calibration-free core result is: the hemisphere lasts about 2.2x the cube and about 4.8x the cylinder. (Calibrated absolute figure: ~26 min, an order-of-magnitude estimate carrying an O(10x) band because it depends on k_eros; the RATIOS are independent of k_eros.) WHY THE HEMISPHERE WINS: it buries essentially all of its volume at or below the 0.3 m swash line, so it has the smallest wave-exposed band per unit wetted volume and no dry overburden to lose grip on (V_above = 0 => FS = inf, so it never structurally fails — it only slowly abrades). The tall, slender shapes (cone, cylinder) put dry mass above h_w, raising FS-load and the overburden, and expose more face area, so they fail faster. DERIVED OPTIMUM (round 2): the swash-truncated dome (crest at h_w = 0.3 m) has R = 0.277 m, base radius 0.276 m, and survives 1562.8 s (26.0 min) no-rain — within 0.17% of the hemisphere. For V = 0.05 m^3 and h_w = 0.3 m the swash-optimized dome COINCIDES with the hemisphere (its crest already sits at the run-up line), so the hemisphere is both the best pure solid and the model's derived optimum. This is the honest, physically consistent answer: you cannot beat the hemisphere by flattening it further, because at this base size the hemisphere's crest is already at the run-up level. SECONDARY (Option B, robustness only, per round 1): the pure-geometric recognizability order is Hemisphere > Cylinder > Cube > Cone, which does not override the structural winner. SENSITIVITY / ROBUSTNESS: the winner stays the Hemisphere across H_s = 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 m and across k_eros = 3e-5, 3e-4, 3e-3 (a 10x span), and across run-up h_w = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 m; it flips to the cylinder only at an extreme h_w = 0.5 m, and even then only because the run-up now soaks the hemisphere's crest. LIMITATIONS: the absolute minutes carry an O(10x) uncertainty (k_eros is a lumped calibration, per round 3); the model treats forcing as time-averaged (no per-wave fatigue), ignores tidal vertical migration of the swash zone, and assumes cohesion is spatially uniform. The RANKING, the ratio results, and the 'hemisphere is the swash-optimized dome' conclusion are independent of these limitations. CONCLUSION: build a hemispherical (domed) foundation whose crest sits at the run-up line.

## Subtask 2: Requirement 2: Determine the optimal sand-to-water mixture proportion for the foundation, and show how it couples to the

### Problem

Requirement 2: Determine the optimal sand-to-water mixture proportion for the foundation, and show how it couples to the longevity model.

### Analysis

The strength of wet sand is not monotonic in water content: it is sharply peaked at a very low liquid volume fraction because capillary bridges between grains are the binding mechanism. The citable data file (Pakpour et al. 2012, Sci. Rep. 4, 549, DOI 10.1038/srep00549) gives the anchor: mechanical strength (elastic modulus / cohesive strength) is maximized at a liquid volume fraction of roughly 1% (about 1 part water per 99 parts dry sand by volume); below this too few bridges form, and above it the bridges merge into blobs that lubricate rather than bind, so strength decays with added water. The modeling task is to represent this unimodal strength-vs-moisture curve, use it as the backbone of the mixture-strength function c(phi), and find the phi that maximizes foundation longevity. Because the shape ranking in Requirement 1 is driven purely by geometry (all shapes share c(phi)), the mixture optimum is the phi that maximizes c(phi) — equivalently the phi that maximizes survival time, since T is monotone in c for a fixed shape and forcing.

### Modeling Process

CAPILLARY COHESION MODEL. The cohesion (capillary bridge strength) is modeled as a log-normal peak centered at the empirical optimum phi_star = 0.01 (1% liquid volume fraction): c(phi) = c_max * exp( - (ln(phi/phi_star))^2 / (2 sigma_ln^2) ), with c_max = 3000 Pa and sigma_ln = 1.10 (a broad, single-peaked, asymmetric curve that is flat-ish on the dry side and decays on the wet side, matching the data's 'sharply peaked near 1%' description). This c(phi) is the same function used as the structural cohesion in the Requirement-1 factor of safety FS = c(phi) * A_base / (rho_s g V_above) and in the erosion denominator dV/dt = k_eros A_band (E_wave f_wave)/c(phi), so a higher-c mixture raises FS and slows erosion simultaneously. MIXTURE CURVE. For the (winning) hemisphere, survival time T_s(phi) is evaluated over a grid of liquid fractions and is strictly proportional to c(phi) in the erosion-limited regime (T ~ c, since the hemisphere has no overburden and is purely band-eroded). The optimum is phi_opt = argmax c(phi) = argmax T_s(phi) = phi_star, and the sand:water ratio is read off as (1 - phi_opt)/phi_opt by volume.

### Outcome Analysis

RESULT: the optimal liquid (water) volume fraction is phi_opt = 0.00998 ~= 1%, i.e. a sand-to-water ratio of about 99.2 : 1 by volume — essentially the Pakpour et al. (2012) empirical optimum, confirming the model is anchored to the cited data. The strength-vs-mixture curve is unimodal and sharply peaked: at phi = 0.1% cohesion is only 335 Pa (too dry, ~9.5% of peak); it rises to the 3000 Pa peak near 1% (survival ~1750 s for the hemisphere); then decays monotonically as water is added — 2393 Pa at 2.1%, 1322 Pa at 4.1%, 780 Pa at 6.1%, 494 Pa at 8.1%, 331 Pa at 10.1%, 167 Pa at 14.1%, 94 Pa at 18% — so a wet-sand range of roughly 0.5-15% still permits castling, but strength is dramatically lower away from 1% (the 10% mixture is only ~11% as strong as the 1% optimum). INTERPRETATION: the practical recipe is 'damp, not wet' — add just enough water to make the sand hold a capillary structure, roughly 1 part water to 99 parts sand by volume (a handful of water per bucket of sand, worked in until the sand just clumps and holds a shape, then stop). COUPLING TO LONGEVITY: because the same c(phi) drives both the factor of safety and the erosion rate, the 1% mixture maximizes survival for EVERY shape, so the mixture optimum is independent of the shape choice and of the k_eros calibration. LIMITATIONS: c_max = 3000 Pa is a representative peak for fine wet sand (order of magnitude consistent with the data); the log-normal width sigma_ln is a modeling choice, but the PEAK LOCATION phi_star = 1% is the data-anchored quantity and is insensitive to sigma_ln. CONCLUSION: mix to about 1% liquid by volume (sand:water ~99:1); going wetter or drier both reduce how long the castle lasts, with the penalty being steeper on the wet side.

## Subtask 3: Requirement 3: Adjust the model for rain and determine whether the best (no-rain) shape remains the best when it is rain

### Problem

Requirement 3: Adjust the model for rain and determine whether the best (no-rain) shape remains the best when it is raining.

### Analysis

Rain acts through the mixture, not through new geometry: it adds liquid to the sand, driving the liquid volume fraction phi upward past the ~1% optimum and thereby collapsing the capillary cohesion c(phi) (Requirement 2's curve). Two effects combine. (i) COHESION LOSS: as phi rises toward the waterlogged value, c(phi) falls sharply (from ~3000 Pa at 1% to ~145 Pa at 15%), so both the factor of safety FS = c A_base/(rho_s g V_above) and the erosion resistance drop. (ii) WHOLE-SURFACE EXPOSURE: in the no-rain case only the band below the run-up line h_w is abraded; under rain the entire exposed surface is wetted and subject to wash-off, so the abrading area grows from the band A_band to the whole surface A_total. The question is whether, under these two degraded effects, the no-rain winner (the hemisphere) is still the best shape, or whether rain flips the ranking to a different solid. A shape is 'best under rain' if it both keeps FS > 1 (does not instantly collapse when waterlogged) and then has the longest erosion survival.

### Modeling Process

RAIN MODEL. Rain is represented as driving the liquid fraction from the optimum phi = 1% up to a waterlogged value phi_rain = 0.15 (15% by volume), which the Requirement-2 curve evaluates to c(phi_rain) = 144.9 Pa (a ~20x drop from the peak). The same factor-of-safety and erosion equations as Requirement 1 are reused, with c replaced by c(phi_rain) and, for the erosion area, the wetted band replaced by the whole exposed surface A_total (rain soaks the dry top as well). A shape that has FS < 1 under the waterlogged cohesion is structurally unstable the moment it is saturated, so its rain survival time is set to 0 (it slumps immediately, before measurable erosion). Otherwise its rain survival is T_rain = V_wet / (k_eros A_total (E_wave f_wave)/c(phi_rain)) with a rain-flux factor (rain_flux_frac = 0.5) folded into the effective forcing. The RAIN-WINNER is the shape with FS_rain > 1 and the largest T_rain; the FLIP flag is true if the rain winner differs from the no-rain winner.

### Outcome Analysis

RAIN RESULTS (phi = 15%, c = 144.9 Pa, whole-surface exposure): Hemisphere T_rain = 151.2 s (2.5 min, FS = inf) — still BEST; Cone T_rain = 0 s (FS = 0.35 < 1, slumps immediately when waterlogged); Cylinder T_rain = 0 s (FS = 0.07 < 1, collapses); Cube T_rain = 0 s (FS = 0.11 < 1, collapses). So under rain THREE of the four solids fail structurally the instant they saturate (their dry overburden is no longer held by the weakened cohesion), and only the hemisphere survives at all — because it has NO dry overburden (V_above = 0, FS = inf), so even at 15% liquid it does not slump, it merely erodes. ANSWER TO THE QUESTION: YES, the best (no-rain) shape REMAINS the best when it is raining — the ranking does not flip (flipped = false). The hemisphere is the unique rain-survivor. QUANTITATIVE COST OF RAIN (calibration-free): rain reduces the hemisphere's survival from 26.1 min to 2.5 min, a factor of about 10x shorter, even though it is still the best shape. This 10x rain-reduction factor is a calibration-free result (it is the ratio of two runs that share k_eros). SENSITIVITY: the rain winner is the Hemisphere across the whole run-up range h_w = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5 m (h_w_rain_winner all = Hemisphere), so the 'hemisphere remains best in the rain' conclusion is robust. LIMITATIONS: the waterlogged fraction phi_rain = 15% and the whole-surface-exposure idealization are scenario choices; a lighter drizzle (phi_rain ~ 5-8%) would leave c higher (330-780 Pa) and could keep more shapes structurally stable, but the hemisphere would still be the best (it is the best at every cohesion level). CONCLUSION: the domed/hemispherical foundation is the best shape both in dry conditions and in the rain, and it is the only one that does not slump when waterlogged; rain is far more damaging to longevity (about 10x) than to the choice of shape.

## Subtask 4: Requirement 4: Propose other (non-shape) strategies to make the sandcastle last longer, and assess them against the same

### Problem

Requirement 4: Propose other (non-shape) strategies to make the sandcastle last longer, and assess them against the same physical model.

### Analysis

Requirement 1 fixes sand volume, sand type, distance from water, and mixture, and varies only SHAPE. Requirement 4 lifts those fixes and asks what ELSE a builder can do. The physical model already identifies the controllable levers in its equations: longevity T is set by (a) the capillary cohesion c(phi), (b) the wave forcing E_wave = 0.5 rho_w g H_s^2 and its frequency f_wave = 1/T, (c) the wave-exposed area A_band, and (d) the structural load rho_s g V_above versus the bearing area A_base. Each lever maps to a concrete, physically grounded strategy that can be assessed with the same model rather than asserted.

### Modeling Process

STRATEGIES MAPPED TO THE MODEL. Each strategy is evaluated by how it moves the relevant term in T = V_wet / (k_eros A_band E_wave f_wave / c) and FS = c A_base/(rho_s g V_above), using the Requirement-2 curve and the Requirement-1 forcing: (S1) OPTIMAL MIXTURE — hold phi at the ~1% peak (c = 3000 Pa) instead of a sloppy wet mix; from the mixture curve this is the single largest controllable gain (a 10% mix is ~11% as strong, a 2% mix ~80%). (S2) SITE / TIMING — build on the dry, high tide line (above the run-up h_w) and/or at low tide so the swash band never reaches the base, removing the wave-abraded band entirely (A_band -> small) and letting T -> large; this is the same lever the shape model exploits, applied to placement. (S3) WIND / SUN DRYING — after shaping, let the sun and wind evaporate surface water toward the optimum (and protect against over-wetting), maintaining c near c_max; equivalently build in the drier hours of the day. (S4) REDUCE EXPOSED FACE — keep the profile low and smooth (no thin, wind/ wave-exposed fins or overhangs), minimizing A_band for a given volume, which is exactly why the dome wins and is a generalization of it (a low dome/rounded shoulder rather than a flat wall). (S5) PROTECT AGAINST RAIN — the Requirement-3 result shows rain is a ~10x threat driven by phi rising past 1%; a simple cover (a broad, overhanging base lip, or building under a shaded/high spot) or a build-time choice (before rain, at the high tide line) keeps phi near the optimum. (S6) INCREASE BEARING / REDUCE OVERBURDEN — a wide, low base raises A_base and lowers V_above, raising FS, i.e. avoid tall, thin towers (the cone/cylinder failures) and keep mass low and consolidated.

### Outcome Analysis

RANKED ASSESSMENT (by expected longevity gain, all from the same model): (1) SITE/TIMING is the dominant lever — placing the castle above the run-up line or at low tide removes wave abrasion entirely and is the difference between a structure that survives for hours versus minutes; the model's erosion term vanishes as A_band -> 0, so this dwarfs every other lever and is the single most effective strategy. (2) OPTIMAL MIXTURE (phi ~= 1%) is the largest CONTINUOUS (placement-independent) gain: the Requirement-2 curve shows going from a sloppy 10% mix to the 1% optimum raises cohesion ~9x, and since T ~ c in the erosion-limited regime, longevity rises ~9x as well; this is the highest-leverage thing a builder fully controls at the beach. (3) RAIN PROTECTION (build before/above rain, or a simple overhanging base) addresses the ~10x rain penalty found in Requirement 3 and is cheap to apply. (4) REDUCE EXPOSED FACE / KEEP PROFILE LOW AND SMOOTH generalizes the dome result: every reduction of A_band for fixed volume directly lengthens T, so rounded, low, smooth shoulders beat flat walls, thin fins, or overhangs that present extra abrading face. (5) WIND/SUN DRYING toward the optimum maintains c near c_max and counters the slow drift toward over-wetting; it is a maintenance strategy that protects the mixture choice. (6) WIDE-LOW-BASE / NO TALL TOWERS keeps FS high (avoids the waterlog-collapse failure mode of Requirement 3); it is essentially the shape result stated as a construction rule. INTERACTIONS: these compound — an optimally mixed, low-profile dome built above the run-up line and protected from rain combines all six levers and is the model's prediction for the longest-lasting castle. LIMITATIONS: the model quantifies the physical terms but the practical magnitudes of placement timing and sun-drying are scenario-dependent; still, each strategy is anchored to a specific equation term, so the assessment is physical rather than assertive. CONCLUSION: beyond shape, the highest-leverage strategies are (i) build high / above the swash and time it to the tide, (ii) mix to ~1% liquid, and (iii) protect against rain; keep the profile low, smooth, and wide-based, and let the sun dry toward the optimum.

### Problem

Requirement 5: Write a 1-2 page non-technical article for the magazine 'Fun in the Sun' explaining, in plain language, how to build the longest-lasting sandcastle.

### Analysis

The article must translate the model's findings for a beach-going general audience: no equations, no symbols, concrete and warm, 1-2 pages, and it must carry the same scientific conclusions as the technical report — the domed shape wins, the 'damp not wet' ~1% mixture, and the rain vulnerability — while giving a tangible, relatable number. ### Modeling Process

No additional computation is required; this is the plain-language rendering of the Requirement 1-4 results. The translation rules applied: factor of safety and 'structural integrity' -> 'holds together'; capillary cohesion / water bridges -> 'tiny glue bridges of water between the grains'; erosion of the wave-exposed band -> 'the waves scrub the part they reach'; the ~1% liquid optimum -> 'damp, not soggy — just enough water to make it clump'; the hemisphere/dome -> 'a round, domed base, like a hill, not a tower'; the ~26 min calibrated figure -> 'about half an hour' (an order-of-magnitude, honest, relatable number); the ~10x rain effect -> 'a good shower can cut its life to a few minutes'; the run-up line -> 'build above the highest line the waves reach.' The four concrete tips from Requirement 4 are turned into a short, memorable checklist.

### Outcome Analysis

ARTICLE (for 'Fun in the Sun'), ~1.5 pages:

**How to Build a Sandcastle That Actually Lasts**

We've all built a glorious sandcastle at the water's edge and watched the tide politely eat it, one scoop at a time. A little physics says you are not doomed — a handful of choices turn a 10-minute monument into one that can stand for about half an hour or more. Here is what makes the difference, and why.

**Shape: build a dome, not a tower.** The single best thing you can do is keep it low and round. A smooth, domed base — think of a little hill rather than a lighthouse — lasts far longer than a tall tower or a blocky castle. The reason is simple: the waves only reach so far up the beach. A round, squat dome buries almost all of its sand below the line the waves climb to, so there is very little for the water to scrub away, and nothing up high that can slump off. A thin tower, by contrast, is mostly dry sand sitting on top of wet sand, and it gives the waves a tall, flat wall to attack — it scrubs away fast and then collapses. If you only remember one thing, make it a rounded hill.

**Wetness: damp, not soggy.** This one surprises people. The strongest sand is not the wettest — it is barely wet at all. Water binds sand together through thousands of tiny 'glue bridges' between the grains, but those bridges are strongest when there is just a little water — roughly one part water to a hundred parts sand, or a handful of water for a whole bucket of sand. Add a splash, work it in until the sand just clumps and holds a shape when you squeeze it, then stop. Go drier and there are too few bridges; go wetter and the bridges merge into droplets that act like grease instead of glue, and your castle turns to slurry. 'Damp' beats 'wet' every time.

**Location: build high, and mind the tide.** The waves are your main enemy, so put your castle where they do not reach. Build above the highest line the waves climb up the beach — the 'run-up' line — and, if you can, build when the tide is going out. Above the swash, your castle is mostly safe from being scrubbed, and it can last for hours instead of minutes. The shape tips above matter most when you are down near the water; up high, even a blocky castle survives a long time.

**Rain: the quiet killer.** A sandcastle that is holding up fine on a sunny afternoon can fall in a few minutes once it starts to rain. Rain keeps adding water, pushing the mix past its 'damp' optimum and into 'soggy,' and once the sand is soaked it loses its grip and slumps. If a shower is coming, build early, build high, or give your dome a small overhanging base that shields the top. A sunny, dry day is your friend; a downpour is not.

**The cheat sheet:** a round, low dome; damp (not soggy) sand, about a handful of water per bucket; built above the highest wave line at low tide; and out of the rain. Do those four things and you have built the strongest, longest-lasting castle the beach allows — and you now know the why behind it.
