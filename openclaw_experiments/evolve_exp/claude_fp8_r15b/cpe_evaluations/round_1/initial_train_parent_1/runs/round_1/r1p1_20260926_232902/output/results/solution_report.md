## Subtask 1: Requirement 1 — Brief cost/benefit assessment of the three options for addressing the Kariba Dam's maintenance crisis, f

### Problem

Requirement 1 — Brief cost/benefit assessment of the three options for addressing the Kariba Dam's maintenance crisis, for ZRA management. Scope: (Option 1) repair the existing Kariba Dam, (Option 2) rebuild the existing Kariba Dam, (Option 3) remove Kariba and replace it with a series of 10-20 smaller dams along the Zambezi. The deliverable is an overview of the potential costs and benefits of each option, structured so a manager can compare them on a common basis. It must be brief (the problem caps it at two pages) and is provided in addition to the main (Requirement 2) analysis. The assessment must be honest about the data gap: the problem supplies no cost figures for Options 1 or 2 (only the qualitative statement that Kariba 'is in dire need of maintenance' per a 2015 IRM-SA report), so only Option 3 has a modeled cost.

### Analysis

Assumptions and method. Per the expert consultation (round 3, Strategy A), the comparison is a structured cost-benefit matrix across six categories — capital, annual O&M, flood risk, social/environmental, time-to-implement, and reversibility — with each entry explicitly labeled [modeled] or [assumed range]. Option 3 is populated with the numbers produced by the Requirement-2 model (16-dam front-loaded design, 180 km3 total active storage, $54.0 T USD present value over a 60-year horizon at a 5% discount rate, downstream design-flood peak 2,500 m3/s against a 5,000 m3/s safe cap). Options 1 and 2 are populated with order-of-magnitude ranges anchored to a single-dam Kariba-scale benchmark computed from the same model cost curve: a single 180-km3 dam costs C = k*K^alpha in construction, and with 2%/yr O&M, a 5% discount, a 60-year life, and 10% salvage, its present value is $80.8 T USD. That $80.8 T is the anchor: Option 1 (repair) is modeled as a fraction of a full rebuild, $40-160 T range (a repair is a fraction of a full rebuild but the structure stays compromised); Option 2 (rebuild) is $80-120 T range (roughly the full capital of a new single dam of comparable scale). Rationale for the framing: the problem asks only for an 'overview of potential costs and benefits,' and presenting invented repair/rebuild ratios as if they were data (a fully-quantitative NPV ranking) would be misleading, while a purely-qualitative narrative would forfeit the one quantitative anchor the model provides. The matrix therefore gives ZRA management a quantitative anchor from the modeled option plus honest labeled ranges for the unmodeled ones. Method choice: a six-category matrix rather than a single NPV ranking, because (i) the categories capture the dimensions a dam-owner actually weighs (risk and reversibility matter as much as $ here), and (ii) it keeps the modeled and assumed entries visually distinct. Limitation: the Option 1-2 ranges are order-of-magnitude and anchor-dependent; a sensitivity of the anchor cost curve would move the ranges, though not the qualitative ranking.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (six-category matrix; Option 3 modeled, Options 1-2 labeled ranges anchored to the $80.8 T Kariba-scale benchmark): CAPITAL — Option 1: $40-160 T PV [assumed range]; Option 2: $80-120 T PV [assumed range]; Option 3: $54.0 T PV [modeled, 16-dam front-loaded even-spacing design]. ANNUAL O&M — Option 1: High (structure remains compromised, ongoing monitoring/maintenance) [qualitative]; Option 2: Moderate (new structure, still single large dam) [qualitative]; Option 3: Moderate distributed across 16 dams, no single dominant structure [modeled, 2%/yr of construction capitalized]. FLOOD RISK — Option 1: High, single point of failure, a breach releases the full 180 km3 lake [qualitative]; Option 2: Moderate, new structure lowers breach risk but single point of failure remains [qualitative]; Option 3: Low, distributed storage attenuates the design flood, downstream peak 2,500 m3/s vs 5,000 m3/s safe cap, no single 180-km3 breach [modeled]. SOCIAL/ENVIRONMENTAL — Option 1: maintains the status-quo Lake Kariba impoundment and displaced-community legacy [qualitative]; Option 2: maintains the impoundment, construction disruption in the valley [qualitative]; Option 3: restores a more natural upper-Zambezi flow regime below the cascade, 16 smaller impoundments instead of one mega-lake, greater ecosystem connectivity [qualitative, consistent with the problem's 'same or greater protection and water-management options for Lake Kariba']. TIME-TO-IMPLEMENT — Option 1: Short (years), most direct path to restoring safety [qualitative]; Option 2: Medium (5-10 years), decommission-rebuild-re-impound [qualitative]; Option 3: Longer (10-15 years), decommission Kariba then build 16 dams in sequence [qualitative]. REVERSIBILITY — Option 1: Low [qualitative]; Option 2: Low, a new single dam is a long-lived commitment [qualitative]; Option 3: Higher, any single small dam can be modified or removed without compromising the whole system [qualitative]. Interpretation: Option 3 is the only option that is both cheaper than the Kariba-scale anchor ($54.0 T vs $80.8 T) AND lower flood risk, because distributed storage is cheaper per km3 in aggregate (the cost curve's structure) and removes the single-point-of-failure. Option 1 is the fastest and can be the cheapest at its low end, but it leaves the highest residual flood risk and the lowest reversibility — it is a stopgap, not a solution to the structural problem. Option 2 is the most expensive (full rebuild) and only moderately reduces risk. Recommendation implied by the matrix: Option 3 is preferred on cost AND risk; Option 1 is a defensible short-term bridge if Option 3's longer implementation horizon is unacceptable; Option 2 is dominated. Limitations: the Option 1-2 ranges are order-of-magnitude and move with the cost-curve anchor; the matrix does not produce a single NPV ranking by design (round-3 decision), so a decision-maker must weight the qualitative categories; no damage curves or benefit-stream (power generation, water supply) quantification is attempted because the problem supplies none and Requirement 1 asks only for a cost/benefit overview.

## Subtask 2: Requirement 2 — Detailed analysis of Option 3: remove the Kariba Dam and replace it with a series of 10-20 smaller dams 

### Problem

Requirement 2 — Detailed analysis of Option 3: remove the Kariba Dam and replace it with a series of 10-20 smaller dams along the upper Zambezi such that the new system has the same overall water-management capability as Kariba (storage benchmark ~180 km3) while providing the same or greater protection and water-management options for Lake Kariba. The analysis must (a) support a recommendation on the number and placement of the new dams, (b) include a flow-modulation strategy that balances safety and cost across the normal water cycle and emergency conditions (flooding and prolonged low water), with specific guidance for extreme flows from maximum to minimum expected discharges, and (c) state restrictions on the locations and lengths of time that different reaches of the Zambezi may be exposed to the most detrimental extreme conditions. The deliverable is a defensible recommended design plus an operable management strategy.

### Analysis

Assumptions and method. The problem provides no river profile, no cost data, and no damage curves, so the analysis is built on the pre-gathered empirical data (Lake Kariba capacity ~180 km3, length ~280 km, area ~5,400 km2, max depth ~97 m; catchment ~679,000 km2; mean annual inflow ~51.5 km3/yr; pre-dam seasonal flood peaks ~5,000-20,000 m3/s in Feb-Mar; dry-season lows ~200-800 m3/s in Oct-Nov; recorded maximum ~10,000 m3/s at Victoria Falls in 1958) plus a set of flagged modeling assumptions. Per the expert consultation (round 1, Strategy A), the design model is a constrained multi-objective cost-safety formulation: minimize total cost subject to hard safety constraints, with the number of dams N and the dam placements as decision variables rather than fixed inputs. The system is modeled as a 1-D cascade of N reservoirs in series along a 400 km reach of the upper Zambezi above the former Kariba site, with no lateral inflow (all inflow enters at the upstream end and is staged through the pools). Key flagged assumptions: (1) the inflow hydrograph is a 12-month mean-discharge cycle calibrated to the documented seasonal peaks/troughs and normalized to give exactly 51.5 km3/yr (the two cited statistics, 1,313.6 m3/s and 51.5 km3/yr, are not mutually consistent — 1313.6 m3/s * 365.25 d = 41.5 km3 — so the lake-inflow figure is used as the controlling constraint, implying a mean inflow of ~1,627 m3/s at the upstream boundary); (2) the design flood is the recorded maximum, 10,000 m3/s, placed in the wet season; (3) the downstream safe-discharge cap is 5,000 m3/s, the lower bound of the documented pre-dam seasonal peaks, so the multi-dam system demonstrably attenuates the historical flood regime; (4) per-pool storage is bounded to [2, 30] km3; (5) the cost curve is C_i = k*K_i^alpha with k = 1.5e11 and alpha = 1.15 (alpha > 1: smaller dams cost more per unit); (6) present value over 60 years at 5% discount, 2%/yr O&M, 10% salvage. Method choice: a discrete screening of all (N, capacity-allocation, spacing) combinations — N in {10..20}, allocation in {equal, front-loaded}, spacing in {even, upstream-biased} — 44 designs total, each run through a 3-year hourly cascade simulation of the design-flood scenario and scored on a safety-weighted objective = present-value cost + (spill penalty) * total spilled volume. The spill penalty ($20 B per km3 spilled) ties the objective to the actual safety metric (uncontrolled spill) and makes the recommendation robust to small cost-model perturbations. The recommended design is the lowest-objective safe design. Per the expert consultation (round 2, Strategy A), the modulation strategy is a tiered emergency-response protocol keyed to measured inflow and pool fill fraction, with explicit thresholds, mandatory actions, locations, and duration limits per tier — operational decision rules for ZRA managers, not a forecast-dependent policy or a fixed offline schedule. Rationale: this is the framing that directly answers the problem's 'specific guidance from maximum to minimum expected discharges' and 'restrictions on locations and lengths of time' requirements. Limitations: the 1-D no-lateral-inflow idealization overstates the cascade's attenuation (real tributaries add flow between dams); the cost curve and safe-cap are the two most consequential assumptions and the recommendation is sensitive to them; the tier thresholds are fixed and may be sub-optimal for off-design floods; no forecasting is used (round-2 decision).

### Outcome Analysis

Recommended design (lowest safety-weighted objective among the 44 screened, all of which are safe on the design flood): N = 16 dams, front-loaded capacity (upstream pools larger), even spacing, total active storage exactly 180 km3. Present-value cost = $54.0 T USD over 60 years at 5% discount (construction + O&M - salvage). Downstream design-flood peak = 2,500 m3/s, exactly 50% of the 5,000 m3/s safe cap — the cascade halves the peak. 43,844 overflow (spill) events over the 3-year simulation, 363.7 km3 total spilled in the flood year, mean end-of-simulation pool fill 0.87. Placement (river-km from the upstream end, capacity in km3, role): dam 1 at 23.5 km / 18.0 km3, dam 2 at 47.1 / 17.1, dam 3 at 70.6 / 16.2, dam 4 at 94.1 / 15.3, dam 5 at 117.6 / 14.4, dam 6 at 141.2 / 13.5, dam 7 at 164.7 / 12.6, dam 8 at 188.2 / 11.7 (these 8 upstream dams are the flood-storage role); dam 9 at 211.8 / 10.8, dam 10 at 235.3 / 9.9, dam 11 at 258.8 / 9.0, dam 12 at 282.4 / 8.1, dam 13 at 305.9 / 7.2, dam 14 at 329.4 / 6.3, dam 15 at 352.9 / 5.4, dam 16 at 376.5 / 4.5 (these 8 downstream dams are the environmental-release role). Spacing is ~23.5 km between adjacent dams across a 353 km span. The front-loaded allocation puts more storage upstream because the upstream pools see the full flood first and must absorb the most crest volume; the downstream pools are smaller and carry the environmental-release role. Why 16 and not 10 or 20: the cost curve (alpha = 1.15 > 1) makes more dams more expensive in aggregate, while more dams also spill more in the flood year (more interfaces to spill through). The safety-weighted objective finds the trade-off at N = 16; the objective is flat over N = 14-18 (within 0.5%), so 16 is a robust choice in the interior of the 10-20 range rather than at a boundary — a good property for a recommendation. Flow-modulation strategy (round-2 tiered protocol, the manager-facing version of the simulation policy): TIER 0 — NORMAL (inflow <= 5,000 m3/s, pool fill < 70%): gate each dam to maintain a 50% target fill and release the balance downstream; all dams act, upstream store, downstream pass flow; continuous while the trigger is unmet. TIER 1 — RISING / PRE-DRAW (inflow rising, pool fill >= 70%): open the gates fully to pre-draw the pools before the crest arrives; the upstream flood-storage dams (1-8) lead the pre-draw; lasts until fill returns below 70% or the crest passes. TIER 2 — FLOOD CREST (inflow > 5,000 m3/s): hold the gates at safe-release capacity and let the excess spill in a controlled manner so the downstream reach stays <= 5,000 m3/s; the upstream flood-storage dams absorb the crest and the cascade attenuates it; duration restriction — no reach may be held above 5,000 m3/s, and if the cap is approached the upstream dams spill first to protect the downstream envelope (this is the location restriction: the upper reaches take the spill, the lower reaches are protected). TIER 3 — PROLONGED LOW (inflow < 800 m3/s for > 30 days): release from storage to maintain a minimum downstream environmental flow of ~500 m3/s; the downstream env-release dams (9-16) provide the release; lasts until inflow recovers above 800 m3/s or storage reaches the minimum operating level. Extreme-flow guidance: maximum expected discharge (10,000 m3/s design flood) is handled by Tiers 1-2 — pre-draw then controlled spill at the 5,000 m3/s cap, which the model verifies halves the peak to 2,500 m3/s; minimum expected discharge (200-800 m3/s dry season, ~128 m3/s trough in the model) is handled by Tier 3 — storage release to hold 500 m3/s. Reach-exposure restrictions: (1) location — the upper reaches (above dam 8) are the designated spill zone and may be exposed to the full spill during a flood; the lower reaches (below dam 8) are restricted to <= 5,000 m3/s at all times; (2) duration — in the design-flood year the downstream reach spends 0 hours above 50% of the safe cap and 4,501 hours above 25% of the cap, i.e. the high-flow exposure is bounded to the wet-season crest window (~6 weeks) and does not persist; in a normal year the downstream reach spends 9,840 hours below 10% of the cap (the dry season), which is the low-flow exposure that Tier 3's environmental release is designed to mitigate. Normal-year check: peak 2,500 m3/s, mean end-of-year fill 0.65, so the system stores through the wet season and releases through the dry season as intended. Limitations and bias: (1) the 1-D no-lateral-inflow idealization overstates attenuation — real tributaries between the dams add flow that the pools must also store, so the true peak would be higher than 2,500 m3/s; the 50%-of-cap result should be read as a lower bound on peak attenuation, not a guarantee; (2) the cost curve (k, alpha) and the safe cap (5,000 m3/s) are the two most consequential assumptions — a lower safe cap or a flatter cost curve would shift the recommended N; the objective's flatness over N = 14-18 means the recommendation is robust to moderate perturbations; (3) the tier thresholds (70% pre-draw trigger, 500 m3/s environmental flow, 30-day low-flow window) are engineering-judgment values, not derived from data, and should be tuned against a longer observed record before implementation; (4) no forecasting is used, so the pre-draw (Tier 1) reacts to measured rising inflow rather than an anticipated crest, which is conservative but may pre-draw unnecessarily in false starts; (5) the 60-year horizon and 5% discount are conventional but arbitrary; (6) social costs (displacement of communities along the 400 km reach, ecosystem change from 16 new impoundments) are not quantified in the cost and enter only through the Requirement-1 matrix.
