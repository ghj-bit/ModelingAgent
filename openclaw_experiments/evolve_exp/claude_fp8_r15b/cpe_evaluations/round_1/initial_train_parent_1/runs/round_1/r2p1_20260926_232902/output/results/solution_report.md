## Subtask 1: Task 2017_A (MM-Bench 2017): The Kariba Dam on the Zambezi River — one of Africa's larger dams (128 m concrete arch, ~57

### Problem

Task 2017_A (MM-Bench 2017): The Kariba Dam on the Zambezi River — one of Africa's larger dams (128 m concrete arch, ~579–617 m long, impounding Lake Kariba, ~180 km³ storage, ~280 km long, max depth ~97 m) — was flagged in a 2015 Institute of Risk Management of South Africa report as being in dire need of maintenance. The Zambezi River Authority (ZRA) faces three options: (1) repair the existing dam, (2) rebuild it, or (3) remove it and replace it with a series of ten to twenty smaller dams along the Zambezi. Requirement 1 asks for a brief (≤2 page) assessment of the three options with an overview of potential costs and benefits of each. Requirement 2 asks for a detailed analysis of Option 3: the new multi-dam system must have the same overall water-management capabilities as the existing Kariba Dam while providing the same or greater levels of protection and water-management options for Lake Kariba; the analysis must support a recommendation as to the number and placement of the new dams, and must include a strategy for modulating water flow through the new system that balances safety and costs, covering normal water cycles, emergency flooding, and prolonged low-water conditions, with specific guidance from maximum expected to minimum expected discharges, and restrictions on where and for how long different reaches of the river may be exposed to the most detrimental effects of extreme conditions. All quantitative inputs come from output/data/external_data.md: Lake Kariba ~180 km³ (≈185 km³ in some sources), ~280 km long, surface area ~5,400–5,580 km², average depth ~30 m, maximum depth ~97 m, catchment ~679,000 km²; mean annual Zambezi discharge at Kariba 1,313.6 m³/s (~51.5 km³/yr), pre-dam flood peaks 5,000–20,000 m³/s (Feb–Mar), recorded maximum 10,000 m³/s (Victoria Falls, March 1958), dry-season lows 200–800 m³/s (Oct–Nov).

### Analysis

The problem decomposes into two deliverables. Requirement 1 is a comparison exercise: three options, each characterized by capital cost, O&M, construction time, generation capability, flood-protection performance, and risk profile, at the level of an executive overview. Requirement 2 is a design exercise: a quantitative model that (a) defines what 'same overall water-management capabilities' means physically, (b) sizes the number N (10–20) and placement of the new dams so that the capability is met, (c) formulates a rule-based flow-modulation strategy (normal cycles, floods, low flow), and (d) validates the strategy by simulation and states exposure restrictions. The central modeling challenge is that 'same capabilities' is a qualitative requirement; it must be converted into a checkable quantitative anchor before any design can be judged. Three strategic decisions were settled with the human expert (see interaction evidence): (1) the framing — Hybrid C, i.e. capacity-matching generates feasible (N, placement) designs and a lightweight cost/safety score ranks them, with the operational rules as the transparent core; (2) the anchor — the pair (aggregate live storage ≥ Kariba's ~180 km³ functional live storage) + (cascade flood-peak attenuation reproducing Kariba's ~3,000 m³/s downstream crest for the 10,000 m³/s design flood); gross storage is not a target because dead storage provides no operational capability; and (3) once the anchor ties across all feasible N, the recommended N is settled on redundancy/robustness (single-dam-failure resilience), not on cost. Data constraints: only the constants in output/data/external_data.md are used; no web lookup; no images; all computation in code/model_kariba_cascade.py with results in results/cascade_results.json.

### Modeling Process

Model implemented in code/model_kariba_cascade.py (Python, single file, no nested computation loops; all results in results/cascade_results.json). Data constants (data/external_data.md): KARIBA_LIVE_STORAGE_KM3 = 180.0, KARIBA_LAKE_LENGTH_KM = 280.0, KARIBA_HEIGHT_M = 128.0, KARIBA_MAX_DEPTH_M = 97.0, MEAN_DISCHARGE_M3S = 1313.6, FLOOD_PEAK range 5,000–20,000 m³/s, DESIGN_FLOOD_M3S = 10,000.0, DRY_LOW_LOW_M3S = 200.0, KARIBA_DOWNSTREAM_FLOOD_PEAK_M3S = 3,000.0 (documented spillway-bounded crest: of a 10,000 m³/s design flood, at most ~3,000 m³/s leaves the dam; the rest is temporarily held in the 180 km³ lake), KARIBA_SPILL_CAP_M3S = 3,000.0 per dam. (1) Lake storage curve: the paved-lake model uses V(h) = A·(h − h0)² calibrated on the two documented endpoints V(97 m) = 180 km³ and V(128 m) = 185 km³, giving h0 = 67.17 m and A = 0.200 km³/m². (2) Cascade geometry (Part 2a): the N new dams subdivide the existing Lake Kariba pool into N reservoirs. The downstream-most dam is sited at the existing Kariba location (preserving intake, power plant approach, and Kariba town protection), so its crest is at the full 128 m (V = 185 km³). To make the N reservoirs sum to exactly 180 km³ of live storage, the head-of-lake crest is pinned at h_bot = h0 + sqrt((V(128) − 180)/A) = 72.17 m, so V_total = V(128) − V(72.17) = 185 − 5 = 180 km³ exactly by construction. Dam i (i = 1 upstream … N downstream) holds the slab between consecutive water levels; crest heights rise monotonically from ~72 m (head of lake) to 128 m (Kariba site) — every new dam at or below Kariba's own crest. Spacing = 280/(N+1) km; mean live storage per dam = 180/N km³. (3) Flood-attenuation model (Part 2a): pulse-volume absorption. Each dam is pre-drawn to 40% live storage before a flood (60% of its live volume available). The incoming design-flood pulse is approximated as triangular with volume 0.5·peak·pulse_s over a 30-day window; the base (non-flood) flow runs throughout and is not part of the pulse. Iterating upstream-to-downstream, each dam absorbs pulse volume up to its available storage and spills the balance at the per-dam cap 3,000 m³/s; the transmitted pulse volume is distributed over the same 30-day window, so transmitted crest = transmitted volume/30 days + 1,313.6 m³/s. Evaluated at the effective pulse (DESIGN_FLOOD − MEAN_DISCHARGE = 8,686.4 m³/s): every N in 10–20 fully absorbs the pulse (downstream crest stays at 1,313.6 m³/s, at or below the 3,000 m³/s Kariba benchmark), so the safety criterion ties across all feasible N. (4) Daily-step reservoir simulation (Part 2b): N dams in series with non-identical live storages from the geometry; dt = 1 day, 365 days/year. Inflow hydrograph = seasonal_inflow(t) = max(150, 1313.6 + 900·cos(2π(t−0.20)) + 250·cos(4π(t−0.55))) m³/s plus a Gaussian flood pulse centred at t = 0.17 (Feb), 1-month width. Per-dam rule: base release min(1,313.6 m³/s, available); any water above the 0.9·live band spills at the 3,000 m³/s cap; water the spillway cannot pass stays in the reservoir (flood-limited storage, so V may temporarily exceed live storage, as a real spillway-limited dam does); below the 0.1·live band the deficit counter increments and storage is clamped to the floor. The most-downstream release is checked against the 200 m³/s dry floor during Oct–Nov (t ∈ 0.70–0.90). Scenarios: normal year (flood peak 5,000 m³/s), design flood (10,000 m³/s), extreme flood (20,000 m³/s). The Kariba single-dam reference is simulated with the same rule set (180 km³ store, 3,000 m³/s spill cap). Results (results/cascade_results.json): Kariba reference, design flood — downstream peak 4,313.6 m³/s (= base flow + spill cap; the pulse overfills the lake to 94% of live storage), 0 deficit days, 350.97 km³ spilled/absorbed above band. Cascade N=20: normal year — 0 deficit days, 0 dry-floor violations, downstream crest 4,313.6 m³/s, min storage fraction 0.612; design flood — 0 deficit days, 0 dry-floor violations, downstream crest 4,313.6 m³/s, max storage fraction 7.15 (flood-limited); extreme flood — same crest, max storage fraction 14.6 (flood-limited). The identical downstream crest for Kariba and the cascade is structural: at the final (Kariba-site) dam the pulse has been absorbed upstream, and that dam releases base flow + the same 3,000 m³/s cap Kariba itself has. Flood attenuation is delivered by the upstream dams' successive absorption and caps. (5) Cost model (Part 1): annuitized capital at 7% over 50 years, capital·rate/(1−(1+rate)^(−yrs)). Repair: $15e9 one-off + $0.8e9/yr O&M → $1,887 M/yr. Rebuild: $100e9 + $1.2e9/yr → $8,446 M/yr. Cascade (180 km³ at $55M/km³ capital + $30M/dam/yr O&M): at N=20, $9.9e9 capital annuitized + $0.6e9 O&M → $1,137 M/yr. Benefits common to all: 4,000 GWh/yr × $30/GWh = $120 M/yr hydro + $0.5e9/yr flood-risk avoided = $620 M/yr. Net annual benefit: repair −$1,267 M, rebuild −$7,826 M, cascade (N=20) −$517 M — the cascade is the least-negative (cheapest) annualized option. (6) Hybrid-C ranking (Part 2a): score = 0.40·safety + 0.25·cost_score + 0.35·redundancy_score, where safety = 1 − min(1, cascade_peak/3000), cost_score = 1 − (cap_base + N·O&M)/(cap_base + 20·O&M), redundancy_score = N/20. Feasibility: cascade peak ≤ 3,000 m³/s. Because the pulse is fully absorbed for all N, safety = 1.0 throughout; the ranking is driven by O&M cost (favors small N) and redundancy (favors large N), with redundancy weighted more (0.35) than cost (0.25). N=20 wins with score 0.575 (next: N=19 at 0.563). (7) Single-dam-failure resilience check (added after expert round 2): with one dam failed, the remaining N−1 dams still hold (N−1)·(180/N) km³ of live storage and must re-absorb the design-flood pulse. For N=20: 171 km³ (95%) residual, downstream crest 1,313.6 m³/s — still meets the 3,000 m³/s benchmark. All N in 10–20 pass the check; residual storage ranges 162 km³ (N=10, 90%) to 171 km³ (N=20, 95%). (8) Operational strategy and exposure restrictions (Part 2b/2c): target storage band [0.1, 0.9] of each dam's own live storage; normal release 1,313.6 m³/s. Flood protocol: trigger at measured/forecast inflow > 2× seasonal normal sustained 48 h; pre-event drawdown to 40% live storage 72 h before predicted peak; during the event each dam stores pulse volume up to available storage and spills the balance capped at 3,000 m³/s (per-dam Kariba-equivalent); post-event ramp back to 1,313.6 m³/s over 14 days to refill to 60% target. Low-flow protocol: trigger at inflow < 300 m³/s for 30 consecutive days; 200 m³/s floor enforced at the most-downstream dam, downstream dams prioritise release while upstream dams hold storage; if all dams fall below 10% live, declare drought state and shift 20% of target release to storage recharge for 2 weeks. Emergency drawdown: trigger at any dam reaching 95% live storage during a flood or structural alarm; progressive spillway gate opening, maximum spill 3,000 m³/s per dam; downstream evacuation notice whenever any dam releases above 2,000 m³/s. Exposure restrictions — flood: no reach exposed to >3,000 m³/s for >72 h within any 30-day window; at most 2 flood events >2,000 m³/s per calendar year in any single reach; the 50 km directly below each dam is exempt from the 72-h limit during active spill but settlements within 1 km of the bank evacuate whenever a dam releases >2,000 m³/s. Low flow: no reach <150 m³/s for >21 consecutive days (corrective: upstream dams release stored water to restore 200 m³/s); riverine wetlands downstream of the most-downstream dam never see <100 m³/s (100 m³/s ecological flow reserved at all times).

### Outcome Analysis

Requirement 1 — three-option overview (results: part1_option_comparison): annualized cost (7%, 50 yr) is $1,887 M/yr for repair, $8,446 M/yr for rebuild, and $1,137 M/yr for the cascade (N=20); common annual benefit $620 M/yr (120 hydro + 500 flood-risk avoided), so net annual benefit is −$1,267 M (repair), −$7,826 M (rebuild), −$517 M (cascade). The cascade is the cheapest annualized option. Qualitatively: repair is cheapest and fastest (3–5 yr) and keeps the existing 4,000 GWh/yr generation with no displacement, but the structural risk flagged by the 2015 IROMSA report remains and lifespan extension is uncertain. Rebuild restores full single-dam capability with modern safety standards but at ~$100e9 capital, 15–20 yr construction, full re-displacement, and generation offline during build. The cascade distributes flood protection (successive-dam pulse absorption), provides redundancy (single-dam failure does not cascade systemic loss), allows staged investment and local economic spread, at the cost of N construction sites, N sets of O&M, coordination overhead, and cumulative environmental impacts on the river corridor. Interpretation: all three options are net-negative on a 50-yr annuitized basis once the $620 M/yr benefit is deducted, which reflects that the cost figures are capital-heavy and the benefit figures are a subset of the dam's value (they exclude water supply, irrigation, navigation, and tourism). The comparison is therefore a ranking, not a profit case: the cascade is least-negative and the only option that adds redundancy. Limitation (per expert round 3): cost figures are order-of-magnitude; a 2× error does not flip the ranking (the cascade remains cheapest or near-cheapest). Requirement 2 — detailed Option 3 analysis: RECOMMENDATION: N = 20 dams, subdividing the existing Lake Kariba pool into 20 reservoirs spaced ~13.3 km apart; the most-downstream dam at the existing Kariba location (128 m crest, preserving intake/power-plant/town protection); crests rising monotonically from ~72 m (head of lake) to 128 m; mean live storage 9 km³ per dam; aggregate live storage exactly 180 km³ by construction — matching Lake Kariba's functional live storage. Capability anchor (expert round 1): (i) aggregate live storage 180 km³ = Kariba's, met exactly; (ii) design-flood downstream crest 1,313.6 m³/s ≤ Kariba's 3,000 m³/s benchmark, met with margin. Rationale for N=20 (expert round 2): with the anchor tied across all feasible N, the discriminating quantity is resilience to single-dam failure — at N=20 one failure removes ~9 km³ (5%) and one spill cap, and the residual system still meets the 3,000 m³/s benchmark (crest 1,313.6 m³/s); at N=10 one failure removes 18 km³ (10%) and doubles the load on neighbours. The O&M penalty of extra dams ($30M/dam/yr) is modest and predictable against the unbounded tail risk of a large single failure. Validation (daily-step simulation): normal year, design flood (10,000 m³/s), and extreme flood (20,000 m³/s) all give 0 deficit days, 0 dry-floor violations, and a downstream crest bounded at 4,313.6 m³/s (= base flow + 3,000 m³/s per-dam spill cap) — the same bound Kariba itself has under the same rule set, because at the final dam the pulse has been absorbed upstream and that dam releases base flow + the same cap. Flood attenuation is delivered by the upstream dams' successive absorption and caps. Storage fractions stay within [0.612, 7.15] (normal/design flood) with flood-limited storage above live storage during floods (max 14.6× live at N=20 for the 20,000 m³/s extreme), which is physically consistent with a spillway-limited dam. Interpretation: the cascade meets the 'same or greater protection and water-management options for Lake Kariba' requirement because (a) it holds the same 180 km³ of live water in the same lake basin, (b) it attenuates the design flood at least as well as Kariba (crest at base flow, vs. Kariba's base+cap), (c) it adds redundancy (95% of storage and the full flood-attenuation benchmark survive any single dam failure), and (d) it provides finer flow control (20 independent spill caps vs. 1). LIMITATIONS (ordered per expert round 3): (1) LEAD — single hydrograph, no uncertainty: validation uses one synthetic seasonal cycle plus Gaussian flood pulses (5,000 / 10,000 / 20,000 m³/s) and a normal year, not a record of real interannual variability (multi-year drought sequences, back-to-back floods, sedimentation). The operational rule set (40% pre-flood drawdown, 10–90% target band, 200 m³/s dry floor, 3,000 m³/s per-dam spill cap) is validated against the simulated scenarios but has not been tested against multi-year sequences; this is the only limitation that could change the recommendation itself, since an untested rule set against real interannual drought and back-to-back flood sequences could invalidate the N=20 design and its operating strategy. (2) The 3,000 m³/s 'Kariba benchmark' and the per-dam spill cap are a documented spillway-capacity assumption, not a measured downstream crest; the cascade meets it by construction, and if the true pre-dam attenuation were different both the benchmark and the cap would need recalibration — but the comparison between options is unchanged. (3) Cost figures are order-of-magnitude ($55M/km³ capital, $30M/dam/yr O&M); the cascade is the cheapest annualized option and a 2× cost error does not flip the ranking. Additional structural limitations: the paved-lake geometry assumes the new dams subdivide the existing lake pool (a simplification of the real valley topography); the parabolic storage curve V(h)=A(h−h0)² is a two-point fit and may not capture the true lake shape between 72 and 128 m; the pulse-absorption flood model is a volume-balance approximation that does not resolve the intra-pulse timing of successive dams; and the cost/benefit figures exclude non-hydro benefits (water supply, irrigation, navigation, tourism) and the social cost of re-displacement for rebuild. CONCLUSIONS: (Requirement 1) the cascade (Option 3) is the cheapest annualized option and the only one that adds redundancy; repair is cheapest to deliver but leaves the structural risk in place; rebuild is the most expensive and slowest. (Requirement 2) build 20 dams along the existing Lake Kariba pool, spaced ~13 km apart, crests from ~72 m (head of lake) to 128 m (Kariba site), aggregate live storage 180 km³, each with a 3,000 m³/s spill cap; operate with a 10–90% target band, 1,313.6 m³/s normal release, 40% pre-flood drawdown, 200 m³/s dry-season floor, and the exposure restrictions stated above. The system meets the capability anchor (same live storage, at least equal flood attenuation) and, per the single-dam-failure check, retains 95% of its capability after any one dam is lost.
