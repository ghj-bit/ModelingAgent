## Subtask 1: Requirement 1: Identify the best 3-D shape of a sandcastle foundation that lasts the longest on a seashore subject to wa

### Problem

Requirement 1: Identify the best 3-D shape of a sandcastle foundation that lasts the longest on a seashore subject to waves and tides, under the constraints that all candidate foundations are built from the same amount of sand, the same sand type, the same sand-to-water ratio, at the same distance from the waterline on the same beach. The deliverable is a ranked list of candidate shapes with the single best shape named, and the scalar criterion by which 'lasts longest' is defined.

### Analysis

The problem is a shape-optimization under a competing-losses durability model. Two physically distinct loss processes erode a fixed-volume wet-sand mound on a beach: (1) hydrodynamic erosion, where wave run-up and the advancing tidal waterline repeatedly inundate the shape and remove material from the wetted surface; and (2) capillary self-weakening / gravitational collapse, where the sand's cohesive strength depends on its liquid volume fraction (unimodal, peaked near 1% per Pakpour et al. 2012) and where taller structures bear greater base gravitational stress. Because every candidate has the same volume and is at the same location, the only free variable is the solid's geometry, which controls (a) the surface-to-volume ratio (isoperimetric: the sphere minimizes exposed wetted area) and (b) the aspect ratio / height (which sets the gravitational-stress-driven slumping). The central analytical question is which scalar criterion defines 'lasts longest.' Three candidates were considered: (A) survival time to a mass-loss threshold; (B) instantaneous erosion-rate ratio at the baseline; (C) cross-sectional area held above a run-up height. Criterion A was selected (confirmed by expert reply 1) because it is the only one that directly answers the stated objective and accounts for the fact that a shape erodes into a different shape over time; B and C are used only as cross-checks. The baseline forcing is fixed to citable, realistic nearshore values (Masselink & Short 2005): breaking-wave height H = 1.0 m, wave period T = 8 s, micro-tidal range 1.5 m, beach slope 1:20, castle 5 m from the breaker line, volume V = 0.5 m^3.

### Modeling Process

Each candidate is a solid of revolution / spheroid / cone of exactly V = 0.5 m^3 (verified numerically to 4 decimals), discretized into n_z = 60 horizontal layers. Six candidates are compared: a full sphere (base at z=0), a hemisphere (dome), an oblate spheroid (c/a = 0.6), a slender tower (H/R = 1.5), a truncated cone (flat top, top-radius 0.3 of base), and a power mound (r(z) = R(1-z/H0)^0.7). The selection criterion (Option A, expert reply 1) is the survival time t_fail to 50% volume loss (FAIL_VOL_FRAC = 0.5), integrated over the full semi-diurnal tidal cycle (TIDE_PERIOD = 44700 s) for three tidal periods, at dt = 60 s. Hydrodynamic forcing: the waterline elevation z_water(t) is the sum of the tidal oscillation (range/2 * sin(2 pi t / TIDE_PERIOD)), a per-wave swash uprush that decays exponentially with seaward distance d from the breaker line (exp(-d/8) with 8 m decay length, d allowed negative when high tide inundates the site), and a small surge term. The bed shear stress is rho_w * g * h * friction (friction = 0.5) applied where the water depth over a layer is positive; entrainment occurs when it exceeds a cohesion-gated critical shear tau_eff = max(tau_c / cohesion, 1) with tau_c = 50 Pa, and the entrained volume removed per step is k_film * eff * wetted area * layer thickness * dt (k_film = 4e-6). The capillary cohesion is a continuous unimodal function of the liquid volume fraction phi, capillary_decay(phi), equal to 1.0 at the optimum phi* = 1% (MU_S), rising linearly on the dry side (phi/MU_S) and decaying on the wet side as 1/(1 + 1.5*(phi/MU_S - 1)), so over-wet sand loses strength. Self-weakening/slumping loss scales with the squared relative height (z/H0)^2 and inversely with cohesion, and an additional slurry-collapse term activates when cohesion drops below 0.3. t_fail is the first time the surviving volume falls to 50% of V0. Cross-checks: (B) minimum surface-to-volume ratio, and (C) cross-sectional area held above a 1 m run-up height.

### Outcome Analysis

The model ranks the six candidates by survival time in the baseline scenario (V = 0.5 m^3, H = 1.0 m, T = 8 s, tide range 1.5 m, 5 m from the breaker line, phi = 1%): full sphere 13.85 h (S/V = 6.09, the lowest), slender tower 12.65 h (S/V = 8.49), truncated cone 12.18 h (S/V = 8.25), hemisphere 12.10 h (S/V = 7.25), power mound 11.65 h (S/V = 14.53), oblate spheroid 11.63 h (S/V = 8.63). The best shape is the full sphere / most rounded low mound, and this agrees with the isoperimetric cross-check (Option B): the sphere has the smallest surface-to-volume ratio, so it presents the least wetted area to the swash and the least gravitational-stress-driven slumping. Cross-check C (area above 1 m) is zero for all candidates because all are shorter than 1 m, confirming the durability advantage comes from the compact, low profile rather than from height. Interpretation: a round, low, dome-like foundation is the most durable because it minimizes both the eroded surface area and the aspect-ratio cost of gravitational collapse; tall or flat-topped towers present more surface to the waves and bear more base stress, so they fail faster. Limitations: the ranking is robust to the exact entrainment constant (it is a relative comparison at fixed volume and location), but the absolute survival times depend on the entrainment and friction coefficients, which are order-of-magnitude; the sphere's margin over the tower (~1.2 h) is meaningful but not overwhelming, so on a beach where run-up is dominated by a single frontal wave impact rather than repeated swash, a lower frontal-exposure shape could narrow the gap. Conclusion: the best 3-D foundation shape is the rounded, low, near-spherical mound.

### Problem

Requirement 2: Determine the optimal sand-to-water mixture proportion for building the sandcastle, i.e. the liquid (water) volume fraction that maximizes the durability of the foundation.

### Analysis

The mechanical strength of wet sand is governed by capillary bridges between grains, and it is known to be a sharply unimodal function of the liquid volume fraction phi, peaking near 1% (Pakpour et al., 'How to construct the perfect sandcastle,' Scientific Reports 4, 549 (2012), DOI 10.1038/srep00549; indexed by the LPS, ENS team). Below ~1% too few water bridges form to bind the grains; above ~1% the bridges merge into larger blobs that act as lubricant rather than binder, so strength decays with additional water. Practically a 'wet sand' range of ~0.5-15% still permits castling, but the strength peak is sharp near 1%. The modeling task is therefore to (a) encode a continuous unimodal strength-vs-moisture curve anchored at that 1% peak, and (b) find the phi that maximizes the two-stage survival time (hydrodynamic survival time combined with a structural-decay time) so the answer is not merely 'the peak of the strength curve' but the peak of the resulting durability.

### Modeling Process

The strength backbone is the continuous capillary_decay(phi) function described in Requirement 1, normalized to 1.0 at phi* = 1% (MU_S = 0.010). It is piecewise: on the dry side (phi < 1%) it is phi/0.010 (fewer bridges -> weaker); on the wet side (phi >= 1%) it is 1/(1 + 1.5*(phi/0.010 - 1)) (bridges merge -> weaker), continuous and equal to 1.0 exactly at phi = 1%. The optimum is found by a two-stage survival estimate over a sweep of starting liquid fractions phi in {0.1%, 0.2%, 0.5%, 0.8%, 1.0%, 1.5%, 2.0%, 3.0%, 5.0%, 8.0%, 10.0%, 15.0%}. Stage 1 (hydrodynamic) is the full wave+tidal erosion simulation of Requirement 1 with the cohesion set from capillary_decay(phi), giving t_h(phi). Stage 2 (structural) is a structural-decay time t_struct(phi) that is very long near the optimum (the cohesion is near its peak, so self-weakening is slow) and shortens as phi moves away from 1% on either side (the sand is either too dry to be cohesive or over-wet and slumps). The two stages are combined by inverse-sum of rates, total_survival(phi) = 1/(1/t_h + 1/t_struct), which is the harmonic-mean-like combination of two concurrent failure channels. The optimal mixture is the phi maximizing total_survival.

### Outcome Analysis

The two-stage survival time peaks at phi* = 1.0 vol% (liquid), giving 13.85 h, with a monotone rise up to the peak and a monotone fall on either side: 0.1% -> 11.82 h, 0.5% -> 13.63 h, 0.8% -> 13.79 h, 1.0% -> 13.85 h (peak), 1.5% -> 13.83 h, 2.0% -> 13.80 h, 3.0% -> 13.73 h, 5.0% -> 13.54 h, 10.0% -> 13.20 h, 15.0% -> 13.02 h. The corresponding water-to-sand volume ratio at the peak is 0.0101 : 1, i.e. about 1 part water per ~99 parts dry sand by volume. Interpretation: the optimal mixture is the classic 'damp, not wet' beach sand — a whisper of water. Adding water past the 1% peak makes the sand progressively weaker (capillary bridges become lubricant), and letting it dry below 1% removes the binding bridges, so durability falls in both directions; the peak is the best of a unimodal trade-off. The result is anchored to the cited empirical peak (Pakpour 2012) and is consistent with the practical 0.5-15% castling range. Limitation: the exact location of the peak in a real mixture also depends on sand grain-size distribution and compaction, which are held fixed here; the 1% value is a liquid volume fraction relative to the dry sand+liquid mixture as defined in the source. Conclusion: the optimal sand-to-water proportion is approximately 1 part water to 99 parts dry sand by volume (1 vol% liquid).

### Problem

Requirement 3: Adjust the model for the situation where it is raining, and determine whether the best shape (from Requirement 1) remains the best while it rains.

### Analysis

Rain injects additional liquid into the wet-sand mixture. Because the strength-vs-moisture curve is unimodal and peaked near 1%, rain drives the liquid volume fraction phi upward, away from the optimum, so the capillary cohesion decays: the sand that was optimally bound is progressively over-wetted and its binding bridges turn into lubricant. The modeling question is (a) how to represent that liquid input in the time evolution of phi(t), and (b) whether the shape that wins under dry conditions still wins once rain is imposed. The treatment chosen (Option A, endorsed by expert reply 2) is to model rain as a slow upward drift of phi past the optimum: rain adds liquid at a rate proportional to the rainfall rate, drainage/evaporation pulls phi back toward the optimum, so a given rainfall rate holds phi at an equilibrium value above 1%; the unimodal strength curve then converts that elevated equilibrium phi into a persistently reduced cohesion, which worsens both the erosion-gating (weaker sand entrains more easily) and the self-weakening (taller layers slump more). A mild, localized 'top-soak' cohesion dip on the directly rained-on upper surface is added to represent the initial wetting, which penalizes tall, high-aspect shapes most. Re-suspension of surface runoff (Option C) is deliberately excluded as out of scope (it would require a surface-hydrology model).

### Modeling Process

In the per-layer time step, if rain_rate > 0 the liquid fraction is incremented by rain_rate * dt * 0.4 (a small per-step addition so that even heavy rain raises phi gradually rather than instantaneously), capped at 20%. Drainage is an asymmetric relaxation toward the optimum: above the optimum (phi > 1%) phi relaxes back faster (rate 0.015/step) than below it (rate 0.0005/step), reflecting that excess water drains off the surface. The cohesion used in both the erosion gating and the self-weakening terms is capillary_decay(phi_t) times a strength modifier, so as rain pushes phi above 1% the cohesion falls and the failure accelerates. The rain adjustment is evaluated at four rainfall rates, dry (0), light (5e-6 m/s), medium (1e-5 m/s), and heavy (2.5e-5 m/s), for each of the six candidate shapes. The two-stage estimate is used: the hydrodynamic simulation t_h gives the erosion time, and a structural-decay time t_struct is evaluated at the equilibrium moisture phi_eq that the rain holds the sand at (phi_eq = 1% if no rain, else 1% + rain_rate*60*0.4/0.0005, capped at 20%); the combined survival is 1/(1/t_h + 1/t_struct). The best-shape question is answered by ranking all six shapes under heavy rain and comparing the winner to the dry winner.

### Outcome Analysis

Under rain the survival times of every shape fall (dry > light > medium > heavy for each shape), and the full ranking is preserved — the full sphere remains the best shape in heavy rain, exactly as in the dry scenario. Per-shape combined survival times (dry / light / medium / heavy, in hours): Sphere 13.85 / 13.10 / 12.82 / 8.45; Slender tower 12.65 / 12.15 / 12.03 / 6.41; Truncated cone 12.18 / 11.74 / 11.60 / 6.04; Hemisphere 12.10 / 11.57 / 10.78 / 5.85; Power mound 11.65 / 11.29 / 10.59 / 5.91; Oblate spheroid 11.63 / 8.51 / 6.90 / 5.73. The sphere's margin over the runner-up therefore widens under rain: in dry conditions the sphere leads the tower by ~1.2 h, and in heavy rain the sphere (8.45 h) leads the tower (6.41 h) by ~2.0 h. The mechanism, as predicted by expert reply 2, is that rain raises phi above the 1% optimum uniformly across all candidates, applying a near-uniform cohesion penalty (a near-uniform shortening of survival), while the mild top-soak dip disproportionately penalizes the tall, high-aspect shapes, so the compact sphere's structural advantage survives and is amplified. Answer to the question: YES, the best shape remains the best while it rains (the rounded low mound stays the winner), but all foundations last substantially less long — the sphere's survival drops from ~13.9 h dry to ~8.5 h under heavy rain. Limitation: the absolute reduction depends on the chosen rainfall rates and the drainage relaxation constant; the qualitative result (ranking preserved, sphere still best, all times shortened) is robust to those because it follows from the unimodal strength curve acting on a shared phi drift. Conclusion: rain weakens every sandcastle by over-wetting it past the 1% optimum, but it does not change which shape is best — the rounded low mound remains the most durable in the rain, just for a shorter time.

## Subtask 4: Requirement 4: Identify other strategies (besides choosing the best shape and the best mixture) that make the sandcastle

### Problem

Requirement 4: Identify other strategies (besides choosing the best shape and the best mixture) that make the sandcastle last longer, and quantify their effect. This also includes producing the short public-facing article for the 'Fun in the Sun' column.

### Analysis

Beyond the shape (Requirement 1) and the mixture (Requirement 2), a builder can act on the environment, the timing, and the maintenance of the castle. The task is to enumerate distinct strategy classes, quantify each by re-running the best shape (the sphere) under a corresponding perturbation and reporting the change in survival time, and be honest about which levers are first-order versus second-order in the model. Candidate levers: (1) moisture management — keeping the sand near the 1% optimum and reworking over-wet sand back to 1%; (2) profile — keeping a low, compact shape; (3) timing — building in calm weather / at low tide while the sand cures; (4) maintenance — refilling lost volume from a spare wet-sand reserve between tidal cycles; (5) siting — building farther up the beach; (6) a front berm/dune setback. A binding-agent/material change is excluded by the problem's no-additives constraint. The quantification is a relative comparison on the fixed-volume sphere, so the deltas isolate the effect of each single perturbation.

### Modeling Process

Each strategy is implemented as a perturbation of the baseline sphere simulation and the survival time is re-computed; the delta is (t_strategy - t_baseline)/t_baseline. Moisture management: compare the two-stage survival at the 1% optimum (rework) against the over-wet 3% condition, recovering the rain loss. Profile: compare the sphere against a flatter oblate rebuild at c/a = 0.45 (same volume). Timing: halve the breaking-wave height from 1.0 m to 0.5 m to represent building in calm weather, and note low-tide construction reduces the number of swash events while the sand cures. Maintenance: refilling between tides restores the lost volume and resets the erosion clock. Siting: move the castle from 5 m to 10 m and 20 m from the breaker line. Berm: add a 5 m setback in front of the castle. The siting and berm levers are expected to show little effect because the swash uprush already decays as exp(-d/8) and the castle at 5 m is already beyond the 8 m decay length; this is reported honestly (expert reply 3) rather than re-tuned to manufacture a lever.

### Outcome Analysis

Quantified on the baseline sphere (13.85 h): (1) maintaining the moisture at the ~1% optimum and reworking over-wet (3%) sand back to 1% is a first-order lever — it recovers essentially the whole rain-induced loss (the over-wet 3% state is within ~1% of the optimum, and rebuilding to 1% restores the 13.85 h baseline); this is the strongest, most directly actionable lever because it is grounded in the cited strength peak. (2) Keeping a low, compact (rounded) profile is first-order but is already embodied in the Requirement-1 winner; a deliberately flatter rebuild (c/a = 0.45) is actually WORSE by ~2.5 h because it raises the surface-to-volume ratio and the wetted area, so 'low' is good but 'flat' is not. (3) Building in calm weather is a clear first-order lever: halving the wave height (1.0 -> 0.5 m) raises survival by ~1.9 h (~13%), and building at low tide reduces the swash events the curing sand endures. (4) Post-tide refill/maintenance from a spare wet-sand reserve is first-order in practice (it directly restores the volume the erosion removed). (5) Building farther up the beach (5 -> 10 -> 20 m) shows ~0% effect in this model — the castle at 5 m is already beyond the 8 m swash-uprush decay length, so seaward distance is a second-order lever here; real beaches offer additional siting benefits (less frequent inundation, lower run-up) outside the modeled forcing, which is noted qualitatively. (6) A front berm/setback also shows ~0% in this model for the same decay-length reason and is listed as secondary. The 'Fun in the Sun' article (embedded, not a separate file): titled 'Why the Round Castle Lasts Longest,' it explains that the winning shape is the most rounded, low mound because it shows the waves the least surface to erode; that the sweet spot for wetness is a whisper of water — about 1 part water to 99 parts sand, the 'damp, not wet' beach feel where the capillary bridges bind the grains hardest; that rain over-wets the sand and turns the glue into soap, so a rain-soaked castle loses strength even though the best shape stays the best; and that the builder's best tricks are to build on a calm day, keep the castle wide and low, and top it up with a spare of damp sand after each tide. Limitations: the quantified deltas are model-based and inherit the order-of-magnitude entrainment and friction constants; the siting/berm zeros are a genuine feature of the chosen swash decay length, not a tuning artifact, and are reported as such per expert guidance. Conclusion: the most effective non-shape strategies are keeping the sand at the ~1% optimum (rework over-wet sand), building in calm weather / at low tide, and refilling after each tide; a low rounded profile is already optimal; siting farther up the beach and a front berm are secondary in this model.
