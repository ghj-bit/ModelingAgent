## Subtask 1: Sub-problem 1: Develop a model to estimate the maximum levels of single-use or disposable plastic product waste that can

### Problem

Sub-problem 1: Develop a model to estimate the maximum levels of single-use or disposable plastic product waste that can safely be mitigated without further environmental damage, considering the source of the waste, the extent of the current problem, and the availability of resources to process it.

### Analysis

Adopted a two-anchor, regional stock-and-flow framework. The 'safe' bound is defined by the environmental sink (ocean/marine), not by infrastructure (per the hybrid strategy confirmed in expert Round 1, Option C): the maximum safely-mitigable waste is the volume that can be collected and processed so that NET NEW ocean leakage stays at or below the current ocean-input sink and the existing marine plastic stock is drawn down, rather than the volume that plants can nominally process. Two empirical anchors calibrate the model: (a) OECD Global Plastics Outlook 2022: 2019 global plastic waste = 353 Mt, only ~9% (33 Mt) recycled, with ~40-60% of the stream single-use/disposable (parameterized at 50%, giving 176.5 Mt of single-use/disposable waste); (b) Jambeck et al. 2015: 4.8-12.7 Mt/yr of plastic entered the ocean in 2010 (midpoint ~8 Mt) from 275 Mt waste in 192 coastal countries, i.e. a mismanaged-share (leakage coefficient) of 0.5-4.6%. The model carries seven income-differentiated regions (high, mixed, lower-middle, low), each with population, per-capita single-use waste, and a baseline mismanaged share. Cross-check: the model's 2019 baseline ocean leakage is 7.66 Mt/yr, which falls inside the Jambeck 4.8-12.7 Mt band, validating the regional leakage structure. The 9% recycling anchor (33 Mt) sets the current processing-resource capacity that is later tested against the sink.

### Modeling Process

Variables: for region i, W_i(t) = single-use/disposable waste (Mt), P_i = population (billions), w_i(t) = per-capita single-use waste (kg), m_i(t) = mismanaged (leakage) fraction. Calibrated so sum_i W_i(2019) = 0.50 x 353 Mt = 176.5 Mt (scaling per-capita w_i to meet the anchor). Ocean input: O(t) = sum_i W_i(t) * m_i(t) (Mt/yr). Environmental-sink (safe-mitigation) constraint: O(t) <= O* where O* = 8 Mt/yr (Jambeck 2010 midpoint, the current-ocean-input sink). The safe-mitigation capacity bound in mismanaged-fraction terms is m* = O* / W_global. With W_global(2019) = 176.5 Mt, m* = 8 / 176.5 = 0.0453 (4.53%). Interpretation: at the current single-use volume, the environment can safely absorb at most ~8 Mt/yr of new ocean leakage; waste above that leakage level is 'further environmental damage.' The baseline global mismanaged fraction is 4.34% (ocean 7.66 Mt), already just under the 4.53% sink bound, so the 'safe level' is essentially the current volume PROVIDED leakage is held at/below 4.5% and the marine stock is drawn down; the binding resource constraint is collection/processing, which currently recycles only ~9% (33 Mt) of all waste. Safe-mitigable volume therefore equals the collectable-and-processable share of the 176.5 Mt stream such that O <= 8 Mt/yr.

### Outcome Analysis

Result: maximum safely-mitigable single-use waste at current volume = 176.5 Mt, subject to the ocean-leakage constraint O <= 8 Mt/yr, which binds at a global mismanaged fraction m* = 4.53%. The 2019 baseline (ocean 7.66 Mt/yr, mismanaged 4.34%) sits just under this bound, meaning the current system is at the edge of the sink. Regional baseline: East & SE Asia (50.6 Mt, 2.02 Mt ocean), South Asia (32.8 Mt, 1.97 Mt), North America & Europe (37.7 Mt, only 0.38 Mt ocean), Sub-Saharan Africa (15.8 Mt, 1.42 Mt, highest mismanaged 9%), Latin America & Caribbean (17.0 Mt, 0.85 Mt), Middle East & Central Asia (8.4 Mt, 0.59 Mt), Other/Small Island States (14.1 Mt, 0.42 Mt). Limitations: the 8 Mt/yr sink is a proxy for marine-ecological tolerance (true carrying capacity is uncertain); terrestrial/soil microplastic damage is not in the sink; the 40-60% single-use share is parameterized, so the safe bound scales with it. The bound is robust to the mismanaged-share range (0.5-4.6%) but sensitive to the chosen ocean threshold.

## Subtask 2: Sub-problem 2: Discuss to what extent plastic waste can be reduced to reach an environmentally safe level, considering s

### Problem

Sub-problem 2: Discuss to what extent plastic waste can be reduced to reach an environmentally safe level, considering sources and uses of single-use/disposable plastics, availability of alternatives, impact on citizens, and city/region/country/continent policies and their regional-specific effectiveness.

### Analysis

Modeled reduction as two levers acting on the stock-and-flow: (1) source reduction, which lowers per-capita single-use waste w_i via reuse, recyclable design, substitution, and circular-economy policy; and (2) collection/processing investment, which lowers the mismanaged fraction m_i. Regional-specific constraints matter: high-income regions already have strong collection (baseline mismanaged ~1%) and can reach near-zero leakage quickly, while low-income coastal regions start at ~9% mismanaged and lack capital for collection infrastructure, so the same policy spend buys different leakage reductions by region (the equity dimension). The environmentally safe level is the one that satisfies the sink O <= 8 Mt/yr and draws down the marine stock; the question becomes how much source reduction is needed to make that achievable given the processing-resource gap (only ~9% recycled today).

### Modeling Process

Safe level as a function of source-reduction: for a per-capita scaling factor s (s=1.0 = no source reduction), the volume is s x 176.5 Mt and the mismanaged ceiling that keeps O <= 8 Mt/yr is m*(s) = 8 / (s x 176.5). Three scenarios: (a) process-only (s=1.0): volume stays 176.5 Mt, requires m <= 4.53% (barely the baseline, so no margin). (b) moderate source reduction (s=0.70, -30%): volume 123.5 Mt, allows m <= 6.48% (relaxes the collection requirement because there is less waste to leak). (c) strong source reduction (s=0.45, -55%): volume 79.4 Mt, allows m <= 10.07% (collection becomes the easier binding constraint). The safe level is thus reached by combining source reduction (which shrinks the numerator) with collection investment (which shrinks the leakage fraction); the two are complements — source reduction lowers the absolute leakage even before m improves. Policy effectiveness varies by region: EPR/extended-producer-responsibility and reuse mandates bite hardest where consumption is high (high-income, mixed); collection-finance and technology transfer bite where leakage is high (low-income coastal).

### Outcome Analysis

Extent of reduction needed to be safe: at minimum the system must hold ocean input at/below ~8 Mt/yr (m* ~4.5% at current volume) and, to be genuinely 'safe' with marine-stock drawdown, push net ocean input below the current 7.66 Mt/yr. The process-only path (no source reduction) leaves no margin (baseline 4.34% vs bound 4.53%) and cannot draw down the stock. Realistic reduction requires 30-55% source reduction plus collection investment: a -30% source-reduction case halves the required stringency on collection (m ceiling rises to 6.48% but the absolute volume is 123.5 Mt), and a -55% case brings the volume to 79.4 Mt. Citizen impact: source reduction (reuse, refill, recyclable design) and safe substitution (paper, fiber, glass, metals, certified bioplastics) alter convenience but avoid the microplastic hazard of some alternatives; low-income regions bear a heavier collection-cost burden. Regional policy effectiveness: strong in high-income (collection near-saturated), mixed in middle-income (growing, investment-sensitive), weak in low-income coastal (finance-constrained), so 'region-specific constraints make some policies more effective than others' holds exactly as the problem posits. Limitations: substitution safety (bioplastic persistence, composting infrastructure) is not quantified; per-capita target s values are assumptions, not derived from a demand model.

### Problem

Sub-problem 3 (first 3): Using the model and discussion, set a target for the minimal achievable level of global single-use/disposable plastic waste and discuss the impacts of achieving it (human life, environment, the multi-trillion-dollar plastic industry). Sub-problem 3 (second 3 / equity): discuss the equity issues from the global crisis and the intended solutions, and how ICM should address them.

### Analysis

Per the dual-target strategy (expert Round 2, Option C), the target is a global minimum (the headline) PLUS an equity floor (a binding per-region ceiling), because a single average can be met by rich-region cuts while the poorest, most coastal regions remain the largest leakage sources. The global minimum is the minimal achievable single-use/disposable waste level given the processing-resource trajectory and source-reduction potential; the equity floor makes the distributional commitment concrete and enforceable.

### Modeling Process

Global minimum (headline): reduce global single-use/disposable waste to 40-50% of the 2019 level (176.5 Mt), i.e. ~71-88 Mt by 2050, with net ocean input ~0 so the existing marine stock is drawn down (not merely held). This is consistent with the strong source-reduction scenario (s=0.45 -> 79.4 Mt) plus circularity. Equity floor (binding on every region by 2050): (i) mismanaged fraction m_i <= 3% for all regions; (ii) per-capita single-use waste <= ~33 kg (the 2019 high-income level), preventing rich-region offsetting of poor-region leakage; and (iii) finance/technology transfer flowing to the regions that cannot meet the floor unaided. Base-case policy trajectory (ease-in curve 2019->2050, per-capita SU down 45-55%, mismanaged to an income-differentiated 0.5-5% floor): 2025 = 148.5 Mt / 5.42 Mt ocean; 2030 = 129.8 Mt / 4.13 Mt; 2040 = 104.8 Mt / 2.69 Mt; 2050 = 96.5 Mt / 2.28 Mt ocean. The base case lands slightly above the 40-50% headline, so the headline assumes stronger source-reduction/circularity than the base trajectory.

### Outcome Analysis

Impacts of achieving ~71-88 Mt by 2050: human life — reduced convenience of disposables but safer food-contact materials, less microplastic exposure, and health co-benefits from cleaner water/soil; the per-capita ceiling keeps high-income lifestyles from simply substituting to other pollutants. Environment — net ocean input ~0 reverses the 'more plastic than fish by 2050' trajectory and allows the marine stock to draw down; terrestrial leakage falls as collection improves. Industry — the multi-trillion-dollar plastic industry contracts in single-use/disposable volume by ~50-60% but pivots to durable, recyclable, and circular models; EPR and producer-responsibility shift cost to producers, favoring firms already in reusable/recyclable formats and pressuring commodity single-use packaging. Equity (second 3): the baseline shows the inequity clearly — high-income regions have the lowest leakage (4.9% of global ocean input) but the highest per-capita consumption, while mixed + lower-middle + low regions carry ~95% of the ocean-leakage burden with ~25% of consumption. Low-income and high-mismanaged regions (Sub-Saharan Africa 9%, Middle East & Central Asia 7%, South Asia 6%) are the ones that need finance to meet the 3% floor. ICM recommendations: (1) adopt the dual-target (global minimum + equity floor) as the binding international framework; (2) a dedicated plastic-waste finance/technology-transfer mechanism (loss-and-damage-style) for low-income coastal regions so the floor is achievable for all; (3) per-capita and mismanaged ceilings with EPR, enforced internationally; (4) differentiate timelines by capability (richer regions cut faster) so the equity floor is not a uniform impossible deadline; (5) treat substitution safety (microplastics from some alternatives) as a co-regulated standard so equity solutions do not trade one pollutant for another. Limitations: the 3% floor and 33 kg per-capita ceiling are policy parameters chosen to be binding, not derived; the base-case 2050 result (96.5 Mt) exceeds the headline, so the headline relies on the upper end of source-reduction/circularity potential.

### Problem

Sub-problem 4: Write a memo to the ICM describing a realistic global target minimum achievable level of global single-use/disposable plastic waste, a timeline to reach it, and the circumstances that may accelerate or hinder the achievement of the target and timeline.

### Analysis

Per the two-horizon strategy (expert Round 3, Option B), the memo states a realistic 2050 dual-target (global minimum + equity floor) de-risked by a verifiable 2030 milestone, with each accelerator/hindrance mapped to the horizon it affects. A single 2050 deadline would invite drift; a 2040 headline would be aspirational against the base-case trajectory; the two-horizon framing is consistent with the model.

### Modeling Process

Target (2050, headline): global single-use/disposable waste <= 71-88 Mt (40-50% of the 2019 level of 176.5 Mt), with net ocean input ~0 (marine-stock drawdown). Equity floor (2050, binding on all regions): mismanaged <= 3%, per-capita single-use <= ~33 kg, finance to those that cannot meet it unaided. 2030 milestone (checkpoint, verifiable): global ocean input back to <= 8 Mt/yr (the sink), global mismanaged <= 4.53% (the sink bound m* = 8/176.5), per-capita single-use reduced ~25% (toward the base-case 129.8 Mt / 4.13 Mt ocean by 2030). Timeline: 2025 ~148.5 Mt / 5.42 Mt; 2030 milestone ~129.8 Mt / 4.13 Mt; 2040 ~104.8 Mt / 2.69 Mt; 2050 target 71-88 Mt / ~0 Mt ocean. Accelerators (mapped to horizon): [2030] binding per-capita/mismanaged caps with EPR and producer responsibility (fastest where consumption is high); safe substitution and reuse/refill mandates where alternatives are safe and cheap; [2050] finance and technology transfer to low-income coastal regions (closes the collection gap); circular-economy design and recycling-at-scale (closes the 9%->higher processing gap); [both] a binding international treaty with verified reporting (prevents rich-region offsetting). Hindrances (mapped to horizon): [2030] plastics production growth (OECD projects ~1,231 Mt by 2060 absent policy, a doubling) which adds new single-use volume faster than collection scales; substitution-safety concerns (microplastics/persistence in some bioplastics) slowing adoption; [2050] weak enforcement in the high-leakage low-income regions without the finance mechanism; recycling economics (downcycling, contaminated streams) capping the processing-resource trajectory; [both] political economy of the multi-trillion-dollar industry resisting EPR and volume caps.

### Outcome Analysis

The memo's realistic minimum is 71-88 Mt of single-use/disposable waste by 2050 (40-50% below 2019), gated by a 2030 milestone (ocean input <= 8 Mt/yr, mismanaged <= 4.53%, per-capita SU -25%) so ICM can course-correct if the 2030 checkpoint is missed. The base-case trajectory (176.5 -> 96.5 Mt, 2019->2050) is the 'do the standard policy mix' result; reaching the lower end of the 71-88 Mt headline requires the strong source-reduction/circularity case. The load-bearing accelerators are the finance/technology transfer (equity) and the binding caps+EPR (volume); the load-bearing hindrances are production growth (which re-inflates the numerator) and enforcement/finance gaps in high-leakage regions (which keep the denominator high). If the 2030 milestone is missed, the 2050 target should be re-based rather than carried blindly. Limitations: the 2030 and 2050 numbers are model outputs, not negotiated commitments; the 'net ocean input ~0' assumes collection can outpace production growth, which is the central feasibility risk the memo flags.
