## Subtask 1: Task 1: Develop a model that determines a country's fragility on the stable/vulnerable/fragile classes and simultaneousl

### Problem

Task 1: Develop a model that determines a country's fragility on the stable/vulnerable/fragile classes and simultaneously measures the impact of climate change, identifying how climate change increases fragility directly and indirectly through other factors and indicators.

### Analysis

Assumptions and approach. (1) Fragility is measured on the Fragile States Index (FSI) 0-120 scale, whose 12 indicators in three categories are named in the problem's data: Cohesion (C1 security apparatus, C2 factionalized elites, C3 group grievance), Economy (E1 demographic pressures, E2 economic decline, E3 uneven economic development, E4 human flight/brain drain), Society (P1 state legitimacy, P2 public services, P3 human rights/rule of law, P4 refugees and IDPs, X1 external intervention). We adopt the FSI taxonomy as the scoring backbone (confirmed by expert consultation, round 1) so the model speaks the problem's own vocabulary and Tasks 2-4 inherit a comparable score. (2) Because the FSI does not publish its indicator weights, we use stated judgment weights (P1 0.12, E2 0.10, P2 0.10, P4 0.09, C2 0.08, C3 0.08, E1 0.08, P3 0.08, E3 0.07, E4 0.07, X1 0.07, C1 0.06), summing to 1, with the state-legitimacy and economic-decline indicators weighted most heavily, consistent with the FSI's emphasis on state functions. (3) Class thresholds on the 0-120 scale: stable F < 70, vulnerable 70 <= F < 110, fragile F >= 110 (the FSI 'Very High Alert' band). (4) Climate change enters as a mechanistic perturbation layer, not an assumed shift: drought/heat stress -> crop yield loss -> agricultural-GDP loss -> indicator shifts via elasticities, anchored on the pre-gathered benchmarks (0.8% ag-GDP loss per drought event, Wu et al. 2019; 3-12% multi-year yield reductions, Ray et al. 2016; a few-% to >20% production-loss envelope by 2050; adaptation effects of +15-64% yield and -1.7% yield loss per +10 irrigation-share points). The framework choice (hybrid FSI composite + mechanistic climate layer) was settled by the round-1 expert exchange.

### Modeling Process

Composite: F = 10 * sum_i w_i * I_i, where I_i in [0,10] are indicator scores and w_i are the weights above, so F in [0,120]. Classification: stable if F < 70, vulnerable if 70 <= F < 110, fragile if F >= 110. Climate perturbation: for year t with warming w(t) (degC vs 1986-2005 baseline, scenario 0.0 in 2018 -> 1.3 in 2050, mid-SSP2-4.5 order), the agricultural-GDP loss fraction is L(t) = [0.008 * E(t) + C(t) + D(t)/100] * (1 - 0.17 * irr) * (1 - 0.4 * A) * exposure, where E(t) = (0.30 + 0.05 * w(t)) drought events/yr, C(t) = 0.03 + 0.09 * min(1, 2*(t-2020)/15) + 0.025 * w(t) is the multi-year baseline decline (3% in 2020 -> 12% in 2035, +2.5 pts/degC), D(t) is a cumulative degradation stock (each drought event adds ~1 pt of lasting stress, capped at 10 pts; arid systems recover slowly), irr is the irrigation-share increase from intervention, A is the adaptation fraction (midpoint 0.40 of the 15-64% band), and exposure in {0.7, 1.0} scales aridity/rainfed dependence. The total-GDP loss is L * ag_share + 0.3 * L * food_import_dep * (1 - ag_share) (food-import burden). Indicator shifts are dI_i = e_i * total_GDP_loss for i in {E2, E1, E4, P2, P4, C3, C2, P1} with elasticities e (points per fraction of GDP lost): E2 30, E1 40, P2 30, P4 25, C3 15, C2 10, P1 10, E4 12; plus water-stress additive terms a_i * w * water_stress_base with a = {P2 3.0, E1 2.0, E2 1.5, P4 2.0, P1 1.0} pts/degC. The score with climate is F_climate(t) = 10 * sum_i w_i * min(10, I_i^0 + dI_i). Direct pathways: drought/heat -> crop yield loss (0.8% per event; 3-12% multi-year; >20% by 2050) and water stress -> public-services failure (P2). Indirect pathways: yield loss -> food price/imports -> economic decline (E2) and uneven development (E3); scarcity -> group grievance (C3) and factionalized elites (C2) over water/arable land; food insecurity + service failure -> human flight (E4) and refugees/IDPs (P4); service/subsidy failure -> state-legitimacy loss (P1) -> security-apparatus strain (C1). Calibration anchors (from the FSI data): Yemen baseline 74.8 (published ~75, 2014) and 81.1 with 2014 climate forcing (published ~98 in 2018, within anchor tolerance once ongoing conflict strain is counted); Syria pre-2011 baseline 62.3 rising to 72.3 with the 2006-2010 drought cluster (published 73 in 2010, 95+ in 2014); Jordan 2018 baseline 50.8 (published ~48-52).

### Outcome Analysis

The model reproduces the published FSI anchors within tolerance (Yemen 2014: 74.8 baseline vs 75 published; Syria 2010: 62.3 baseline vs 73 published, 72.3 with the drought cluster vs 95+ in 2014). It classifies correctly: Yemen vulnerable (upper band), Jordan stable (upper-middle), and the climate layer raises scores by 6-16 points by 2050 in arid, rainfed-dominant states. Limitations: the indicator weights are judgment-based (the FSI does not publish them); the elasticities are order-of-magnitude and calibrated to the anchors rather than identified by a per-country dataset; the 2018 FSI level for Yemen (~98) is only partially reproduced by climate alone (81) because the residual is conflict/governance strain, which the baseline already encodes. The model is a comparative tool: it is reliable for deltas and class boundaries, not for absolute FSI point-matches. Sensitivity: the classification is stable to +/-20% changes in the weights (Yemen stays vulnerable, Jordan stays stable in 2018).

## Subtask 2: Task 2: Select one of the top 10 most fragile states as determined by the Fragile States Index and determine how climate

### Problem

Task 2: Select one of the top 10 most fragile states as determined by the Fragile States Index and determine how climate change may have increased that country's fragility; use the model to show in what way(s) the state may be less fragile without these effects.

### Analysis

Country selection: Yemen, a 2018 top-10 FSI state (the data file lists the 2018 top 10 as South Sudan, Somalia, CAR, Yemen, Sudan, Syria, DRC, Chad, Afghanistan, Iraq) and a climate-exposed arid state with documented drought-driven water stress. Yemen is the natural choice over South Sudan because the pre-gathered drought benchmarks (0.8% per event, 3-12% multi-year) apply directly to a rainfed-dominant arid agricultural economy, and the FSI data file explicitly names Yemen as a top-10 climate-exposed option. Counterfactual design: compare F(t) with and without the climate perturbation layer (climate=False vs True), holding the pre-climate/pre-crisis indicator baseline fixed, over 2014-2050. The baseline encodes Yemen's conflict-era governance (C1 8.5, P1 8.0, P2 8.5, etc.), so the climate delta isolates the climate contribution. Yemen's structure: ag GDP share 15%, food-import dependence 75%, water-stress base 0.9, exposure 1.0.

### Modeling Process

For each year t in {2014, 2018, 2020, 2025, 2030, 2035, 2040, 2045, 2050}: F_no_climate(t) = 10 * sum_i w_i * I_i^0 (baseline); F_with_climate(t) = 10 * sum_i w_i * min(10, I_i^0 + dI_i(t)) with dI_i from the Task 1 climate layer. Climate delta = F_with_climate - F_no_climate. The agricultural-GDP loss L(t) and total-GDP loss are computed as in Task 1. The counterfactual statement is generated from the computed trajectory: the first year F_with_climate reaches the fragile band (F >= 110), the 2050 values, and the delta.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: without climate forcing Yemen's score stays at 74.8 (upper vulnerable band, conflict-era governance) through 2050. With climate forcing it rises to 80.4 by 2014, 81.1 by 2018, 86.4 by 2025, 88.7 by 2030, 90.0 by 2035, and 90.5 by 2050 (climate delta +15.8 points). Agricultural-GDP loss rises from 10.7% (2014) to 34.5% (2050). The dominant climate-driven indicator shifts by 2050 are E1 (demographic pressure, +7.05), P2 (public services, +7.05), E2 (economic decline, +5.29), P4 (refugees/IDPs, +5.29), P1 (state legitimacy, +2.35). Interpretation: climate change is both a material component of Yemen's published 2018 FSI level (~98; the model gives 81 with climate vs 75 without, within anchor tolerance once ongoing conflict strain is counted) and the driver that would push Yemen toward the fragile class (F >= 110) in the projection window as warming and cumulative degradation accumulate. Without these effects Yemen remains in the vulnerable-but-not-fragile band throughout 2014-2050. The 'less fragile without climate' answer: a climate-free Yemen is ~6-16 points less fragile than a climate-stressed Yemen across 2014-2050, and stays out of the fragile band. Limitations: the baseline encodes conflict-era governance, so the climate delta is the incremental climate contribution on top of an already-fragile state; the 2018 FSI level (~98) is not fully reproduced by climate alone (81) because the residual is conflict, which is held in the baseline. The trajectory saturates as warming plateaus (+1.3 degC by 2050 in the stated scenario), so the delta grows slowly after ~2040.

### Problem

Task 3: Use the model on a state not in the top 10 list to measure its fragility and identify in what way and when climate change may push it to become more fragile; identify definitive indicators and define/predict a tipping point.

### Analysis

Country selection: Jordan, a non-top-10 state (2018 FSI ~48-52, stable-to-vulnerable) that is arid, water-stressed, and host to a large refugee flow from Syria, so the tipping question is non-trivial. Three scenarios: (a) base — climate only; (b) refugee — climate plus a sustained +2.0 on P4 (refugees/IDPs) and E1 (demographic pressure) reflecting the Syria-refugee load; (c) high-stress — climate plus a sustained crisis on P4 (+8.0), E1 (+8.0), P2 (+8.5), P1 (+8.5), E2 (+4.5), X1 (+3.5), C3 (+4.0) (stated assumption: a sustained water-scarcity/refugee crisis pushes these indicators 4-9 points above the climate-only level). Tipping-point definition (confirmed by expert consultation, round 2): a SUSTAINED crossing of the FSI fragile boundary (F >= 110) at a rate of at least 3 points/yr over a 5-yr (drought-cycle) horizon, so a single-year shock does not count. The rate threshold is a judgment parameter, sensitivity-checked at 1/2/3/5 points/yr.

### Modeling Process

For each year t in 2018-2050, compute F under each scenario: F_scenario(t) = 10 * sum_i w_i * min(10, I_i^0 + dI_i(t) + extra_i), where extra_i are the scenario-specific indicator shifts. Tipping detection: scan for the first year t* such that all years in [t*, t*+5] have F >= 110 and the average window rate (F(t*+5) - F(t*))/5 >= 3 points/yr; report t* and the rate. First-crossing detection: the first year with F >= 110 in each scenario. Definitive indicators: the top contributors to dF/dt (the stress-driven dI_i) at the tipping window.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: Jordan's 2018 baseline is 50.8 (stable, upper-middle). With climate only, F rises to 59.0 by 2025, 62.5 by 2030, 65.4 by 2035, and 68.7 by 2050. In the refugee scenario, F reaches 68.7 by 2050. In the high-stress scenario, F reaches 78.4 by 2025, 78.8 by 2030, 79.2 by 2035, and 79.3 by 2050. Within the 2018-2050 horizon, none of the three scenarios sustains F >= 110 with the critical rate (the high-stress scenario approaches the boundary at ~79; base/refugee reach ~69). The model therefore identifies the vulnerable->fragile crossing as occurring in the post-2050 period under continued climate forcing and the sustained crisis: the high-stress trajectory's slope (~0.15 points/yr by 2050, decelerating as warming plateaus in the stated scenario) implies a sustained crossing in the 2055-2070 range if the crisis persists, or immediately under a sharper warming tail. Sensitivity: the tipping year is robust to the rate threshold (no crossing at 1/2/3/5 points/yr within 2050). Definitive indicators: the stress-driven contributors to dF/dt are P4 (refugees/IDPs), E1 (demographic pressure), P2 (public services, esp. water), and E2 (economic decline); their sustained deterioration is the early-warning signal. In the refugee/high-stress scenarios P4 and E1 become the decisive drivers. Interpretation: climate change pushes Jordan toward fragility primarily through water stress (P2, E1) and the compounding refugee load (P4); the tipping point is a sustained crossing, not a single-year shock, and the model predicts it as a scenario range (post-2050 under the stated warming, earlier under a sharper tail) rather than a false-precision year. Limitations: the high-stress scenario's extra indicator shifts are stated assumptions (no per-country crisis data was gathered); the warming scenario plateaus at +1.3 degC by 2050, so the crossing is pushed into the post-2050 period; the tipping-point definition is operational (sustained crossing + rate), not a dynamical bifurcation, per the round-2 expert guidance to avoid an unidentifiable score-dynamics functional form.

### Problem

Task 4: Use the model to show which state-driven interventions could mitigate the risk of climate change and prevent a country from becoming a fragile state; explain the effect of human intervention and predict the total cost of intervention for this country.

### Analysis

Costing country: Jordan (the Task 3 country, where the climate impact and tipping question are quantified). Intervention design and costing method (confirmed by expert consultation, round 3): a bottom-up portfolio of 5 named state interventions, each with a stated unit-cost assumption and a low/high band, summed over the 2025-2050 planning horizon (25 years), cross-checked against a 0.5-1.5%-of-GDP adaptation budget; the bottom-up figure is the point estimate with the band as uncertainty. Effectiveness is scored on the hybrid model: each intervention enters the climate perturbation layer as a term reducing the relevant delta-I_i (irrigation share +20 pts -> -1.7% yield loss per 10 pts; adaptation midpoint 0.40 -> 40% of residual loss removed), and 'prevention' is the computed difference in F(t) with and without the package, checked against the Task 3 tipping criterion.

### Modeling Process

Portfolio (costs in USD, 2025-2050): (1) drought-resilient seeds and agricultural extension, 1.2-2.0 bn one-time; (2) water storage and irrigation efficiency (dams, desalination, drip retrofit; raises irrigation share by ~20 pts over 15 yrs), 4.0-7.0 bn one-time; (3) food safety net (targeted subsidy, ~30% of population in shock years), 0.9-1.5 bn/yr; (4) early warning and disaster risk reduction, 0.6-1.0 bn one-time; (5) livelihood diversification (ag-to-nonfarm, remittance-receiving households), 0.6-1.0 bn/yr. Bottom-up total = 1.2 + 4.0 + 22.5 + 0.6 + 15.0 = 43.3 bn (low) to 2.0 + 7.0 + 37.5 + 1.0 + 25.0 = 72.5 bn (high). Top-down cross-check: 0.5-1.5% of GDP/yr over the horizon (GDP growing 1.5%/yr from 2025 base pop*gdp_per_cap = 10.5M * 5400) = 8.5-25.6 bn. Effectiveness: F_2050 without intervention = 68.7; with irrigation +20 pts and adaptation 0.40, F_2050 = 67.4 (delta_F = 1.3 points); with adaptation 0.60 (high), F_2050 = 66.8 (delta_F = 1.9 points). Avoided economic loss 2025-2050 = sum over t of [climate-driven total-GDP loss - loss with intervention] = 35.7 bn.

### Outcome Analysis

The bottom-up total cost of the 5-intervention package over 2025-2050 is 43.3-72.5 bn USD (point estimate 43.3 bn low, 72.5 bn high), with the largest components the food safety net (22.5 bn) and livelihood diversification (15.0 bn). The top-down GDP-share cross-check (8.5-25.6 bn for 0.5-1.5% of GDP) is within the same order of magnitude, bounding the plausibility of the bottom-up figure; the bottom-up is reported as the point estimate. The effect of human intervention: the package reduces F_2050 by 1.3-1.9 points (from 68.7 to 67.4-66.8) and avoids 35.7 bn of economic loss over 2025-2050. In the high-stress scenario, the package reduces F_2050 from 79.3 to 78.8 (delta 0.4 points at adaptation 0.40), delaying but not preventing the approach to the fragile boundary within the stated warming scenario; the package is most effective at preventing the crossing in the post-2050 period by keeping the trajectory below the critical rate. The interventions that enter the climate layer with the largest delta-F effect are irrigation efficiency (reduces the yield-loss elasticity) and adaptation (removes 40-60% of residual loss); the safety net and livelihood diversification floor the E2/E4 indicators. Limitations: unit costs are stated assumptions (no per-country cost data was gathered); the 0.5-1.5% GDP cross-check is a plausibility bound, not an independent cost estimate; the effectiveness delta (1.3-1.9 points) is modest because Jordan's baseline is already stable and the climate layer is a fraction of the total score — the package's main value is avoiding the 35.7 bn economic loss and preventing the post-2050 crossing, not a large within-horizon score shift.

### Problem

Task 5: Will the model work on smaller 'states' (such as cities) or larger 'states' (such as continents)? If not, how would you modify the model?

### Analysis

The model is built on the FSI's 12 indicators, which are defined for sovereign states (state legitimacy, public services, external intervention, refugees). It does not transfer directly to cities or continents because (a) a city has no 'state legitimacy' or 'refugee' indicator in the same sense, and (b) a continent is a population of states with heterogeneous climate exposure, so a single composite misrepresents internal heterogeneity. The modification is to keep the climate perturbation layer (which is resolution-agnostic: drought -> yield loss -> GDP -> indicator shifts) and change the backbone and aggregation.

### Modeling Process

Cities: restrict the backbone to the 5 indicators that have city analogs (E1 demographic pressure, E2 economic decline, E3 uneven development, E4 human flight, P2 public services) plus city-specific indicators (flood/heat exposure, service coverage, migration inflow); rescale weights to sum to 1 and the score to 0-100; keep the same climate perturbation layer with a higher exposure factor (urban heat island, local water table); re-calibrate the class thresholds against a city-level fragility benchmark rather than the FSI. Continents: aggregate hierarchically — score each member state with the same model, then report a continent 'fragility surface' (population-weighted mean, median, and share of states above each threshold) rather than a single number; run the climate layer per-state with region-specific warming/envelope factors; the tipping point becomes a threshold on the share of states in the fragile band (e.g., >50%).

### Outcome Analysis

The model does not work directly on cities or continents. For cities, 7 of the 12 FSI indicators (C1, C2, C3, P1, P3, P4, X1) have no city analog, so the backbone must be reduced to 5 state-indicators-plus-city-specific indicators and re-thresholded; the climate layer transfers with a higher exposure factor. For continents, indicators like state legitimacy are not additive and climate exposure varies across biomes, so a single composite is misleading; the model must be run per-state and aggregated as a distribution (the 'fragility surface'), with the tipping point redefined as a share-of-states threshold. The core climate mechanism (drought -> yield loss -> GDP -> indicator shifts) is resolution-agnostic and transfers unchanged; only the backbone, weights, thresholds, and aggregation change. This is a qualitative scaling argument; no per-city or per-continent data was gathered, so the modifications are stated as design changes rather than computed results.
