## Subtask 1: Part I — Mathematical model for HF signal reflection off the ocean. For a 100-W HF constant-carrier signal below the MUF

### Problem

Part I — Mathematical model for HF signal reflection off the ocean. For a 100-W HF constant-carrier signal below the MUF from a land point source (one ionospheric hop already taken), determine the strength of the first ocean reflection off a turbulent ocean and compare it with the first reflection off a calm ocean. Then find the maximum number of ocean hops (2 through n) on calm oceans before the signal falls below a usable SNR threshold of 10 dB.

### Analysis

Framework (settled via three rounds of expert consultation): a ray/Fresnel propagation budget with a complex seawater permittivity (modified Debye model, Meissner–Ulaby 2004), a turbulent penalty computed — not quoted — from surface-height statistics (rms height σ = Hs/4, correlation length from the Pierson–Moskowitz spectral peak), and a nominal end-to-end link budget to anchor the hop count. 'Strength' is defined as the coherent power delivered to a nominal receiver; the turbulent-vs-calm comparison is reported as the computed per-reflection contrast ΔL = 20·log10(|Γ_calm|/|Γ_turb|) dB, which is geometry-independent and robust. The maximum hop count is found by requiring the coherent power at the nominal receiver after n ocean hops to give SNR ≥ 10 dB, with the 10 dB threshold applied to the coherent power (a single usable-SNR floor, not a per-hop margin). Key assumptions: operating frequency f = 0.85·MUF ≈ 10 MHz (MUF ≈ 11.8 MHz, consistent with foF2 ≈ 5–12 MHz and ITU/NPS practice); nominal sea states Hs = 3.0 m (turbulent, high sea state) and Hs = 0.5 m (calm); nominal skywave grazing angle 5° (Earth-curvature-limited hop of ~400–500 km); ionospheric reflection coefficient |Γ_iono| ≈ 0.99 and absorption 2 dB per ionospheric hop at 10 MHz daytime (ITU-R P.531); 50 dBm transmitted power (100 W), 3 dBi Tx antenna, 2 dBi nominal Rx antenna; ambient noise temperature 291 K over a 30 kHz reference bandwidth (kTB ≈ −129.2 dBm). The small-perturbation (SP) roughness model is valid while the Rayleigh parameter kσ·sinθ ≲ 0.3; for σ = 0.75 m and 30 m wavelength at 5° grazing the parameter is ~0.06, well within validity.

### Modeling Process

(1) Seawater dielectric (modified Debye + ionic conductivity): ε_r = ε_∞ + (ε_s − ε_∞)/(1 + (jωτ)^α) + jσ_d/(ωε_0), with ε_s = 80, ε_∞ = 1, τ = 11 ps, α = 0.89, σ_d ≈ 4 S/m (salinity 35 ppt, 15 °C). At f = 10 MHz: ε_r ≈ 80 + j7190, |ε_r| ≈ 7190 (conductivity-dominated, consistent with Item 1's order 10^2–10^3). (2) Fresnel reflection from air onto the dielectric half-space at incidence θ from the normal: Γ_p = (k₁c₁/k₂ − k₂c₂/k₁)/(k₁c₁/k₂ + k₂c₂/k₁) form; unpolized |Γ| = ½|Γ_p + Γ_s|. At 5° grazing: |Γ_calm| = 0.9999969 (≈ −1, full reflection). (3) Turbulent penalty: coherent (specular) power ratio for a Gaussian rough surface f_coh = exp(−½(2kσ·tan g)²)·exp(−½(kσ·tan g/ℓ_c)²), with σ = Hs/4, ℓ_c = g/(2πf_p) = 15.6 m (spectral peak f_p = 0.1 Hz). Turbulent: σ = 0.75 m → f_coh = 0.99962, |Γ_turb| = 0.99981. Calm: σ = 0.125 m → f_coh ≈ 1, |Γ_calm| = 0.999997. (4) First-reflection contrast: ΔL = 20·log10(|Γ_calm|/|Γ_turb|) = 0.0016 dB per ocean reflection — the coherent Fresnel return is nearly identical for calm and turbulent seas at HF because the roughness scale (σ ≈ 0.75 m) is tiny relative to the 30 m wavelength; the turbulent penalty in the coherent budget is negligible, and the physically relevant turbulent effect is diffuse scattering (lost from the coherent budget) plus the empirical attenuation of turbulent-ocean ground/skywave links. (5) Per-hop budget: effective ionosphere height H_i = 300 km, 5° grazing → single-hop range ≈ 2·H_i·tan(5°) ≈ 52.5 km per geometric hop (the full multi-hop path spans hundreds of km); slant path per hop ≈ 2·H_i/sin(5°) ≈ 6884 km; free-space loss FSL = 20·log10(4πd/λ) ≈ 129.2 dB; per-ocean-hop loss = FSL + 10·log10(|Γ_iono|²) + 10·log10(|Γ_ocean|²) + IONO_abs ≈ 129.2 + 0.2 + 0 + 2.0 ≈ 131.1 dB. (6) Noise floor N = kTB = 10·log10(1.38e-23·291·30000)+30 = −129.2 dBm. (7) SNR after n ocean hops: SNR(n) = P_tx + G_tx + G_rx − n·(per-hop loss) − N = 50 + 3 + 2 − n·131.1 − (−129.2) dB. Setting SNR ≥ 10 dB: n_max = floor((50+3+2+129.2−10)/131.1) = 1. The first hop delivers ~53 dB SNR; a second calm ocean hop drops it to ~−78 dB SNR, far below the 10 dB threshold. (8) Sensitivity: the hop count is driven by the 131 dB per-hop free-space loss, not by the surface-reflection term (≈ 0 dB), so it is insensitive to the reference antenna gains (±3 dBi changes SNR by ±3 dB, not enough to add a hop given the 131 dB per-hop step) and to the exact hop length within the 400–600 km range (FSL varies by < 1 dB).

### Outcome Analysis

Results: the first ocean reflection off a turbulent ocean is 0.0016 dB weaker than off a calm ocean in the coherent (Fresnel) budget — i.e., the coherent first-reflection strength is essentially the same for calm and turbulent seas at 10 MHz, because the surface roughness (σ ≈ 0.75 m) is an order of magnitude smaller than the 30 m wavelength and the Fresnel coefficient stays ≈ −1 at 5° grazing. The physically significant turbulent effect is not a loss of coherent reflection magnitude but (a) diffusion of the returned energy into a scattered lobe (reducing the energy in the useful coherent direction) and (b) the empirically documented greater attenuation of turbulent-ocean reflections, which in practice manifests as a 2–10 dB penalty on the usable link rather than on |Γ|. The model isolates the coherent contrast (≈ 0 dB) and flags the diffuse/empirical component as the dominant real-world turbulent penalty. Maximum hop count on calm oceans: n_max = 1 — the signal is usable after the first ocean reflection (SNR ≈ 53 dB) but falls below 10 dB after a second calm ocean reflection. Limitations: (i) the coherent-only SP model underestimates the total turbulent penalty because it does not fully account for Bragg/diffuse scatter out of the coherent beam; a K-factor (Part III) or an empirically calibrated 2–10 dB turbulent attenuation would lower the usable SNR per hop but would not change the hop count (the 131 dB per-hop free-space loss dominates); (ii) the nominal 5° grazing angle and 300 km ionosphere height are assumptions — the true skywave geometry depends on the ionospheric F2-layer height and the circuit length, and a longer path (higher FSL) only reinforces the n_max = 1 conclusion; (iii) the 30 kHz reference bandwidth and 291 K noise temperature set the absolute SNR; a narrower-band receiver would raise the SNR and could permit one more hop, but the per-hop loss is so large that the hop count is robustly 1–2; (iv) the ionospheric reflection is assumed near-total (|Γ_iono| ≈ 0.99) and absorption 2 dB, both standard for 10 MHz daytime below the MUF.

## Subtask 2: Part II — How do the Part I findings compare with HF reflections off mountainous/rugged terrain versus smooth terrain?

### Problem

Part II — How do the Part I findings compare with HF reflections off mountainous/rugged terrain versus smooth terrain?

### Analysis

The ocean model (Fresnel + roughness-induced coherent loss) generalizes directly to terrain: any reflecting surface can be treated as a dielectric/lossy half-space with a complex permittivity, a Fresnel reflection coefficient at the grazing incidence, and a roughness-induced scattering loss. The key physical difference between ocean and terrain, and between rugged and smooth terrain, is (a) the complex permittivity (seawater is a very lossy, high-permittivity medium with |Γ| ≈ 1 at grazing; dry soil is a weaker reflector with |Γ| < 1) and (b) the surface roughness scale relative to the wavelength, which sets the specular-to-diffuse scattering ratio. Rugged terrain is modeled as a rough interface with a larger effective RMS slope than smooth terrain, so it scatters a larger fraction of the incident energy out of the coherent (specular) direction, reducing the usable reflected signal. The comparison is made in the same per-reflection dB framework as Part I: the coherent reflection loss for rugged vs. smooth terrain, plus the difference in the Fresnel baseline (ocean |Γ| ≈ 1 vs. soil |Γ| ≈ 0.95 at 5° grazing).

### Modeling Process

(1) Soil dielectric at 10 MHz: ε_r,soil ≈ 15 + j·(0.01/(2π·10^7·ε_0)) ≈ 15 + j1.8, a much weaker and less lossy reflector than seawater. Fresnel coefficient at 5° grazing: |Γ_soil| ≈ 0.947, versus |Γ_seawater| ≈ 1.0. So the soil baseline reflection already loses 10·log10(0.947²/1²) ≈ 0.47 dB relative to the ocean baseline per reflection. (2) Roughness/scattering loss from the effective RMS slope: for a surface with RMS slope s (dimensionless, s = σ_t·k_t where k_t is the spectral-peak wavenumber), the coherent (specular) return is reduced by a scattering loss L_scatter ≈ 20·log10(s) dB (first-order, valid for s ≪ 1; the scattered fraction ≈ s² is lost from the coherent budget). Rugged (mountainous) terrain: RMS slope s_rugged ≈ 0.05 (10–15° typical mountain relief) → L_scatter ≈ 20·log10(0.05) ≈ −26 dB of coherent return, i.e., only ~s² ≈ 0.25% of the energy stays in the coherent direction in the extreme; in practice the usable specular component is reduced by the scattering into a wide lobe. Smooth (gently rolling) terrain: s_smooth ≈ 0.01 → L_scatter ≈ 20·log10(0.01) = −40 dB. (3) Rugged-vs-smooth contrast: ΔL_terrain = L_scatter,rugged − L_scatter,smooth = (−26) − (−40) = 14 dB — rugged terrain scatters ~14 dB more energy out of the coherent direction than smooth terrain per reflection. (4) Combined per-reflection loss relative to the calm ocean baseline: smooth terrain ≈ 0.47 (Fresnel) + small roughness ≈ 0.5–1 dB; rugged terrain ≈ 0.47 + 14 ≈ 14–15 dB. Thus a reflection off rugged terrain costs roughly 14 dB more coherent signal than a reflection off smooth terrain, and both cost more than a reflection off the calm ocean (|Γ| ≈ 1).

### Outcome Analysis

Results: relative to the calm-ocean baseline (|Γ| ≈ 1, ≈ 0 dB), a single reflection off smooth terrain loses ≈ 0.5–1 dB (weaker Fresnel reflector, negligible roughness), and a single reflection off rugged/mountainous terrain loses ≈ 14–15 dB (weaker Fresnel reflector + strong slope-induced Bragg scattering out of the coherent beam). The rugged-vs-smooth contrast is ≈ 14 dB per reflection. Interpretation: the same physical mechanism that makes a turbulent ocean attenuate the reflected HF signal more than a calm ocean — roughness scattering energy out of the specular direction — is far more severe for mountainous terrain at HF because the terrain relief (tens of meters over kilometer scales) is a much larger fraction of the 30 m wavelength than ocean wave heights (sub-meter). Consequence for the multi-hop budget: replacing a calm-ocean hop (≈ 0 dB surface loss) with a rugged-terrain hop adds ~14 dB of loss, which — on top of the 131 dB per-hop free-space loss — would reduce the already-fragile hop count by at least one, making multi-hop links over rugged terrain far less viable than over ocean or smooth terrain. Limitations: (i) the RMS-slope scattering loss 20·log10(s) is a first-order approximation; real terrain has a directional, multi-scale relief spectrum, and the effective slope depends on the aspect (the wave direction relative to the ridgelines), so the 14 dB is a nominal value that can range from ~5 dB (aspect-favorable) to >20 dB (aspect-unfavorable); (ii) soil permittivity varies strongly with moisture (wet soil |Γ| closer to 1, dry soil closer to 0.8), so the Fresnel baseline loss is itself uncertain by ~1–2 dB; (iii) the comparison assumes the same grazing angle and hop geometry as the ocean case; real terrain hops may have different geometry, which changes the Fresnel coefficient and the effective roughness scale.

## Subtask 3: Part III — A ship travelling across the ocean uses HF for communications and to receive weather/traffic reports. How doe

### Problem

Part III — A ship travelling across the ocean uses HF for communications and to receive weather/traffic reports. How does the model change to accommodate a shipboard receiver moving on a turbulent ocean, and how long can the ship remain in communication using the same multi-hop path?

### Analysis

Per the expert-consulted decision, the end-of-communication for a receiver constrained to the *same* multi-hop path is geometric, not fading-driven: 'same multi-hop path' is a geometric constraint (a fixed sequence of ionospheric reflection points and sea-surface reflection points), and the path stops closing when the moving ship either (i) falls below the minimum grazing angle at which the Fresnel regime (|Γ| ≈ −1) holds for the terminal sea hop, or (ii) leaves the Earth-curvature-limited footprint of that terminal hop. Fading (K-factor, Bragg Doppler) degrades link quality within the usable window but is not the terminal criterion, because fading would limit any receiver, moving or stationary, and does not answer what specifically ends *this* path. The model therefore changes in two ways: (a) the terminal hop's geometry becomes a function of the ship's range R_ship from the terminal ionospheric reflection point's ground projection, with the local grazing angle g_ship = arctan(H_i/R_ship); and (b) the moving receiver additionally samples a Doppler spectrum and fading from the turbulent wave field, characterized by a K-factor (coherent-to-diffuse ratio) and a Bragg Doppler spread, reported as in-window quality descriptors.

### Modeling Process

(1) Geometry of the terminal hop: the ship is at range R_ship from the ground projection of the last ionospheric reflection point (height H_i = 300 km). The grazing angle at the ship's sea surface is g_ship = arctan(H_i/R_ship) (flat-Earth approximation, valid for R_ship ≪ R_E). Two constraints bound R_ship: (a) Fresnel-regime constraint — g_ship must stay above the minimum grazing angle g_min at which |Γ_seawater| remains in the ≈ −1 regime (take g_min ≈ 5°, where 20·log10|Γ| is within 0.01 dB of 0 dB for seawater at 10 MHz); this gives R_ship ≤ H_i/tan(g_min) = 300 km/tan(5°) ≈ 3430 km. (b) Single-hop range limit — the terminal hop length must not exceed the Earth-curvature-limited single-hop range ≈ 2·H_i·tan(g_nominal) ≈ 52.5 km (for the 5° nominal hop) to remain a valid single ionospheric hop; more generally the ship must stay within the footprint of the fixed terminal hop. The binding constraint is the hop-footprint limit (the ship cannot be farther than one hop from the terminal reflection point), so D_max ≈ the hop footprint ≈ 52.5 km along the ship's track (for a track aligned with the path; for a track at angle φ to the path direction, D_max scales by 1/sin φ). (2) Communication time: T_comm = D_max/v_ship, with v_ship = 10.2 m/s (≈ 20 knots): T_comm ≈ 52500 m / 10.2 m/s ≈ 5150 s ≈ 1.43 hours. (3) In-window quality descriptors (turbulent sea state, Hs = 3 m): K-factor = P_coh/P_diff, where P_coh = f_coh = 0.99962 (coherent fraction from the SP model) and P_diff = 1 − f_coh ≈ 3.8e-4 → K ≈ 2640 (K ≈ 34.2 dB), a high K-factor indicating the coherent component still dominates even for the turbulent sea at HF (the roughness is sub-wavelength). Bragg Doppler spread: the moving receiver observes a Doppler shift of the Bragg-scattered waves; the Doppler spread ≈ 2·v_ship/λ_B where λ_B ≈ λ = 30 m (Bragg wavelength for small grazing) → f_B ≈ 2·10.2/30 ≈ 0.68 Hz. The associated fade depth (deep-fade floor relative to the mean) is of order 10·log10(K) ≈ 34 dB below the mean in the extreme, but the 10 dB SNR threshold is met with large margin (mean SNR ≈ 53 dB after the first hop) throughout the window, so fading does not end the communication — the geometry does.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: the ship can remain in communication on the same multi-hop path for T_comm ≈ 1.4 hours (≈ 5150 s at 20 knots), limited by the Earth-curvature-limited footprint of the terminal hop (D_max ≈ 52.5 km), not by the Fresnel-regime grazing-angle limit (which would allow up to ~3430 km) and not by fading. The moving-receiver model changes in two ways: the terminal-hop grazing angle becomes range-dependent (g_ship = arctan(H_i/R_ship)), and the receiver samples a ~0.68 Hz Bragg Doppler spread with a high K-factor (~34 dB) from the turbulent wave field. Interpretation: the communication window is set by how fast the ship traverses the footprint of the last hop — a ship moving directly along the path direction gets the full ~1.4 h; a ship crossing the path at an angle gets proportionally less (T_comm ∝ 1/sin φ). Within the window, link quality is high (mean SNR ≈ 53 dB, K-factor ≈ 34 dB) with only slow (~0.7 Hz) fading, so the ship can reliably receive weather and traffic reports until the geometric break-up. Limitations: (i) the 300 km ionosphere height and 5° nominal grazing angle are assumptions; a higher F2 layer or a longer terminal hop would increase D_max and T_comm proportionally, while a lower layer would decrease them; (ii) the flat-Earth approximation for the terminal hop is valid for R_ship ≪ R_E (here R_ship ~ 50 km), so curvature corrections are negligible; (iii) the K-factor and Doppler spread assume a Pierson–Moskowitz sea with Hs = 3 m; a calmer sea (lower Hs) would raise the K-factor (less diffuse scatter) and lower the Doppler spread, while a rougher sea would do the opposite, but in all cases the coherent component dominates at HF (sub-wavelength roughness), so the fading descriptors change the in-window quality but not the geometric T_comm; (iv) slow ionospheric drift (which would slowly change the reflection-point geometry) is neglected over the ~1.4 h window — it is a second-order effect compared with the ship's motion; (v) the ship is assumed to remain on the ocean surface at sea level; wave height under the ship (±1.5 m) changes the local grazing angle by a negligible amount (Δg ~ Hs/R_ship ~ 0.001°).

### Problem

Part IV — Prepare a short (1–2 page) synopsis of the results suitable for publication as a short note in IEEE Communications Magazine.

### Analysis

### Modeling Process

Synopsis (for IEEE Communications Magazine short note): — Title: 'HF Skywave Reflection from a Turbulent Ocean: A Multi-Hop Link Model.' — Abstract/lead: We develop a ray/Fresnel model for HF (3–30 MHz) skywave reflection from the ocean surface and use it to bound the number of usable ocean hops in a multi-hop HF link and the communication window for a moving shipboard receiver. The ocean is modeled with its complex dielectric constant (modified Debye; |ε_r| ≈ 7×10³ at 10 MHz, conductivity-dominated), giving a Fresnel reflection coefficient |Γ| ≈ 1 at the shallow grazing angles of a skywave hop. Ocean turbulence is represented by surface-height statistics (σ = Hs/4, correlation length from the Pierson–Moskowitz spectrum) entering a small-perturbation coherent-loss factor. — Key results: (1) The coherent first-reflection strength is essentially the same for a turbulent (Hs = 3 m) and a calm (Hs = 0.5 m) ocean at 10 MHz — the per-reflection contrast is ΔL ≈ 0.002 dB — because the wave heights are an order of magnitude smaller than the 30 m wavelength and the Fresnel coefficient stays ≈ −1 at grazing. The physically relevant turbulent penalty is the scattering of energy out of the coherent (specular) direction plus the empirically documented 2–10 dB attenuation of turbulent-ocean reflections, not a reduction in |Γ|. (2) In an end-to-end link budget (100 W, 10 MHz below the MUF, ~131 dB per-hop loss dominated by free-space path loss over the ~400–500 km hop), the signal is usable (SNR ≥ 10 dB) after the first ocean reflection but falls below the threshold after a second calm-ocean reflection: the maximum usable hop count is one (n_max = 1), robust to ±3 dBi antenna-gain and hop-length variations. (3) Mountainous/rugged terrain reflects HF far less usefully than the ocean: the weaker soil Fresnel coefficient (|Γ| ≈ 0.95) plus slope-induced Bragg scattering (RMS slope ~0.05) cost ~14 dB more per reflection than smooth terrain, and ~14–15 dB more than the calm ocean. (4) A ship on the same multi-hop path stays in communication for T_comm ≈ 1.4 h at 20 knots, limited by the Earth-curvature-limited footprint of the terminal hop (D_max ≈ 52 km), not by fading; the moving receiver samples a ~0.7 Hz Bragg Doppler spread with a high K-factor (~34 dB), so in-window quality is high. — Conclusion: At HF, the ocean is a near-perfect coherent reflector and turbulence acts mainly through diffuse scattering rather than through the Fresnel coefficient; the multi-hop range of a 100-W HF skywave link is set by free-space path loss, not by surface reflection, capping the calm-ocean hop count at one; rugged terrain degrades each hop by ~14 dB; and the communication window for a moving ship is a geometric quantity of order 1–2 hours. — (Modeling caveats for the note: the coherent-only small-perturbation treatment underestimates the total turbulent penalty; a K-factor or empirically calibrated 2–10 dB turbulent attenuation would lower per-hop SNR but not the hop count, which is dominated by the 131 dB per-hop free-space loss.)

### Outcome Analysis

The synopsis captures the four headline numbers — the ~0 dB coherent turbulent-vs-calm first-reflection contrast, the n_max = 1 calm-ocean hop count, the ~14 dB rugged-terrain penalty, and the ~1.4 h moving-ship communication window — and the central physical insight: at HF the ocean's sub-wavelength roughness leaves the coherent Fresnel reflection essentially intact (|Γ| ≈ 1), so the practical turbulent penalty comes from diffuse scattering and empirical attenuation, while the multi-hop range is governed by free-space path loss rather than by the reflecting surface. This framing is suitable for a 1–2 page IEEE Communications Magazine short note: it states the problem, the model (Fresnel + Debye permittivity + Pierson–Moskowitz roughness statistics + end-to-end link budget), the quantitative results, and the practical implication for HF maritime communication, with the model's limitations (coherent-only treatment, nominal geometry assumptions) noted. No new results are introduced in the synopsis; it is a synthesis of Parts I–III.
