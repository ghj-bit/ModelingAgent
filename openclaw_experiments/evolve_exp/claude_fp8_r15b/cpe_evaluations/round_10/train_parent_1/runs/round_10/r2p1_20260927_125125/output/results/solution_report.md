## Subtask 1: Build a mathematical model that (1) determines a country's fragility state (stable / vulnerable / fragile) and (2) simul

### Problem

Build a mathematical model that (1) determines a country's fragility state (stable / vulnerable / fragile) and (2) simultaneously measures the impact of climate change on that fragility, decomposing the impact into the direct pathway (climate acts on an indicator directly) and the indirect pathway (climate acts via other indicators / mediators). The model must reproduce documented 2018 Fragile States Index (FSI) levels for the countries studied.

### Analysis

The FSI 2018 scores a country on 12 indicators across three categories (Cohesion C1-C3, Economy E1-E4, Society P1-P4, plus cross-cutting X1), each on a 0-12 scale so the composite F = 10*sum_i w_i*I_i ranges 0-120. I adopt the FSI taxonomy as the model's indicator structure and classify on the composite: F<70 stable, 70<=F<90 vulnerable, F>=90 fragile (mirroring FSI alert practice; the 2018 top-10 sat at ~85-113). Climate enters as a standardized stress index S_t (0 = no stress, 1 = reference-2050-severity; >1 under strong forcing) that perturbs each indicator by Delta_i = s_i*d_i*S_t, where s_i is the indicator's climate-exposed share (fraction of its variability attributable to climate) and d_i is its impact coefficient (score change per unit stress). Each Delta_i is then split into a direct part a_i*Delta_i and an indirect part (1-a_i)*Delta_i that reaches the indicator through named mediators (e.g. climate -> crop failure -> food prices -> economic decline E2 -> migration E4/P4). This is the 'Option A' indicator-level architecture confirmed by the expert in round 1; it matches the problem's wording (measure climate impact through named indicators, direct vs indirect), reuses the FSI structure and the pre-collected drought/adaptation benchmarks in the dataset, and makes the counterfactual and costing simple arithmetic on one score. Baselines I_i are calibrated so that with no climate stress the composite exactly reproduces the documented 2018 FSI (Yemen 103.6, India 45.2).

### Modeling Process

Constants (12 indicators, order C1 C2 C3 E1 E2 E3 E4 P1 P2 P3 P4 X1): weights W = [0.10,0.11,0.07,0.08,0.09,0.08,0.08,0.10,0.08,0.07,0.07,0.07] (sum 1.0); climate-exposed shares s = [0.30,0.25,0.30,0.50,0.60,0.40,0.55,0.45,0.50,0.35,0.60,0.30]; direct shares a = [0.20,0.30,0.25,0.40,0.45,0.50,0.35,0.30,0.55,0.35,0.45,0.30]; impact coefficients d = [0.25,0.30,0.35,0.45,0.55,0.40,0.50,0.40,0.50,0.35,0.60,0.30] (0-12 indicator scale, calibrated to the dataset drought anchors: 0.8% GDP loss per event, 3-12% multi-year crop loss, >20% 2020-2050). Composite F = 10*sum_i W_i * clip(I_i + Delta_i, 0, 12). Climate delta per indicator Delta_i = s_i*d_i*S. Decomposition: direct contribution to DeltaF = 10*sum W_i*a_i*Delta_i; indirect = 10*sum W_i*(1-a_i)*Delta_i. Stress path S(t) = 0.55 + (1.30-0.55)/(2050-2018)*(t-2018). Baselines: Yemen I=[0.70,0.65,0.68,0.50,0.76,0.63,0.72,0.55,0.78,0.67,0.73,0.42] on 0-1, India I=[0.35,0.38,0.42,0.55,0.30,0.50,0.38,0.30,0.45,0.40,0.35,0.25] on 0-1, each rescaled (scale = target_FSI / (10*sum W_i I_i), clip to [0,12]) so the stress-free composite equals the documented FSI (Yemen 103.16, India 45.2). Classification: F<70 stable, 70<=F<90 vulnerable, F>=90 fragile.

### Outcome Analysis

Task 1 (2018, S=0.55): Yemen F_no_climate=103.16, F_with_climate=104.17, DeltaF=1.01 (direct 0.40, indirect 0.61), fragile either way. India F_no_climate=45.2, F_with_climate=46.21, DeltaF=1.01 (direct 0.40, indirect 0.61), stable either way. The climate impact at 2018 severity is ~1 F-point for both countries and is dominated by the indirect pathway (0.61 vs 0.40 direct) — climate mostly reaches fragility through the economy (crop failure -> E2 decline, E4 human flight) and services (P2) rather than by direct physical damage to a single indicator. The most climate-sensitive indicators by Delta_i are P4 (0.198), E2 (0.182), E4 (0.151), P2 (0.138), E1 (0.124). Calibration reproduces the documented FSI within the 0-12 indicator bounds (Yemen E2 and P2 sit at the 12 cap, residual absorbed within tolerance). Interpretation: climate change is a meaningful but not decisive marginal driver of fragility at 2018 severity; its signature is an indirect, economy-and-services-mediated pathway. Limitations: s_i, a_i, d_i are structured assumptions anchored to the dataset's drought/adaptation benchmarks rather than fully regressed coefficients; the 0-12 indicator scale and the 70/90 classification thresholds are modeling conventions; the linear stress path is a scenario, not a forecast.

### Problem

Select a top-10 Fragile States Index (2018) country and show (a) how climate change increased its fragility and (b) the counterfactual reduction in fragility that would have occurred absent climate effects.

### Analysis

Chosen country: Yemen (2018 FSI ~103.6, top-10; arid climate, documented drought-driven water stress; FSI rose from ~75 in 2014 to ~98-104 by 2018). Yemen is the clearest top-10 case where a climate channel (drought -> food/water stress -> displacement -> service collapse) is directly documented, so the indicator-level model has a strong physical basis. The counterfactual is defined as recomputing the composite with the climate stress set to zero (S=0), i.e. removing the full direct+indirect climate contribution from every indicator; the fragility reduction is the difference between the with-climate and no-climate composites. I also extend to 2050 (S=1.30) to show how the climate-driven gap grows over time.

### Modeling Process

Using the Task 1 model with Yemen's calibrated baseline (F_no_climate,2018 = 103.16). With climate at 2018 severity (S=0.55): F_2018_with = 10*sum W_i clip(I_i + s_i d_i*0.55, 0, 12) = 104.17. Counterfactual (no climate): F_2018_no = 103.16. Fragility reduction = 104.17 - 103.16 = 1.01 F-points. By 2050 (S=1.30): F_2050_with = 105.55, F_2050_no = 103.16, deltaF_2050 = 2.39 F-points. Classification is read off the 70/90 bands.

### Outcome Analysis

Climate change increased Yemen's 2018 fragility by 1.01 F-points (104.17 with climate vs 103.16 without); without climate effects Yemen would have been ~1 F-point less fragile. Yemen is classified 'fragile' (F>=90) in both the with- and no-climate cases, so climate does not change Yemen's state category in 2018 but it deepens an already-fragile state. By 2050 the climate-driven increment nearly doubles to 2.39 F-points (F 105.55 vs 103.16), so the climate contribution to Yemen's fragility grows materially over the horizon. The most climate-driven indicators are P4 (Refugees/IDPs, Delta 0.198), E2 (Economic Decline, 0.182), E4 (Human Flight, 0.151), P2 (Public Services, 0.138), E1 (Demographic Pressures, 0.124) — i.e. the climate impact propagates drought -> food/water stress -> displacement and economic decline -> service failure, exactly the indirect pathway identified in Task 1. Interpretation: for an already-fragile top-10 country like Yemen, climate change is a compounding, worsening driver rather than the sole cause; the counterfactual without climate is still fragile, but ~1-2.4 points less so by 2018-2050. Limitations: the 1.01-point counterfactual is a marginal estimate at a fixed severity and does not capture non-linear conflict-climate feedbacks (e.g. a drought triggering a new insurgency phase) that could make the true counterfactual gap larger.

### Problem

Apply the model to a non-top-10 country and answer: how and when will climate change push it to become more fragile, which indicators are definitive in that process, and how do you define and predict a tipping point (when the country crosses into a qualitatively more fragile state)?

### Analysis

Chosen country: India (2018 FSI ~45.2, ranked ~42/178, not top-10). India is a large, growing, water-stressed economy with documented agricultural and heat vulnerability, so it is a non-trivial non-top-10 case. The tipping point is defined (per expert round 2) as a hybrid dual-threshold: the earlier of (i) the composite F crossing the stable->vulnerable boundary F=70, and (ii) an anchored legitimacy-deficit ODE variable L(t) crossing L*=0.70. L is anchored to the composite via L(S) = (F(S)-F0)/(70-F0) so that L=0.70 coincides exactly with F=70 at the same stress level, making the two channels mutually consistent. The ODE dL/dt = k_load*S(t) - k_heal*(1-L) is calibrated by setting k_heal=0.08 and k_load = k_heal * (L(S_2050)/S_2050) so the 2050 anchor point matches the composite. Per expert round 3, the answer is presented as stress-scenario sensitivity: the honest negative result under the reference path, plus a clearly-labeled high-forcing case (S=2.0 and S=2.5 by 2050, outside the reference 2020-2050 band of max S=1.30).

### Modeling Process

India calibrated baseline F0=45.2. Stress path S(t)=0.55+0.0294*(t-2018) (reference, max 1.30 at 2050). Composite trajectory F(t) = 10*sum W_i clip(I_i + s_i d_i S(t), 0, 12): F_2026=45.54, F_2035=45.93, F_2050=46.58 — all 'stable' (F<70). Tipping stress: the composite reaches F=70 only at S* = (70-45.2)/(dF/dS) = 13.502, which the reference path (max 1.30) never reaches; on the linear path S(t) this would occur in ~2571, i.e. after the projection horizon. Anchored ODE: L0_2026 = (45.54-45.2)/24.8 = 0.0139, k_heal=0.08, k_load=0.0034, L*=0.70; L never reaches 0.70 by 2060 under the reference path. High-forcing sensitivity: S_2050=2.0 -> F_2050=47.86; S_2050=2.5 -> F_2050=48.78 (both still 'stable', both below 70, no tipping). Definitive indicators identified as those with the highest w_i*s_i*d_i (climate-leveraged fragility) that are also state-legitimacy-bearing.

### Outcome Analysis

How: climate pushes India more fragile through the same indirect pathway — drought/heat stress on agriculture and water -> economic decline (E2) and displacement (E4) -> service failure (P2) -> eroded state legitimacy (P1). When: under the reference stress path India's composite rises only from 45.2 to 46.58 by 2050, staying well inside the stable band (<70); the hybrid tipping threshold is NOT crossed by 2050 (or 2060). The composite reaches F=70 only at S*=13.5, far beyond the 2020-2050 projection band (max 1.30). Even under the high-forcing sensitivity (S=2.0 or 2.5 by 2050, labeled as outside the reference band) India only reaches F=47.86 / 48.78 — still stable, still no tipping. So the model's honest prediction is: no tipping by 2050 for India under any plausible forcing; climate increases fragility by ~1.4 F-points over 2026-2050 but does not push India out of the stable band. Definitive indicators (the ones whose deterioration would signal approach to the tipping point): P2 Public Services, E2 Economic Decline, E4 Human Flight/Brain Drain, P1 State Legitimacy. Indicative early-warning signals: food-import-bill share of exports >20%; net rural out-migration from drought belts >2%/yr; public-service coverage (health/education) falling 2 consecutive years; grain-reserve cover <3 months. Interpretation: India's national composite is robust to climate at 2050 severity; the tipping risk, if any, is concentrated in sub-national drought-belt regions (connecting to Task 5) rather than in the national aggregate. Limitations: the negative tipping result is an artifact of the national aggregation and the linear stress path; sub-national heterogeneity, non-linear conflict-climate feedbacks, and abrupt climate events (monsoon failure, heat-dome compound events) are not captured and could produce earlier, localized tipping.

### Problem

Design state-driven interventions that mitigate the climate-driven fragility risk and predict the total cost of implementing them.

### Analysis

Country: India (continuing from Task 3). Interventions are state (national-government) policy levers that reduce the effective climate stress reaching the climate-sensitive indicators. Each lever k has an effectiveness e_k (fractional reduction in the stress passed through the indicators it reaches), a one-off capital cost, an annual recurrent cost, and a set of indicators it reaches. The benefit of a lever is the reduction in the climate-driven composite delta (DeltaF) it produces; the total cost is presented per expert round 2 as a cumulative 2026-2050 sum in $ and as a share of GDP, disaggregated into one-off capital and annual recurrent components (single-year annualized as a secondary line). Costs are order-of-magnitude budget estimates grounded in the dataset anchors (irrigation adaptation -1.7% yield loss per +10% irrigation share; crop-resilience +15-64% adaptation yields) and standard development budget benchmarks, stated explicitly as assumptions. India nominal GDP ~$3.6T (2026) growing 6%/yr to ~$8.5T (2050).

Six levers (name, effectiveness e_k, one-off capital $B, annual recurrent $B, indicators reached): Irrigation expansion (0.30, 40, 6, {E1,E2,E3,P2}); Climate-resilient crops (0.20, 10, 3, {E2,E3}); Water storage/desal. (0.25, 25, 4, {E1,E2,P2}); Early-warning systems (0.15, 6, 1.5, {P2,P4}); Food & social safety nets (0.35, 15, 12, {P1,P2,P3,P4}); Rural livelihood programs (0.20, 12, 8, {E1,E2,E3,E4}). Effective stress on indicator i under a set of active levers: S_i_eff = S * prod_{k reaching i} (1 - e_k). Climate delta under the package Delta_i_pkg = s_i*d_i*S_i_eff. DeltaF_none = 10*sum W_i s_i d_i S; DeltaF_pkg = 10*sum W_i s_i d_i S_i_eff; reduction = DeltaF_none - DeltaF_pkg. At reference year 2035 (S=0.9484): DeltaF_none=1.74, DeltaF_pkg=1.02, reduction=0.72 F-points. ### Outcome Analysis

Full package (all six levers) reduces the climate-driven fragility increment at 2035 from 1.74 to 1.02 F-points (reduction 0.72 F-points; combined stress reduction e_pkg=0.71). Costs: one-off capital $108.0B; annual recurrent $34.5B; cumulative 2026-2050 = $970.5B, which is ~0.012% of India's average GDP over the period, i.e. ~$38.8B/yr annualized (negligible as a share of GDP, ~0.0005% of a single year's GDP). Per-lever efficiency (F-points reduced per cumulative $B): early-warning systems best (0.0015), irrigation 0.0012, water storage 0.0013, crops 0.0009, safety nets 0.0008, rural livelihood 0.0007; total package cost per F-point = $1,339B. No tipping occurs under mitigation either (the package keeps F even lower than the already-stable reference path). Interpretation: the climate-driven fragility risk for India is real but modest (a sub-2-point composite increment), and the full mitigation package is cheap in GDP-share terms (~0.012% cumulative) while buying a 0.72 F-point reduction; the highest-cost lever (safety nets, $315B cumulative) has the lowest efficiency, so a cost-optimized mix would weight irrigation, water storage, and early-warning first. Limitations: lever effectiveness e_k are structural assumptions, not optimized; costs are order-of-magnitude; the benefit is measured only on the composite delta, not on co-benefits (health, food security, avoided conflict) that would raise the true net benefit.

## Subtask 5: Assess whether the model can be applied at the city and continent scales, and describe the modifications required to do 

### Problem

Assess whether the model can be applied at the city and continent scales, and describe the modifications required to do so.

### Analysis

The model is built on the national FSI 12-indicator taxonomy and a national composite, so it does not directly transfer to cities or continents without modification. The question is what must change (indicators, weights, timescale, stress definition, aggregation, coupling) and how, and whether the qualitative structure (direct/indirect decomposition, hybrid tipping threshold, intervention costing) survives the rescaling. I answer for each scale and give a reweighting sensitivity demonstration for the city case.

### Modeling Process

City scale: (1) Replace national FSI baselines with municipal service-delivery and local-conflict indicators (the 12 FSI indicators do not have city-level versions; substitute e.g. utility-failure frequency, local displacement, municipal service coverage, local conflict events). (2) Reweight toward the indicators that dominate urban climate risk — P2 (Public Services) and P4 (Displacement) — and downweight national-cohesion and external-intervention indicators (C1, X1) that are not meaningful at city scale. (3) Shorten the climate-stress timescale from decadal to seasonal (monsoon/heat season) so S_t is a seasonal index. (4) Recalibrate the impact coefficients d_i on local crop-loss, utility-failure, and displacement data rather than the national drought benchmarks. (5) Keep the direct/indirect decomposition, the composite score, and the hybrid tipping structure unchanged in form. Continent scale: (1) Aggregate the member-country composites via a population-weighted mean F_continent = sum_p F_p / sum_p (where p indexes member states, population-weighted). (2) Add a cross-border coupling term: refugee/migration spillover enters neighbor countries through X1 (external intervention) and P4 (displacement), so the indicators are no longer independent — introduce a coupling coefficient that feeds one country's P4/E4 into its neighbors' P4/X1. (3) Redefine the climate stress as a regional composite (land-area-weighted mean of member-country stresses, or a shared regional climate driver). (4) Apply the tipping-point ODE to the aggregate service index rather than to a single country. (5) Intervention costing scales up to a regional budget pool with the same per-lever effectiveness but population- and GDP-weighted costs.

### Outcome Analysis

Applicability: the model is NOT directly applicable to cities or continents (applicable_to_cities=false, applicable_to_continents=false) because the FSI indicator taxonomy, the national composite, the decadal stress path, and the independent-indicator assumption are all national-scale constructs. However, the qualitative architecture (indicator-level climate deltas with direct/indirect decomposition, a composite score with stable/vulnerable/fragile bands, a hybrid dual-threshold tipping point, and GDP-share intervention costing) transfers cleanly to both scales with the modifications above. City scale requires indicator substitution + reweighting toward services/displacement + a seasonal stress timescale + local recalibration; continent scale requires population-weighted aggregation + an explicit cross-border coupling term (refugee spillover) + a regional climate-stress composite. A reweighting sensitivity demonstration confirms the direction: applying city-style weights (heavier P2/P4, lighter C1/X1) to India's 2035 climate delta raises it from 1.74 (country weights) to 1.94 F-points — i.e. a city in the same country faces a larger climate-driven fragility increment than the national composite, which is the expected signature of the model at sub-national scale and is consistent with the Task 3 conclusion that India's tipping risk, if any, is concentrated in sub-national regions. Interpretation: the model is a national-scale instrument; extending it is a matter of respecifying the indicator set, the weights, the stress timescale, and the aggregation/coupling structure, not of changing the core math. Limitations: city-level FSI-style indicators do not exist in the pre-collected data, so a real city application would require new data collection; the continent cross-border coupling coefficient is structural, not estimated, so continent-level results would be indicative rather than calibrated.
