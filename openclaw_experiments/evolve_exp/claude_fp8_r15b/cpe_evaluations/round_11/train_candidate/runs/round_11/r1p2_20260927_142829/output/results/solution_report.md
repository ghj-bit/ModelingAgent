### Problem

Requirement 1 — brief assessment of the three Kariba options (repair, rebuild, replace with a multi-dam cascade) with a cost/benefit overview, within the two-page limit. Scope: screen the three options on capital cost, annual benefits (hydropower, irrigation, flood protection), lifecycle, and risk, and rank them to give ZRA management a decision-ready comparison.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) Kariba reference = 180 km3 gross, 143 km3 active (79% operable), 128 m arch dam, catchment 679,496 km2, mean annual inflow 51.5 km3/yr (1,313.6 m3/s), 24-h design flood 15,000 m3/s, dry-season minimum 250 m3/s (sources: external_data.md Items 1-2, Wikipedia Kariba Dam / Zambezi, IIASA WP-88-089, ZRA river flows). (2) Dam capital cost follows a power law C = c0 * V_active^beta * (H/100)^0.5 with beta = 0.65 (economies of scale), calibrated so a Kariba-scale unit (143 km3 active, 128 m) costs ~$35B in today's dollars. (3) Repair = 30% of rebuild cost, restores ~40 yr of service; rebuild = full cost, 80 yr; multi-dam = sum of 12 individual dams. (4) Hydropower: P = rho*g*Q*H*eta, annual energy = P * cf * 8760 h with capacity factor cf = 0.5 (seasonal Zambezi flow, wet-season spill, low dry-season head), priced at $25/MWh; calibrated so a Kariba-scale unit yields ~4 TWh/yr (~$87M/yr), consistent with the real Kariba output. (5) Irrigation: 600 m3/s dry-season allocation over 150 days at $0.30/m3. (6) Flood protection: expected annual damage avoided = $350 per (m3/s) of peak attenuation, reflecting the downstream ZM/ZW/BW floodplain (agriculture, livestock, settlements). (7) 5% discount rate, 80-yr horizon, one replacement at end of life at 60% cost. Method: deterministic cost-benefit screening with net present value; appropriate for a two-page overview because the goal is a relative ranking, not an exact optimum. Limitation acknowledged: pure power+flood NPV understates Option 3's value because it omits risk-diversification and safety benefits (the dam is a single point of failure 'in dire need of maintenance').

### Modeling Process

Cost: C(V,H) = c0 * V^0.65 * (H/100)^0.5, c0 = 3.5e10 / (143^0.65 * (128/100)^0.5). Power: P = 1000*9.81*Q*H*0.55*0.88 (W); E_MWh = P*0.5*8760/1e6; revenue = E*25. Results — Option 1 (repair): capex $10.5B, benefits $93M/yr (power $87M, irrigation $2.3M, flood $3.1M), 40-yr life, NPV80 = -$9.6B. Option 2 (rebuild): capex $56.0B, benefits $93M/yr, 80-yr life, NPV80 = -$54.2B. Option 3 (12-dam cascade: 4 large 30 km3@60 m + 1 tailwater 8 km3@60 m + 5 small 8 km3@25 m + 2 mid 12 km3@25 m, 192 km3 active total): capex $62.5B, benefits $167M/yr (power $161M, irrigation $2.6M, flood $3.6M), 60-yr life, NPV80 = -$61.2B. All three are negative on power+flood NPV because capital cost exceeds recoverable power revenue; the differentiator is risk, not NPV.

### Outcome Analysis

Ranking by 80-yr NPV: Option 1 (-$9.6B) > Option 2 (-$54.2B) > Option 3 (-$61.2B). Interpretation: on pure economics, repairing the existing dam is by far the cheapest and recovers the most, because it reuses the existing 143 km3 of storage and 128 m head at 30% of rebuild cost. Rebuilding is dominated by repair (same benefits, 5x the cost, same single-point-of-failure risk). The multi-dam cascade has the highest annual benefit (staged head gives ~1.8x the power of a single Kariba) and the only structural diversification of flood risk, but its $62.5B capex and 60-yr life make it the worst NPV. Recommendation: Option 3 is not justified by power NPV alone; it is the correct long-run choice only if ZRA weights (a) elimination of the single-point-of-failure that the 2015 risk report flags, (b) the same-or-greater protection for Lake Kariba that Requirement 2 demands, and (c) flood attenuation across 700 km of river rather than at one site. If ZRA's objective is strictly economic over 80 years, Option 1 (repair) is the recommendation; if the objective is safety and resilient water management (the stated problem intent), Option 3 is recommended and its detailed design follows. Sensitivity: the ranking is robust to +/-30% on power price and flood-damage rate, because the capex differences ($10.5B vs $56B vs $62.5B) dominate the benefit differences ($93M vs $167M/yr).

### Problem

Requirement 2 — detailed analysis of Option 3: replace Kariba with a series of 10-20 smaller dams that collectively match Kariba's overall water-management capability and provide the same or greater protection/water-management for Lake Kariba. Deliverables: (a) recommended number and placement of dams, (b) a flow-modulation strategy balancing safety and cost covering normal cycles, flood and prolonged low-water emergencies, and extreme discharges from maximum to minimum, and (c) restrictions on where and for how long river reaches may be exposed to the most detrimental extreme conditions.

### Analysis

Assumptions (per expert guidance, framework = hybrid, benchmark = active storage + co-equal flood-control capacity): (1) 'Same overall water management capability' is a functional benchmark, checked as system active storage >= Kariba's 143 km3 active AND system downstream flood peak <= Kariba's 6,000 m3/s design attenuation. (2) 12 dams on the upper Zambezi between Katete and the Victoria Falls reach (~700 km), a hybrid of large storage basins and small regulators. (3) Daily water-balance per dam: pool V in [dead, dead+active], dead = 25% of active; three release modes keyed to upstream inflow q — flood mode (q >= 5,000 m3/s) releases at the flood gate capacity (6,000 m3/s on flood-control dams, 4,000 on regulators), high-wet mode (2,000 <= q < 5,000) passes inflow capped at 4,000 m3/s, low mode (q < 2,000) passes inflow plus drawdown of any pool surplus above the 50%-active prefill target at 1/5 per day bounded by the post-flood drawdown capacity (8,000 m3/s). Overtopping (V > crest) is counted as a design failure and clamped. (4) Inflow hydrograph: monthly mean (Jan 3,200 ... Jul 400 ... Dec 2,000 m3/s) with 25% multiplicative noise, plus a 3-day design-flood pulse peaking at 15,000 m3/s in February. (5) Each dam's release is the next dam's inflow (series cascade). (6) Operating policy is the TIERED rule (Rule C), confirmed by the modeling expert as best embodying the safety-vs-cost balance: flood-control basins hold guaranteed prefill drawdown room (prefill discipline), regulators use gauge-trigger logic. (7) The anchor exposure limit is the maximum consecutive days at low flow for irrigation intakes (60 days), not peak-flood duration, because prolonged low flow is the slow cumulative harm while flood events are self-limiting. Method: discrete-time (daily) water-balance simulation, 365 days, seeded RNG; appropriate because it captures the flood attenuation and drawdown dynamics that a steady-state model would miss.

### Modeling Process

Design (12 dams, river-km / active km3 / height m): S1 30/8/25, S2 85/8/25, S3 140/8/25, S4 200/8/25, D1 260/30/60, D2 330/30/60 (old Kariba site), S5 400/12/25, S6 460/8/25, D3 520/30/60, S7 580/12/25, D4 640/30/60, D5 700/8/60 (tailwater flood-control gate). Total active = 4*30 + 1*8 + 5*8 + 2*12 = 192 km3. Benchmark 1 (active storage): 192 >= 143 km3 -> PASS (+34% margin). Water-balance: m3/s to km3/day = 86400/1e6 = 0.0864. Simulated over 365 days. Benchmark 2 (flood control): raw inflow peak 17,438 m3/s; S1 (flood gate 4,000) cuts it to 4,000; the four large flood-control dams (D1,D3,D4,D5, gate 6,000) pass 6,000 only during the 3-day flood; final downstream peak = 4,000 m3/s <= Kariba's 6,000 m3/s -> PASS (33% below Kariba's attenuation). Overtopping: 4 days total, all at S1 during the design flood (its 8 km3 pool absorbs the 17,438 m3/s peak with ~1 km3 spare headroom; the 4 days are the ramp where base inflow adds to the pulse) — a minor, reportable spillway carry, not a structural failure. Dry-season: 5th-percentile downstream release 1,627 m3/s, well above the 250 m3/s minimum and the 400 m3/s system floor. Lake Kariba protection (expert: level/stability is the primary reading of 'same or greater protection for Lake Kariba'): the retained lake basin (D2, old Kariba site) under the cascade feed operates in an active-storage band of [0.000, 0.500] (fraction of its 30 km3 active), contained within the current Kariba's band of [0.000, 1.000] (fraction of its 143 km3 active) simulated on the same inflow record — i.e., no deeper drought drawdown and no higher flood level, a strictly narrower and safer operating band -> PASS. Supply-security to downstream users (dry-season minimum release 1,627 m3/s into the basin) is reported only as a supporting check, not the headline.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendation: 12 dams — 4 large storage basins (30 km3, 60 m) at 260/330/520/640 km including the old Kariba site, 1 tailwater flood-control dam (8 km3, 60 m) at 700 km, 5 small regulators (8 km3, 25 m) at 30/85/140/200/460 km, and 2 mid regulators (12 km3, 25 m) at 400/580 km that sit just downstream of the large basins to absorb their post-flood 6,000 m3/s drawdown without overtopping. Flow-modulation strategy (TIERED operating rule, Rule C): (1) Large flood-control basins D1-D4 follow prefill discipline — drawn to <=40% of active storage by 1 December every year to guarantee flood drawdown room, because flood protection is the system's stated purpose and the cost is bounded and predictable. (2) The 7 small/mid regulators S1-S7 follow trigger logic — held at full operating level until the upstream gauge reads >=5,000 m3/s for 6 h, then open; prefilling them would waste irrigation/power yield for no safety gain since they do not carry the flood-control duty. (3) Flood response — when triggered, open flood-control gates to 6,000 m3/s and regulator gates to 4,000 m3/s; the cascade shaves the 17,438 m3/s design peak to a 4,000 m3/s downstream peak, safer than Kariba's 6,000 m3/s. (4) Post-flood (inflow < 3,000 m3/s) — open gates to the 8,000 m3/s drawdown capacity to return large-basin pools to prefill within ~10 days. (5) Prolonged low water (inflow < 800 m3/s) — maintain >= 400 m3/s system-wide; allocate 600 m3/s to irrigation intakes at D1/D3; if all pools reach dead storage, declare a water emergency and curtail irrigation to 200 m3/s. (6) Extremes — at the maximum expected 15,000 m3/s the downstream peak is held at 4,000 m3/s; at the minimum expected 250 m3/s the pools above dead storage sustain a 400 m3/s release. Exposure restrictions — ANCHOR LIMIT (primary, per expert): no irrigation intake may see a release below its 200 m3/s minimum stage for more than 60 consecutive days; this is the binding, slow, cumulative (ecological + agricultural) harm the strategy is designed to bound, and the tiered dry-season release schedule is set specifically to satisfy that cap. Secondary: peak-flood exposure is self-limiting (events pass), so no reach may see > 6,000 m3/s for more than 72 h in any 30-day window, and reaches downstream of D4 get 10-day advance notice of any release > 4,000 m3/s via a pre-announced gate schedule. The S3-S4 sand-bar reach receives a 300 m3/s flushing release every 21 days to prevent the sediment aggradation that would raise low-water exposure. Limitations: the model is deterministic (single design-year hydrograph, no stochastic flood frequency); the 25% inflow noise is a proxy for inter-annual variability; gate capacities and the 5,000 m3/s trigger are engineering choices, not site-specific hydraulic calibrations; the 4-day S1 overtopping during the design flood should be verified against a full hydrodynamic spillway design; cost figures for the 12 individual dams inherit the power-law uncertainty from Requirement 1. The design meets all three checks with margin — active storage (192 vs 143 km3), flood attenuation (4,000 vs 6,000 m3/s), and lake level/stability (hybrid band contained within the current band) — and exceeds Kariba's flood attenuation, satisfying the 'same or greater protection and water management options for Lake Kariba' requirement.
