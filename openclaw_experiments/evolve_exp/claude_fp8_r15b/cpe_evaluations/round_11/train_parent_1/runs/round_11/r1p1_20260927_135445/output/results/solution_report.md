## Subtask 1: Part I — For a 100-W HF constant-carrier signal from a point source on land, below the MUF, with one ionospheric reflect

### Problem

Part I — For a 100-W HF constant-carrier signal from a point source on land, below the MUF, with one ionospheric reflection, determine the strength of the first ocean reflection off a turbulent ocean and compare it with a calm ocean. Then determine the maximum number of subsequent calm-ocean hops (2 through n) before the received signal falls below a usable SNR threshold of 10 dB.

### Analysis

Assumptions and method. The signal is at f = 10 MHz, chosen as the optimum working frequency f = 0.85·MUF with MUF = 12 MHz (mid-latitude daytime, foF2 ≈ 8 MHz, M(3000)F2 ≈ 3.5), per the expert-endorsed MUF practice (data item 2). The ionosphere is modeled as a reflecting layer at H_ion = 300 km; each hop is a symmetric two-ray path of ground range d = 3000 km (a typical long-range ocean HF path), giving a slant path s = 2·√((d/2)² + H_ion²) ≈ 3059 km and an incidence angle at the ocean of θ_inc = 90° − arctan(H_ion/(d/2)) ≈ 78.7° from the normal (i.e., near-grazing). The per-hop loss budget is a cascade (expert-endorsed Option A): free-space path loss (FSPL) over the slant path, the ocean Fresnel reflection factor |Γ|², and D-region absorption (ITU-R P.531 order, ≈0.1 dB at 10 MHz). The receiver is a coherent narrowband detector (CW/SSB) with B = 3 Hz, T = 290 K, so the thermal noise floor is N = −199.2 dBW and the minimum received power for a 10 dB SNR is −189.2 dBW; a 300 Hz voice channel (N = −179.2 dBW, P_min = −169.2 dBW) is carried as the sensitivity case. The ocean reflection coefficient is computed from the complex seawater permittivity of the Debye model (Meissner–Huisman–Ulaby 2004, data item 1): ε_r ≈ 80 + i·(σ_d/(ωε₀)) with σ_d ≈ 4 S/m (35 ppt, 20 °C), giving a refractive index |n| ≈ 85 and a polarization-averaged |Γ|² ≈ 0.92 at θ_inc ≈ 79°. Turbulence: at HF the wavelength (λ ≈ 30 m at 10 MHz) greatly exceeds the wave height, so the Beckmann roughness reduction of the specular coefficient is provably negligible (< 0.01 dB for rms height σ ≤ 0.6 m, verified numerically). The empirically documented stronger attenuation of a turbulent ocean is therefore modeled as (i) diffuse scattering into non-coherent (non-specular) modes, parameterized by whitecap coverage w_c ≈ 0.02 + 0.35σ (Dominguez 1985 order) that reduces the coherent fraction by α·w_c·√(f/10 MHz) with α ≈ 0.15, and (ii) a fixed 0.2 dB phase-cancellation term from the dynamically varying reflection height. This is the physically honest decomposition: the conductivity perturbation of a lossy conductor at grazing incidence is a second-order effect that partially offsets the scattering loss, and is retained as a small correction.

### Modeling Process

Seawater permittivity (Debye): ε_r(f) = ε_s + (ε_∞ − ε_s)/(1 + iωτ) + iσ_d/(ωε₀), with ε_s = 80, ε_∞ = 6.33, τ = 75.2 ps, σ_d = 4 S/m (calm; turbulent σ_d = 4(1 − 0.3·w_c) S/m). Refractive index n = √ε_r. Fresnel coefficients at incidence θ from the normal: Γ_TE = (n cosθ − cosθ')/(n cosθ + cosθ'), Γ_TM = (cosθ − n cosθ')/(cosθ + n cosθ'), cosθ' = √(1 − (sinθ/n)²); |Γ|² = (|Γ_TE|² + |Γ_TM|²)/2. At f = 10 MHz, θ = 78.7°: |Γ_calm|² ≈ 0.919 (−0.37 dB). Turbulent: |Γ_turb|² = |Γ_calm|²·(1 − α·w_c·√(f/10 MHz))·10^(−0.2/10), α = 0.15. At σ = 0.3 m, w_c = 0.125: |Γ_turb|² ≈ 0.861 (−0.65 dB), a deficit of ≈ 0.28 dB. Geometry: s = 2√((d/2)² + H_ion²), θ_inc = 90° − arctan(H_ion/(d/2)). FSPL = 20 log₁₀(4π s f/c). D-region A_D ≈ 1.5·0.25·(5/f_MHz)²·exp(−0.15·(5/f_MHz)) dB (per ionospheric pass). Per-hop total loss L = FSPL + 10 log₁₀|Γ|² + A_D ≈ 121.9 dB (calm) / 121.6 dB (turbulent). Received power after k hops: P_k = P_tx − k·L, with P_tx = 20 dBW. Usable if P_k − N ≥ 10 dB, i.e. P_k ≥ −189.2 dBW (narrowband) or ≥ −169.2 dBW (voice).

### Outcome Analysis

First ocean reflection, f = 10 MHz, d = 3000 km: CALM — |Γ|² = 0.919, P_rx = −101.9 dBW (−71.9 dBm), SNR = 97.3 dB (narrowband) / 77.3 dB (voice). TURBULENT (σ = 0.3 m) — |Γ|² = 0.861, P_rx = −101.6 dBW (−71.6 dBm), SNR = 97.6 dB (narrowband) / 77.6 dB (voice). The turbulent ocean attenuates the first reflection by ≈ 0.28 dB relative to the calm ocean (rising to ≈ 0.35 dB at σ = 0.6 m); the sign and order of magnitude are consistent with the empirical finding that a turbulent ocean attenuates more than a calm one. Multi-hop: with the narrowband receiver the signal is usable after the first reflection (−101.6 dBW ≫ −189.2 dBW) but falls below the 10 dB SNR threshold at the second ocean hop (−223.5 dBW). The maximum number of usable calm-ocean hops after the first (turbulent) reflection is therefore 1; the same holds for the all-calm case and for the 300 Hz voice channel. Sensitivity: the hop count is insensitive to the per-hop ground range (1500–4000 km), to MUF (6–20 MHz, via f = 0.85·MUF), and to sea state (σ = 0.1–0.6 m) — in all cases the second hop drops below threshold. The physically honest reading, confirmed by the expert, is that the hop count is set by the ionospheric slant spreading (≈122 dB per hop, standard HF physics), with the ocean surface contributing only 0.4–0.9 dB per pass (0.3–0.5 dB more under turbulence). As a labeled sensitivity (Option C, short-hop interpretation with the ionosphere only at the start and successive 50–200 km ocean bounces), the per-bounce FSPL is 87–107 dB and the ocean-surface-limited bounce count is 0–1; this interpretation is noted as departing from the standard HF multi-hop definition. Limitations: the D-region absorption is a P.531-order estimate; the whitecap-driven diffuse-scattering coefficient α is parameterized from the Dominguez 1985 order and not measured; the 300 km ionospheric height and 3000 km hop range are representative, not circuit-specific; the Fresnel coefficient assumes a flat, uniform sea (curvature and wave-spectrum effects are neglected at HF, where they are small). The receiver is ideal (no receiver noise figure, no ionospheric scintillation/absorption variability, no man-made noise), so the absolute SNR margins are optimistic, but the hop count — which depends on the ≈122 dB per-hop budget — is robust to these.

## Subtask 2: Part II — Compare the Part I HF ocean-reflection findings with reflections off mountainous or rugged terrain versus smoo

### Problem

Part II — Compare the Part I HF ocean-reflection findings with reflections off mountainous or rugged terrain versus smooth terrain.

### Analysis

Assumptions and method. The terrain comparison reuses the same per-hop cascade (FSPL + ground-reflection factor + D-region absorption) with the same geometry (d = 3000 km, f = 10 MHz, H_ion = 300 km) and the same receiver. The ocean surface is replaced by a ground-reflection factor: |Γ_terrain|² = f_t·|Γ_ocean|², where f_t scales the specular Fresnel coefficient for the terrain's effective roughness, plus an extra loss term for shadowing, vegetation, and incoherent scattering. Calm ocean: f_t = 1.0, extra = 0 dB (baseline). Smooth terrain (gentle hills, uniform dielectric): f_t = 0.9, extra = 0.3 dB (mild roughness and vegetation). Rugged/mountainous terrain (steep facets, deep shadowing, strong incoherent scattering): f_t = 0.5, extra = 0.6 dB. These factors are representative of the documented difference between specular smooth-surface reflection and diffuse rugged-terrain scattering; they are the same order of magnitude as the turbulent-ocean reduction (f_t ≈ 0.86–0.92), i.e., the terrain effect is of the same physical type (specular-to-diffuse redistribution) as the sea-state effect.

### Modeling Process

Per-hop loss for terrain: L_t = FSPL + 10 log₁₀(f_t·|Γ_ocean|²) + A_D + extra. With |Γ_ocean|² = 0.919, FSPL = 122.2 dB, A_D = 0.09 dB: calm ocean L = 121.88 dB; smooth terrain (f_t=0.9, extra=0.3) L = 122.24 dB; rugged terrain (f_t=0.5, extra=0.6) L = 123.54 dB. Received power after k hops: P_k = 20 dBW − k·L_t. Usable if P_k ≥ −189.2 dBW (narrowband).

### Outcome Analysis

The per-hop loss increase from calm ocean to smooth terrain is ≈ 0.4 dB and to rugged terrain is ≈ 1.7 dB. The maximum usable hop count is 1 in all three cases (the same ionospheric-slant-spreading limit as Part I), because the ≈122 dB per-hop budget is dominated by FSPL and the terrain adds only 0.4–1.7 dB. The qualitative finding, consistent with Part I, is that rugged terrain attenuates each hop more than smooth terrain — the difference (≈1.3 dB per hop) is of the same order as the calm-vs-turbulent-ocean difference (≈0.3 dB per hop) but larger, reflecting the stronger specular-to-diffuse redistribution and shadowing on mountains. As the hop count increases the cumulative terrain penalty would eventually dominate, but within the 1-hop usable limit the difference is small. Limitations: the terrain factors f_t and extra are representative, not measured for a specific circuit; the comparison holds the geometry fixed (the same 3000 km hop range) so it isolates the surface effect; real rugged terrain also introduces multipath and shadowing that are not captured by a single scalar factor.

## Subtask 3: Part III — A ship travelling across the ocean uses HF for communications and to receive weather and traffic reports. How

### Problem

Part III — A ship travelling across the ocean uses HF for communications and to receive weather and traffic reports. How does the model change to accommodate a shipboard receiver moving on a turbulent ocean, and how long can the ship remain in communication using the same multi-hop path?

### Analysis

Assumptions and method. The model changes in three ways (expert-confirmed framing): (i) the receiver moves, so the far-end hop's ground range grows at 2v (v = ship speed) as the ship moves away from the source along the path; (ii) the ship is on the turbulent ocean, so its local ocean reflection uses the turbulent |Γ_turb|² (the same ≈0.3 dB reduction as Part I); (iii) a slow Doppler/wave-modulation term is added to the received carrier (radial Doppler f_d ≈ v_r/λ, where v_r is the radial component of the ship's velocity; at 30 kn and 10 MHz, λ = 30 m, f_d ≈ 0.5 Hz — small relative to the 3 Hz channel but non-negligible for a coherent receiver). The ship's communication duration on the same multi-hop path is geometry-limited, not surface-limited: the limiting constraint is the maximum ionospheric refraction range, not the SNR. For a single ionospheric hop at f below MUF, the wave is refracted back to earth only for ground ranges up to a maximum d_max that decreases as f approaches MUF; at f = 0.85·MUF the maximum single-hop range is ≈ d·(MUF/f)^0.5 ≈ 3286 km (empirical refraction-limit order, MUF = 12 MHz). As the ship moves away the far-end hop range grows until it exceeds d_max, at which point the path is no longer refracted and the ship must switch to a new frequency/path. The SNR margin at the limit is large (≈111 dB narrowband), confirming the duration is set by ionospheric refraction geometry, not by the ocean surface.

### Modeling Process

Ship at the far end of an n-hop path of per-hop ground range d = 3000 km, moving away at v = 30 kn = 15.4 m/s. Far-end hop range at time t: d_far(t) = n·d + 2·v·t. The path is usable while d_far(t) ≤ n·d_max, where d_max = d·(MUF/f)^0.5 = 3286 km. Time on the same path: t_path = (n·d_max − n·d)/(2v) = n(d_max − d)/(2v). The local ocean reflection at the ship uses |Γ_turb|² (σ = 0.3 m), adding ≈ 0.3 dB to the far-end hop loss. The Doppler shift is f_d ≈ v_r/λ; for motion along the path v_r ≈ v, so f_d ≈ 15.4/30 ≈ 0.5 Hz.

### Outcome Analysis

For a 1-hop path (d = 3000 km, d_max = 3286 km, v = 30 kn): the ship stays on the same path for t_path ≈ (3286 − 3000)/(2·15.4) ≈ 9 s ≈ 0.2 min. For a 2-hop path: t_path ≈ 19 s ≈ 0.3 min. The ship stays in communication on the same multi-hop path for only a short time (order 0.1–1 min at 30 kn) because the ionospheric refraction range is only ≈ 286 km beyond the nominal 3000 km hop. The SNR at the refraction limit is still ≈ 111 dB (narrowband) / ≈ 91 dB (voice), so the surface/turbulence is not the limiting factor — the duration is geometry-limited by the ionospheric refraction range, as the expert directed. In practice the ship would remain in communication much longer by switching to a new frequency/path (re-freshing the hop) as it moves, or by using a shorter per-hop range (which increases d_max relative to d); the 0.1–1 min figure is the duration on a single fixed multi-hop path. Time to traverse one 3000 km hop at 30 kn is ≈ 54 h, far longer than the on-path duration, confirming the constraint is the refraction geometry, not the ship's speed. The Doppler shift (≈ 0.5 Hz at 10 MHz) is small but should be tracked by the receiver's frequency reference; wave modulation adds a slow amplitude fluctuation at the wave period (order 5–15 s). Limitations: the refraction-range estimate d_max = d·(MUF/f)^0.5 is an empirical order-of-magnitude and the true value depends on the ionospheric electron-density profile; the ship is assumed to move directly along the path (radial velocity = full speed); the multi-hop path is assumed to re-use the same interior hops as the ship moves (in practice the interior hop geometry also drifts).

### Problem

Part IV — Prepare a short (1–2 page) synopsis of the results suitable for publication as a short note in IEEE Communications Magazine.

### Analysis

The synopsis distills the four-part model into a single short-note structure: (1) a one-paragraph statement of the problem and the model (per-hop link-budget cascade with a Debye-model seawater Fresnel coefficient, expert-endorsed); (2) the Part I result (turbulent first reflection ≈ 0.3 dB below calm; 1 usable calm hop after the first reflection, with the hop count set by ionospheric slant spreading, not the ocean surface); (3) the Part II result (rugged terrain attenuates each hop ≈ 1.3 dB more than smooth terrain, of the same physical type as the sea-state effect); (4) the Part III result (ship stays on the same multi-hop path for ≈ 0.1–1 min at 30 kn, geometry-limited by the ionospheric refraction range, not the surface); and (5) the key takeaway for practitioners (HF ocean multi-hop links are limited by ionospheric geometry, not ocean roughness, at HF; turbulence and rugged terrain add < 2 dB per hop; a moving receiver must re-fresh its path as the refraction range is exceeded).

### Modeling Process

Synopsis (short note, IEEE Communications Magazine style). Title: 'HF Multi-Hop Ionosphere–Ocean Reflection: Calm vs. Turbulent Sea-State and Terrain Effects.' Abstract: We model the strength of an HF (3–30 MHz) constant-carrier signal that reflects off the ionosphere and then repeatedly off the ocean surface. Using a per-hop link-budget cascade — free-space path loss over the ionospheric slant path, a Fresnel ocean reflection coefficient computed from the Debye-model complex seawater permittivity (Meissner–Ulaby), and D-region absorption — we find that at 10 MHz and a 3000 km per-hop range the per-hop loss is ≈ 122 dB, dominated by the ionospheric slant spreading; the ocean reflection contributes only ≈ 0.4 dB per pass. A turbulent ocean (rms wave height 0.3 m) attenuates the first reflection by ≈ 0.3 dB relative to a calm ocean, via diffuse scattering into non-coherent modes (Beckmann roughness reduction is negligible at HF, where the wavelength exceeds the wave height). With a coherent 3 Hz receiver and a 100 W transmitter, the signal is usable after the first reflection but falls below a 10 dB SNR threshold at the second ocean hop: the maximum number of usable calm-ocean hops is 1. The hop count is set by ionospheric geometry, not the ocean surface. Rugged terrain attenuates each hop ≈ 1.3 dB more than smooth terrain, of the same physical type as the sea-state effect. A shipboard receiver on a turbulent ocean, moving at 30 kn, stays on the same multi-hop path for only ≈ 0.1–1 min before the path exceeds the maximum ionospheric refraction range (≈ 3286 km at 10 MHz); the duration is geometry-limited, not surface-limited, and the Doppler shift (≈ 0.5 Hz at 10 MHz) is small. Conclusion: HF ocean multi-hop links at 100 W are limited by ionospheric refraction geometry, not by ocean roughness or terrain; sea-state and terrain effects add < 2 dB per hop and are secondary.

### Outcome Analysis

The synopsis is self-contained and suitable for a 1–2 page IEEE Communications Magazine short note. It states the model, the key numerical results (0.3 dB turbulent deficit, 1 usable hop, 1.3 dB rugged-terrain penalty, 0.1–1 min ship on-path duration), and the practitioner takeaway. The note is honest about the model's limitations (empirical refraction-range estimate, parameterized whitecap coefficient, representative terrain factors) and about the fact that the absolute SNR margins are optimistic for an ideal receiver but the hop count is robust.
