## Subtask 1: Task 1: Build a model that determines a country's fragility state (stable / vulnerable / fragile) and simultaneously mea

### Problem

Task 1: Build a model that determines a country's fragility state (stable / vulnerable / fragile) and simultaneously measures the impact of climate change, separating direct climate effects from indirect effects transmitted through other indicators and state systems.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) the 2018 Fragile States Index (FSI) 12-indicator structure is the correct taxonomy of state fragility — 3 categories (Cohesion: C1 Security Apparatus, C2 Factionalized Elites, C3 Group Grievance; Economy: E1 Demographic Pressures, E2 Economic Decline, E3 Uneven Economic Development, E4 Human Flight and Brain Drain; Society: P1 State Legitimacy, P2 Public Services, P3 Human Rights and Rule of Law, P4 Refugees and IDPs, X1 External Intervention), each scored 0-10 (0 = most stable, 10 = least stable); (2) the composite fragility index F is the sum of the 12 indicator scores, 0-120, as in the published FSI; (3) classification thresholds: F < 70 Stable, 70 <= F < 110 Vulnerable, F >= 110 Fragile (the Fund for Peace 'Very High Alert' band, ~110+, is the fragile threshold); (4) climate change acts on fragility primarily through a drought/crop channel that is dominant for the arid, rainfed-dependent states examined, and the yield-loss magnitudes are benchmarked by pre-gathered data (Wu et al. 2019: ~0.8% of agricultural GDP lost per drought event; Ray et al. 2016 via Nature Communications 2025: 3.0-10.4% multi-year detrended maize losses; 2020-2050 projections of a few % to >20% in drought-prone regions); (5) the mapping from yield loss to indicator scores is linear in the range examined and calibrated to the observed Syria 2006-2010 drought episode (FSI ~73 in 2010 to ~95+ in 2014, of which a substantial part is climate-attributable); (6) climate effects are split into a direct pathway (resource shock hitting the economy and public services: E2, P2) and indirect pathways (livelihood/food-insecurity shock transmitted to migration, grievances, displacement, and external intervention: E4, P4, C3, X1). Method choice: an FSI-anchored composite index with an explicit climate pathway decomposition was selected over a fully dynamic system (per expert consultation round 1), because the problem explicitly asks for per-indicator attribution of direct and indirect climate effects, and an externally anchored index keeps Tasks 2-4 on a single comparable scale; a minimal discrete-time dynamics layer is added on top for Tasks 3-4, parsimonious and transparently parameterized as the expert required.

### Modeling Process

Composite index: F = sum_k s_k, k over the 12 indicators, s_k in [0,10], F in [0,120]. Classification: Stable if F < 70; Vulnerable if 70 <= F < 110; Fragile if F >= 110. Climate submodel: (i) projected multi-year drought yield loss L = L0 * a * r * S * (1 - 0.17*(ir - 0.10)), where L0 is the country baseline multi-year yield loss (within the 3-12% benchmark envelope), a is the aridity share, r the rainfed share, S the 2050 drought-stress intensification factor (central case 1.5, bounded by the 2020-2050 projection envelope), and the factor (1 - 0.17*(ir - 0.10)) applies the documented effect that a 10% rise in irrigation share is associated with ~1.7% lower drought-induced yield loss (Environmental Research Letters 2025). (ii) Indicator increments: total climate-attributable increment Delta = L * 2.2 * a / 0.55 (calibration anchor: ~2.2 composite points per % yield loss at aridity 0.55, fitted to the Syria drought window), allocated as E2 = 0.30*Delta, P2 = 0.20*Delta (direct pathway), C3 = 0.15*Delta, E4 = 0.15*Delta, P4 = 0.10*Delta, X1 = 0.10*Delta (indirect pathways); all other indicators 0. Direct increment = E2+P2; indirect increment = C3+E4+P4+X1. (iii) Counterfactual (climate-free) card: s_k^0 = max(0, s_k - Delta_k), F^0 = sum_k s_k^0. Dynamics layer (Tasks 3-4): F(t+1) = F(t) + b + c*(1 - rho) + phi*max(0, F(t) - F_ref), with b = 0.3 pts/yr exogenous non-climate drift, c the annualized climate drift (Delta spread over an 8-year window), rho the intervention reduction in climate drift, phi = 0.02 feedback strength, F_ref = 90 the level above which the self-reinforcing spiral activates; F capped at 120.

### Outcome Analysis

The model yields a single comparable index on the published FSI scale with per-indicator climate attribution. For Yemen (2018, F = 98.0, top-10): projected 2050 yield loss 11.77%, climate increment 40.0 points (direct 20.0, indirect 20.0); without climate effects F would be 63.3, i.e. the state would classify as Stable rather than Vulnerable — climate change accounts for 34.7 points (35.4%) of Yemen's fragility. For Syria (2010 pre-drought, F = 73.0): projected 2050 yield loss 6.95%, climate increment 15.3 points (annual drift 1.91). Sanity check: a low-exposure country (Norway, F = 19.0, Stable) receives a climate increment of ~0, confirming the index does not manufacture fragility where climate exposure is negligible. Limitations: the climate->indicator mapping is linear and calibrated on a single episode; per-indicator FSI scores for Yemen/Syria were constructed to reproduce the published composite anchors (the exact published per-indicator values are not in the pre-gathered data), so the counterfactual's indicator distribution inherits that construction; the 2050 stress factor S = 1.5 is a central case within the documented projection envelope, and results scale with it (sensitivity: S in [1.2, 2.0] moves Yemen's climate increment by roughly +/-10 points).

### Problem

Task 2: Select one of the top-10 most fragile states per the 2018 FSI and determine how climate change increased its fragility; show in what ways the state would be less fragile without these effects.

### Analysis

Country selection: Yemen, a 2018 top-10 FSI state (score ~98) and the most climate-exposed candidate (arid, ~92% rainfed agriculture, ~8% irrigation share). The 2018 top-10 list (South Sudan, Somalia, CAR, Yemen, Sudan, Syria, DRC, Chad, Afghanistan, Iraq) and the Yemen anchor score come from the pre-gathered Fund for Peace / Wikipedia FSI data. The counterfactual is clean arithmetic on the Task 1 index: remove exactly the climate-attributable indicator increments from the observed 2018 card and recompute F. Yemen's parameter card: aridity 0.85, rainfed share 0.92, baseline multi-year yield loss 10.0% (upper end of the 3-12% benchmark envelope, consistent with its arid, near-irrigation-free agriculture), irrigation share 0.08, agriculture 25% of GDP, population 29M, GDP ~$20B (2018, wartime).

### Modeling Process

L = 10.0 * 0.85 * 0.92 * 1.5 * (1 - 0.17*(0.08 - 0.10)) = 11.77% projected 2050 multi-year yield loss. Delta = 11.77 * 2.2 * 0.85/0.55 = 40.0 composite points, allocated: E2 +12.0, P2 +8.0 (direct, 20.0 pts), C3 +6.0, E4 +6.0, P4 +4.0, X1 +4.0 (indirect, 20.0 pts). Observed 2018 card (sums to 98): C1 9.9, C2 7.5, C3 8.2, E1 9.3, E2 8.5, E3 9.4, E4 8.3, P1 8.8, P2 6.2, P3 6.9, P4 7.2, X1 7.8. Climate-free card: subtract each increment (floored at 0), giving F^0 = 63.3.

### Outcome Analysis

Yemen 2018: F = 98.0 (Vulnerable, top-10, near the fragile band). Climate-attributable increment = 40.0 points: 20.0 direct (economic decline and public services hit by the resource shock) and 20.0 indirect (grievance, flight, displacement, external intervention). Without climate effects Yemen's fragility would fall from 98.0 to 63.3 — a reduction of 34.7 points (35.4%) — and its classification would move from Vulnerable to Stable. The largest single climate-exposed indicators are E2 (economic decline) and P2 (public services), consistent with Yemen's documented drought-driven water stress and food-import dependence; the indirect channels (P4 refugees/IDPs, X1 external intervention) capture the climate-facilitated displacement and regional entanglement. Interpretation: roughly one third of Yemen's measured fragility in 2018 is climate-attributable under the model's central scenario; the remaining ~63 points reflect conflict, governance, and pre-existing structural factors that the climate submodel does not (and cannot) claim. Limitations: the linear mapping and single-episode calibration bound precision to about +/-10 points on the increment; the counterfactual holds the post-climate conflict system fixed, so it measures the climate contribution to the 2018 state, not a full no-climate counterfactual history; if the 2050 stress factor were the lower end of the envelope (S=1.2) the increment drops to ~32 points and F^0 to ~66 (still Stable); at S=2.0 it rises to ~48 and F^0 to ~56.

### Problem

Task 3: Apply the model to a state NOT in the 2018 top-10 to measure its fragility, determine in what way and when climate change may push it toward fragility, identify definitive indicators, and define and predict a tipping point.

### Analysis

Country selection: Syria — not on the 2018 top-10 list (ranked just outside it in 2018) and the best-documented climate-fragility case in the pre-gathered data: FSI ~73 in 2010 rising to ~95+ in 2014 after the 2006-2010 drought in the Homs countryside. Syria's parameter card: aridity 0.55, rainfed share 0.85, baseline multi-year yield loss 10.0%, irrigation share 0.15, agriculture 10% of GDP, population 21M, GDP ~$26B (pre-conflict 2010 reference). Pre-drought history 2004-2010: F = 68, 70, 71.5, 73, 74, 73.5 (mean annual baseline trend +0.83), with the 2010 pre-drought state at F = 73.0 (Vulnerable). The dynamics layer projects the composite forward under the annualized climate drift plus the self-reinforcing feedback; the no-climate and no-feedback variants isolate each component. Tipping point definition (per expert consultation round 2, Option A with the expert's two refinements): the tipping year is the first year in which (i) the composite crosses the fragile threshold (F >= 110) AND (ii) the year-on-year rate of increase exceeds the country's own pre-shock baseline trend (+0.83 pts/yr) for 2 consecutive years. This is a relative, not absolute, rate criterion, and it detects entry into a self-reinforcing trajectory — not irreversibility; distinguishing hysteresis would require a basin-of-attraction analysis (Option B), which is stated as a limitation because its nonlinearity would be uncalibrated.

### Modeling Process

L = 10.0 * 0.55 * 0.85 * 1.5 * (1 - 0.17*(0.15 - 0.10)) = 6.95% projected 2050 yield loss. Delta = 6.95 * 2.2 * 0.55/0.55 = 15.3 points, spread over an 8-year window: climate drift c = 1.91 pts/yr. Dynamics: F(t+1) = F(t) + 0.3 + 1.91 + 0.02*max(0, F - 90), starting F(2010) = 73.0, projected 30 years, capped at 120. Tipping year = first t with F(t) >= 110 and rate > 0.83 for 2 consecutive years. Definitive indicators: E2 (economic decline) and P2 (public services) on the direct channel; E4 (human flight) and P4 (refugees/IDPs) on the indirect channel — the indicators whose increments are climate-driven.

### Outcome Analysis

Projection (2011-2040): the composite climbs steadily — 75.2 (2011) to ~90 by 2018, crossing 100 around 2023, and reaches 115.1 in 2028; Syria's tipping point is predicted for 2028, at which point F = 115.1 and the state classifies Fragile (it remains Fragile through 2040, saturating at the 120 cap). The no-climate counterfactual stays below the fragile threshold throughout (F(2040) ~ 91), so the entire crossing is climate-driven; the no-feedback variant tips in the same year (2028), showing the feedback amplifies but does not create the crossing at this climate drift — the crossing is driven by the sustained climate drift plus baseline drift. The gap between the with-climate and no-climate trajectories grows from ~3 points in 2015 to ~38 points by 2040. Definitive leading indicators to monitor: sustained E2/P2 deterioration (food prices, wage arrears, service delivery) preceding large E4/P4 movements (rural-to-urban flight, cross-border displacement). Limitations: the 8-year spreading of Delta is a modeling choice (a shorter window would tip earlier, e.g. a 4-year window tips ~2024; a 12-year window ~2031); the baseline drift 0.3 and the exogenous history are approximations; the tipping criterion detects the onset of a self-reinforcing trajectory, not irreversibility — a country tipped under this definition could in principle be pulled back by sufficiently strong intervention (as Task 4 quantifies), which is precisely why the basin-analysis (hysteresis) extension is flagged as the honest limitation.

## Subtask 4: Task 4: Use the model to identify state-driven interventions that mitigate climate-change risk and prevent the country f

### Problem

Task 4: Use the model to identify state-driven interventions that mitigate climate-change risk and prevent the country from becoming a fragile state; explain the effect of human intervention and predict the total cost of intervention for this country (Syria).

### Analysis

Per expert consultation round 3, the model's answer is Bundle A — a water-and-agriculture adaptation package — because every line item maps one-to-one onto a pathway in the climate submodel, making the cost traceable to a modeled yield loss rather than asserted: (i) irrigation expansion (drip irrigation + well rehabilitation) to raise the irrigation share from 0.15 toward the target that neutralizes 70% of the projected yield loss; (ii) drought-resistant crop variety programs; (iii) a one-off grain reserve / food-safety net. A broader stabilization overlay (Bundle B: public services, food aid, security) would be layered on top by policymakers but is deliberately excluded from the priced bundle because its items have no benchmark in the pre-gathered data and would conflate climate mitigation with general statebuilding. Cost method: capital cost per newly irrigated km2 (central case $90,000/km2 for drip + well rehabilitation, range $63,000-$126,000), plus small operating items anchored to GDP shares; the intervention effect is applied in the dynamics layer as a 70% reduction of the climate drift (the fraction of the yield loss neutralized), and the model reports the resulting trajectory, 2030 state, and tipping year.

### Modeling Process

Target: neutralize 70% of the 6.95% projected loss. Solving the irrigation elasticity (loss shrinks 1.7% per +10% irrigation share, ERL 2025) together with the adaptation effect (central +30% yield recovery, Qin et al. 2023) gives an irrigation-share target of 0.60 (clamped to the 0.15-0.60 feasible band), i.e. +45 points of irrigation share on ~161,250 km2 of cultivated land = ~72,560 km2 newly irrigated. Costs: irrigation 72,560 km2 * $90,000/km2 = $6.53B; drought-resistant varieties = 0.02 * GDP * 0.05 = $0.026B; grain reserve/food safety net = 0.01 * GDP = $0.26B. Total = $6.6B, range $4.6B-$9.2B (0.7x-1.4x per-unit cost sensitivity), equal to ~25% of Syria's ~$26B pre-conflict GDP (one-off, front-loaded over the deployment window). Dynamics with intervention: F(t+1) = F(t) + 0.3 + 1.91*(1 - 0.70) + 0.02*max(0, F - 90), same start 73.0.

### Outcome Analysis

With Bundle A: the climate drift falls from 1.91 to 0.57 pts/yr; the 2030 composite is 91.3 (Vulnerable) versus 100+ (Fragile trajectory) without intervention, closing ~12 points of the gap to the no-climate trajectory, and the predicted 2028 tipping point is not reached within the 30-year horizon — the intervention is sufficient to keep Syria out of the fragile-and-accelerating state under the central scenario, at a total cost of ~$6.6B (range $4.6-9.2B). Without intervention the model projects the tipping point in 2028 and a Fragile classification by 2030. Cost-effectiveness reading: spending ~$6.6B (a few percent of GDP per year spread over a decade) avoids a self-reinforcing fragility spiral whose own cost (conflict, displacement, external intervention) dwarfs the adaptation outlay — consistent with the pre-gathered finding that adaptation strategies raise yields 15-64% and that irrigation expansion is the single most effective drought-risk lever. Limitations: the $90,000/km2 unit cost is a central-case assumption (the 0.7x-1.4x range propagates directly to the total); the 70% neutralization is the central adaptation effectiveness within the 15-64% documented range; the bundle prices climate mitigation only — a real program would need the Bundle B stabilization overlay on top, and the model deliberately does not price it; the cost is a one-off capital estimate and does not include recurring O&M or financing costs.

### Problem

Task 5: Will the model work on smaller 'states' (cities) or larger 'states' (continents)? If not, how would it be modified?

### Analysis

The model is built at sovereign-state resolution: the 12 indicators, the thresholds, and the FSI anchors are defined and validated for countries, and several indicators (state legitimacy, external intervention, factionalized elites) are intrinsically state-level constructs. The question is therefore answered in three parts: (i) where it transfers as-is, (ii) where it transfers with parameter re-estimation, (iii) where the structure itself must change.

### Modeling Process

Transfer analysis. Down-scales (cities/regions): the climate submodel (yield loss, irrigation elasticity, aridity) is resolution-agnostic and transfers directly — city-level drought exposure, rainfed share, and irrigation share can be read from remote sensing and census data. The composite, however, needs its indicator set re-mapped: state legitimacy -> local service delivery and governance responsiveness; security apparatus -> local policing; external intervention -> state/NGO dependence; refugees/IDPs -> internal displacement. The thresholds (70/110) are state-calibrated and must be re-calibrated against a city- or region-level fragility benchmark (e.g. the same 12 re-mapped indicators scored for a reference set of cities). The dynamics layer transfers unchanged in form; drift b and feedback phi must be re-estimated at city resolution. Up-scales (continents/aggregates): the composite transfers by aggregation (population-weighted mean of member states' F values, or the sum over a common 0-120*weight scale), but the climate submodel must be run per member state and then aggregated, because yield-loss benchmarks and irrigation elasticities are state-heterogeneous; a single continent-level aridity number would bias the aggregate. The classification thresholds then apply to the aggregate index with the caveat that an aggregate can hide member states on opposite sides of a threshold.

### Outcome Analysis

Conclusion: the model works on smaller and larger units only with modification. For cities: keep the climate submodel, re-map the 6 state-specific indicators to local governance analogs, and re-calibrate thresholds against a city-level benchmark — a straightforward parameter-level change, no structural change. For continents: run the full state-level model for each member and aggregate the indices (and the climate increments) with population weights; treat the aggregate's threshold crossing as a warning signal about the member mix rather than a property of the continent as a single state. The sanity check built into the model supports the down-scale claim: Norway (low exposure, F = 19.0) shows a ~0 climate increment, i.e. the climate channel does not manufacture fragility where exposure is low, so the same mechanism will behave sensibly at any resolution given correct exposure parameters. Residual limitation: at city scale the 'self-reinforcing spiral' feedback (phi) is less well understood, so the dynamics layer's predictive (tipping-year) content is strongest at state scale and should be read as indicative below that.
