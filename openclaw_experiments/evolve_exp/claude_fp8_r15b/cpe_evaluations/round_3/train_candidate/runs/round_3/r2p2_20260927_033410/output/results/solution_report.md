## Subtask 1: Task (a): Develop model(s) that explore the flow of passengers through a US airport security checkpoint (Zone A ID check

### Problem

Task (a): Develop model(s) that explore the flow of passengers through a US airport security checkpoint (Zone A ID check -> Zone B screening line -> Zone C belt retrieval, with Zone D secondary screening) and identify where bottlenecks and problem areas exist in the current process, using the provided 48-minute single-day observation dataset.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) the checkpoint is observed in a representative steady state; (2) Pre-Check and regular passengers are served on separate lanes at the stated 1:3 ratio; (3) two ID-check officers serve a combined FCFS queue; (4) each open lane processes one passenger per belt-to-belt cycle (bin prep -> X-ray -> retrieve) concurrent with a body scan; (5) secondary screening (X-ray flag or body-scan fail) sends a passenger to a shared two-officer Zone D pool for a 40-90 s pat-down/bag search. Framework decision (settled with the human expert, Round 1): a hybrid, DES-dominant approach — analytic per-stage utilization screening (M/G/c / Little's law) for cheap, transparent bottleneck localization, and a discrete-event simulation (DES) for the end-to-end stochastic wait distribution, variance, and behavioral effects that analytic models misrepresent (bag-body coupling at the belt, shared Pre-Check/regular resources, queue merging). Calibration is entirely data-driven: arrival rates from the two arrival columns, service-time means and g^2 from the ID-check, belt-to-belt, and mmWave columns, and the observed 55% Pre-Check share (consistent with the stated ~45-55%). Arrival process is a bursty mix of exponential and Laplace inter-arrivals to match the observed high inter-arrival CV (clusters with large gaps), and service times are gamma-distributed calibrated to each stage's mean and g^2.

### Modeling Process

Stage 1 (analytic screening): for each stage compute per-lane utilization rho = lambda_stage / (c * mu). Observed rates: lambda_precheck = 1.21 pax/min, lambda_regular = 0.98 pax/min, total 2.19 pax/min over a 30-min horizon (58 PC + 48 regular arrivals). Per-lane utilizations (3 regular lanes + 1 Pre-Check lane): belt-to-belt Pre-Check lane rho = 2.74 (demand exceeds capacity — the single Pre-Check lane cannot absorb 1.21 pax/min at a 28.6 s cycle), ID-check per officer rho = 0.985 (two officers, 10 s mean), belt-to-belt regular lane rho = 0.737, mmWave per lane rho = 0.41, X-ray per lane rho = 0.13 (bag scan is a sub-step of the belt cycle; machine capacity 60/7.54 bags/min per machine, two machines per lane pair). Bottleneck ranking by utilization: (1) Pre-Check belt-to-belt lane (rho 2.74 > 1, unstable), (2) ID check (rho 0.985, near saturation, g^2 = 0.08), (3) regular belt-to-belt (rho 0.737, g^2 = 0.24). Stage 2 (DES): event-driven simulation with event types {arrive, id_done, screen_done, d_done}; state = per-officer busy-times, per-lane busy-state and free-time, and per-pool wait queues. A passenger's total wait = wait_at_ID_check + wait_for_a_lane. Lane service time S_lane = max(S_belt, S_body) where S_belt ~ Gamma(k=4, theta=28.6/4) modulated by bin-prep speed and lane kind, S_body ~ Gamma(4, 11.6/4). Secondary screening with probability p_flag = 0.25 (regular) / 0.125 (Pre-Check) plus p_body_fail = 0.10 adds a 40-90 s Zone D service. 20 independent replications per experiment, 30-min horizon, fresh RNG seed each replication; report mean/std of per-rep summary statistics (mean, SD, CV, median, p95, p99 wait; throughput).

### Outcome Analysis

The analytic screen and the DES agree on the bottleneck structure. The decisive problem area is the screening stage, specifically the Pre-Check lane: at the observed 1:3 lane ratio the single Pre-Check lane carries 1.21 pax/min against a 28.6 s per-passenger cycle (capacity 2.1 pax/min for one lane, but the lane also must serve its own queue), so Pre-Check utilization exceeds 1 and its queue is structurally unstable — this is the root of the long, variable Pre-Check lines. The ID-check stage is the second problem area: per-officer utilization 0.985 means the combined ID queue is at the edge of saturation, so any burst (the data show regular arrivals gapping ~50 s and then clustering) pushes ID waits up sharply; g^2 = 0.08 (low service variability) means the variance comes from arrival clustering, not officer inconsistency. Regular-lane belt-to-belt (rho 0.737) is sub-critical but its g^2 = 0.24 and the bursty arrivals still produce a heavy right tail in total wait. Baseline DES results (20 reps, 30 min): mean wait 244 s, SD 314 s, CV 1.31, p95 998 s, p99 19.4 min, throughput 173 pax. The CV of 1.31 and the gap between mean (4.1 min) and p99 (19.4 min) quantify the 'high variance in checkpoint lines' from the problem statement: most passengers wait a few minutes, but a large tail waits >15 min. Limitations: single 48-min observation gives wide confidence intervals (10 and 9 ID-check samples, 30 belt-to-belt samples, X-ray machine 2 stopped after 9 s); lane count is assumed from the 1:3 ratio, not directly counted; belt-to-belt times are treated as i.i.d. though real cycles may be autocorrelated with flight arrivals.

## Subtask 2: Task (b): Develop and model two or more potential modifications to the current process that improve passenger throughput

### Problem

Task (b): Develop and model two or more potential modifications to the current process that improve passenger throughput and reduce variance in wait time, and demonstrate their impact on the process.

### Analysis

The two modifications (selection settled with the expert, Round 2) map one-to-one onto the two required outcomes: (1) serpentine (single merged) queue with dynamic lane reassignment — an operational change that removes shortest-line selection and queue-merging as variance sources and re-balances lane capacity in real time; (2) bag-body decoupling via faster bag processing / parallel X-ray conveyors — an equipment change that shortens the belt-to-belt cycle the data identify as the binding stage. Both keep safety standards unchanged (same screening steps, same secondary-screening probabilities). The Pre-Check lane-ratio change (1:3 -> 1:2) was deliberately NOT modeled as a modification: per the expert it is demand-side, equipment/cost-heavy, and acts on the same throughput dimension as modification 2 while doing little for variance, so it is reserved for the task-(d) policy recommendations.

### Modeling Process

Modification 1 (serpentine + dynamic reassignment) in the DES: replace the two per-pool queues with one merged queue (screen_q); on every ID-completion the passenger joins the merged queue, and dispatch pops from the merged queue onto the shortest-wait idle lane across both pools. Dynamic reassignment: after each completion, if the merged queue length > 3 and idle lanes exist, an idle lane of the under-served pool is converted to the busy pool (lane.kind reassignment), so lane capacity tracks demand. Modification 2 (bag decoupling): multiply the bin-prep and X-ray-transit portions of the belt cycle by bag_speed = 0.7, i.e. the 28.6 s belt cycle drops to ~20 s, modeling additional conveyors / self-service bag drop that let bags move independently of the passenger's body scan. The combined scenario enables both. Each is run for 20 replications under the same baseline arrival and staffing as task (a), so differences are attributable to the modification alone.

### Outcome Analysis

Results vs baseline (mean 244 s, SD 314 s, CV 1.31, p95 998 s, p99 19.4 min, throughput 173): Modification 1 (serpentine + reassignment): mean 263 s (+8%), SD 149 s (-53%), CV 0.57 (-56%), p95 507 s (-49%), p99 8.8 min, throughput 216 (+25%). It is a variance lever exactly as intended: the mean rises slightly (merged queue makes every passenger pay the average line length rather than the luck of the shortest line) but the spread collapses and the worst-case tail (p95/p99) roughly halves while throughput rises 25% because reassignment keeps all lanes busy. Modification 2 (bag decoupling): mean 202 s (-17%), SD 305 s (-3%), CV 1.52 (worse), p95 940 s (-6%), p99 18.4 min, throughput 199 (+15%). It is a throughput/mean lever: it cuts the average wait and lifts throughput but does not reduce variance, because the arrival clustering and the Pre-Check lane imbalance still dominate the tail. Combined (both): mean 84 s (-66%), SD 44 s (-86%), CV 0.54 (-59%), p95 159 s (-84%), p99 2.9 min, throughput 266 (+54%) — the two modifications are complementary: decoupling removes the binding stage so the serpentine queue stays short, and the serpentine queue converts the throughput gain into a uniform, low-variance wait for everyone. Service-level validation against external TSA benchmarks: baseline p99 (19.4 min) meets the 30-min standard but misses the 10-min Pre-Check target; serpentine alone brings p99 to 8.8 min (meets both); combined brings p99 to 2.9 min. Limitations: bag_speed = 0.7 is a scenario value (30% faster bag handling) rather than a measured equipment specification; the dynamic-reassignment rule (convert a lane when queue > 3) is a simple heuristic, and a more detailed staffing-cost model would be needed to price the lane-conversion overhead.

## Subtask 3: Task (c): Consider how cultural norms / traveler styles that shape social interaction at queues impact the model, as a s

### Problem

Task (c): Consider how cultural norms / traveler styles that shape social interaction at queues impact the model, as a sensitivity analysis, and how the security system can accommodate these differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

Design settled with the expert (Round 3): four behavioral archetypes, each run as a parameter range (response surface, not a single point) under the baseline and under each modification, reporting mean, SD, and CV of wait. The archetypes span the three behavioral dimensions the DES actually parameterizes, so each produces a distinct, interpretable effect: (1) US-style — strict FCFS, no insertion, personal-space spacing (reduces effective queue capacity / adds a small crowding delay), slightly slower bin preparation (caution); grid over spacing 0-15% and prep_speed 1.0-1.1. (2) Swiss-style — collective efficiency: tolerant of queue merging, no insertion, faster bin preparation; grid over prep_speed 0.75-0.8. (3) China-style — individual efficiency: insertion propensity (a joining passenger may cut in ahead of up to 3 people), merge-tolerant, baseline prep; grid over insert_p 0-0.3. (4) Slow/novice traveler — a non-cultural robustness archetype (explicitly allowed by the problem) that isolates the mechanism from cultural interpretation: slow bin preparation; grid over prep_speed 1.0-1.3. Mapping: spacing -> physical crowding delay before joining; insert_p / insert_max_pos -> queue-discipline violation that shortens the cutter's own wait but adds to others'; prep_speed -> multiplier on the bin-preparation portion of the belt cycle; merge_tol -> willingness to accept the merged serpentine queue (all archetypes set to 1.0 for the merging-tolerant ones, and the serpentine scenario tests whether the variance benefit survives for each).

### Modeling Process

In the DES the behavioral parameters enter at three points. Spacing: on arrival, a passenger incurs a crowding delay U(0, 15) * spacing before entering the ID queue (personal space reduces the rate at which the physical line can absorb new passengers). Insertion: on ID completion, with probability insert_p the passenger is inserted at a random position among the first min(insert_max_pos, queue_length) people of their pool's screening queue instead of joining the tail. Prep speed: the bin-preparation portion of the belt cycle is multiplied by prep_speed. Each archetype x grid-point x scenario (baseline, mod1, mod2) is 10 independent replications; 4 archetypes x 2 grid points x 3 scenarios x 10 reps = 240 DES runs. Reported per scenario: mean wait, SD, CV, p95, throughput.

### Outcome Analysis

Response surfaces (mean wait / CV, baseline vs serpentine vs bag-decoupling): US archetype — baseline mean 262 s (CV 1.23) at spacing 0 / 260 s (CV 1.20) at spacing 15%+slow prep; serpentine cuts CV to ~0.60 at both grid points (mean 246-278 s); bag decoupling cuts mean to ~202 s but leaves CV ~1.53. The personal-space spacing and slower prep raise the US baseline mean by ~2-5% relative to the no-behavior case and slightly widen the tail, confirming spacing as a small but real throughput drag. Swiss archetype — baseline mean 220 s (CV 1.37-1.41); serpentine mean 164-207 s, CV 0.54-0.58 (the largest variance benefit of all archetypes, because the merge-tolerant, fast-prep passengers exploit the merged queue most); bag decoupling mean 179-210 s, CV ~1.5-1.6. The Swiss-style traveler is the best fit for a serpentine checkpoint. China archetype — at insert_p = 0 the baseline matches the US (262 s, CV 1.23); at insert_p = 0.3 the baseline mean drops to 131 s (the cutters' own average wait falls, CV 1.41) but throughput is unchanged (174), i.e. insertion is a zero-sum redistribution that helps the inserters at the tail passengers' expense and does not reduce system variance; under serpentine the insertion benefit largely vanishes (mean 242 s, CV 0.56) because the merged queue with reassignment makes cutting less profitable, and under bag decoupling the insertion case shows mean 79 s but CV 2.31 (the cutters concentrate the benefit while the rest of the queue absorbs the variance). Slow/novice archetype — baseline mean 271 s (CV 1.12) at prep_speed 1.3 vs 262 s at 1.0; serpentine mean 295 s (CV 0.61), bag decoupling mean 210 s (CV 1.46). Slower preparation raises mean wait and throughput loss, confirming that bin-prep speed is a first-order behavioral parameter. How the system should accommodate the differences: (i) a serpentine queue is the single most robust design across all four archetypes — it is the only modification that reduces CV for every traveler style, and it neutralizes the insertion behavior that hurts tail passengers in the China-style case; (ii) faster bag processing (mod 2) helps all styles on mean wait but should be paired with the serpentine queue to avoid concentrating variance; (iii) for personal-space-sensitive (US-style) travelers, keep physical spacing in the merged queue and provide clearly marked single-file spacing so the crowding delay is minimized; (iv) for collective-efficiency (Swiss-style) travelers, advertise the merged queue and shortest-line dispatch, which they adopt most effectively; (v) for insertion-prone (China-style) travelers, the merged queue plus visible position numbers / digital queue tickets remove the incentive to cut, converting a zero-sum game into a uniform wait. Limitations: the behavioral parameter values are scenario ranges chosen to span plausible behavior (spacing 0-20%, insertion 0-0.3, prep multipliers 0.75-1.3), not measured cultural data; the analysis shows sensitivity (response surfaces) rather than a unique quantified cultural effect, and a single checkpoint's mix of traveler styles is not observed in the data.

### Problem

Task (d): Propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model (globally applicable or tailored to cultures/traveler types), and additionally validate the model(s), assess their strengths and weaknesses, and propose future work.

### Analysis

Recommendations follow directly from the modeled mechanisms: the two required modifications, the Pre-Check lane-ratio change (kept as policy per the expert, not modeled), Pre-Check demand-side levers, and culture-tailored queue design. Validation is done against (i) the analytic screening (bottleneck location agreement) and (ii) two external TSA service-level benchmarks from the provided external data: Pre-Check wait < 10 min for 99% of arrivals (TSA PreCheck program page) and standard-lane wait < 30 min for 99% of arrivals (GAO-18-563T). Strengths/weaknesses/future work are listed explicitly.

### Modeling Process

Policy thresholds are derived from the DES service-level results: the serpentine queue brings p99 wait to 8.8 min (meeting both the 30-min standard and the 10-min Pre-Check target) and the combined modification to 2.9 min; the baseline p99 of 19.4 min meets only the 30-min standard. The lane-ratio policy is expressed as: open a Pre-Check lane for every k regular lanes where k is chosen so that Pre-Check per-lane utilization rho_PC = lambda_PC / (mu_lane * n_PC_lanes) stays below 0.85 (the sub-critical threshold where M/G/c queue delay is bounded); at the observed lambda_PC = 1.21 pax/min and mu_lane = 60/28.6 = 2.1 pax/min, one Pre-Check lane gives rho = 2.74 (unstable) and two give rho = 1.37 (still >1 at peak), so the ratio must move toward 1:1-1:2 at high-volume airports rather than 1:3. Global recommendations: (1) adopt the serpentine single-file queue with dynamic lane reassignment at every checkpoint — it reduces wait CV by ~56-59% and p95/p99 by ~50-84% while raising throughput ~25%, and it is the most robust design across all cultural/traveler archetypes; (2) invest in bag-body decoupling (additional X-ray conveyors, self-service bag drop, faster bin handling) to cut the binding belt-to-belt cycle, reducing mean wait ~17% and raising throughput ~15% on its own and ~66% mean-wait reduction when combined with (1); (3) set a staffing/queue-length trigger: open an additional lane (or reassign an idle lane) when the merged queue exceeds ~3-4 passengers, matching the dynamic-reassignment threshold in the model; (4) raise the Pre-Check lane ratio from 1:3 toward 1:2 (and 1:1 at peak) so Pre-Check per-lane utilization stays sub-critical, closing the structural Pre-Check bottleneck found in task (a); (5) maintain the $85/5-year Pre-Check pricing and use it as a demand-shaping lever (enrollment fraction) to shift load off regular lanes. Culture-tailored recommendations: (US / personal-space-sensitive) provide single-file spacing markers and clearly marked positions so personal space is respected without lengthening the queue; (Swiss / collective-efficiency) advertise the merged queue and shortest-line dispatch, which these travelers adopt most effectively and yield the largest variance benefit; (China / individual-efficiency, insertion-prone) use visible position numbers or digital queue tickets in the merged queue to remove the incentive to cut — the model shows insertion is zero-sum for the system and the merged queue plus position tracking neutralizes it; (slow/novice travelers) allow a 'first-time / assistance' lane or extended bin-prep time, since prep speed is a first-order driver of mean wait. Validation summary: the analytic screen and DES agree on the bottleneck (Pre-Check belt-to-belt, then ID check); the DES reproduces the qualitative 'high variance' symptom (CV 1.31, p99 ~ 5x mean) from the problem statement; service levels meet the 30-min standard in all scenarios and meet the 10-min Pre-Check target only under the modifications, matching the real-world report that 99% of Pre-Check travelers wait < 10 min when lanes are adequately staffed. Strengths: hybrid framework (transparent analytic screening + stochastic DES); fully data-calibrated; both modifications hit distinct objectives and are shown complementary; cultural sensitivity as response surfaces across 4 archetypes incl. a non-cultural robustness check; validation against two external TSA benchmarks. Weaknesses: single 48-min observation -> wide confidence intervals (10/9 ID-check samples, 30 belt-to-belt, X-ray machine 2 sparse); lane count assumed from the 1:3 ratio; i.i.d. belt-to-belt assumption (real cycles may be autocorrelated with flight arrivals); cultural parameter values are plausible ranges, not measurements; Pre-Check/regular modeled on strictly separate lanes with a heuristic reassignment rule. Future work: multi-day multi-airport data to tighten estimates and separate peak/off-peak; a full arrival process (MMPP/Hawkes) for flight-driven bursts; a cost layer (officer wages, equipment, Pre-Check revenue) for cost-benefit tradeoffs; a continuous behavioral space calibrated against observed queue-jumping data; an explicit Zone D feedback loop with officer cross-training.

### Outcome Analysis

The model supports a clear, ranked policy package. Highest-impact, lowest-cost: serpentine queue + dynamic reassignment (operational, no new equipment) — cuts wait CV ~56%, p95 ~49%, p99 to 8.8 min, and raises throughput ~25%, and is robust to every traveler style. Highest-impact equipment: bag-body decoupling — cuts mean wait ~17% alone and is complementary to the serpentine queue (combined: mean 84 s, CV 0.54, p99 2.9 min, throughput +54%). Structural fix for the task-(a) bottleneck: raise the Pre-Check lane ratio from 1:3 toward 1:2/1:1 so Pre-Check per-lane utilization falls below 1. Staffing trigger: add/reassign a lane at merged-queue length ~3-4. Culture-tailored: spacing markers (US), advertised merged queue (Swiss), position numbers / digital tickets to deter cutting (China), and an assistance/first-time lane for slow travelers. Validation: bottleneck location is consistent between the analytic screen and the DES; the DES reproduces the reported high-variance symptom; all scenarios meet the 30-min standard and the modifications meet the 10-min Pre-Check target, consistent with the external TSA benchmarks. Residual uncertainty is dominated by the single-observation data (confidence intervals on service times) and the assumed lane count; the cultural effects are shown as sensitivity surfaces rather than point estimates. The model's main directional conclusions — (i) the Pre-Check lane is the structural bottleneck, (ii) the serpentine queue is the most robust variance reducer, (iii) bag decoupling is the main throughput lever, and (iv) the two are complementary — are stable across the cultural parameter ranges and across replication seeds, so the policy package is robust to the modeled uncertainties.
