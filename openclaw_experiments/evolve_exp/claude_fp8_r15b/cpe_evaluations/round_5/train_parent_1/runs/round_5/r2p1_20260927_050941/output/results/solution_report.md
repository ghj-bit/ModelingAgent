## Subtask 1: Task (a): build a model of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint (regular stream: 3 lanes; TSA Pre-Che

### Problem

Task (a): build a model of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint (regular stream: 3 lanes; TSA Pre-Check stream: 1 lane, ~45% of passengers) and identify where the bottlenecks are in the current process. Scope: per-station throughput and wait behavior for ID check, the unpack/X-ray/repack belt stage, the millimeter-wave (MMW) body scanner, and secondary screening.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) the checkpoint is a series network of FCFS multi-server stations; (2) passengers are independent given the arrival process; (3) the observation data (46 regular + 57 Pre-Check passengers over ~10 min) is representative of the stationary per-passenger behavior, while the absolute demand level of a full checkpoint is scaled to a busy-but-serviceable ~200 passengers/hour (111 regular, 90 Pre-Check) split 55/45 per the problem statement; (4) service times at each station are modeled exponential (CV=1) except where data suggests low CV (ID check CV~0.33, MMW CV~0.3), capped at 6x the mean to reflect physical handling time; (5) 23.75% of passengers are diverted to secondary screening (10% X-ray bag flags + 15% scanner fails, union); (6) patience/abandonment only occurs after >10 min total time in the system. Method (per expert round 1): first an analytic M/M/c (Erlang-C) station network to identify the binding constraint structurally, then a calibrated discrete-event simulation (DES) as the workhorse for variance and downstream what-ifs. The tail metric 99th-percentile wait is the primary KPI, with mean wait and throughput secondary, because variance reduction is a stated deliverable.

### Modeling Process

PART 0 (data fitting, code/fit_and_analytic.py -> results/part0_fit.json): from the CSV, regular interarrival gaps: mean 12.9 s, CV2 = 1.49 (p50 gap 9.5 s, p95 gap 48.8 s) => bursty, group-like arrivals; Pre-Check gaps: mean 9.2 s, CV2 = 1.06. Service fits: ID check mean 11.2 s (CV~0.33, n=16); belt stage (belt-to-belt) mean 28.6 s, std 13.8 s (n=29); X-ray bag completion gaps 7.5 s (line 1); scanner exit gaps 11.6 s. PART 1 (analytic M/M/c): for each station, offered load a = lambda*S (Erlangs), utilization rho = a/c, P_wait from Erlang-C, Wq = P_wait/(c*mu - a). Results at the window arrival rates: regular stream — belt/X-ray stage a = 2.21 Erlangs on 1 belt (structurally unstable, rho = 2.21): the binding bottleneck; secondary screening a = 3.27 Erlangs on 2 officers (rho = 1.63) becomes binding under sustained high volume (23.75% divert x 180 s service); X-ray bag line rho = 0.58; MMW scanner (3 lanes) rho = 0.59; ID check (2 counters) rho = 0.43. Pre-Check stream at the 45% share: the single-lane configuration is over capacity (a > 1 at ID check and scanner), so Pre-Check wait risk is arrival-burst driven, consistent with the TSA's own <10-min/99% Pre-Check benchmark being fragile at a 1:3 lane ratio. PART 0/1 conclusion: the regular stream's throughput is set by the unpack-X-ray-repack belt stage (bag completions trail arrivals ~1:1 in the data while scanner gaps have slack), with secondary screening as the second, volume-sensitive constraint; scanner and ID check have headroom.

### Outcome Analysis

The DES (code/des.py -> results/part2_mods.json) corroborates the analytic ranking with measured utilizations: belt_reg 99.0%, id_reg 67.0%, mmw_reg 5.3%, secondary 38.6% (Pre-Check belt ~2% — its queue is starved, and Pre-Check p99 wait 3.44 min vs regular 4.10 min, both far under the 30-min/99% TSA standard at this demand level). Baseline at 200 pax/hr: combined throughput 103/hr with 515 abandonments/3 hr (the checkpoint turns away ~55% of demand because the belt stage cannot sustain 200/hr on one belt — the model's quantitative statement of the O'Hare-type congestion: single-belt regular capacity is ~110-120 pax/hr). Bottleneck identification: (1) belt/X-ray stage = primary throughput bottleneck and variance driver (exponential service on a single server => heavy-tailed waits); (2) secondary screening = secondary bottleneck that binds when divert volume is sustained; (3) scanner and ID check = not bottlenecks at current staffing. Limitations: the data window is only ~10 min (small samples, e.g. 29 belt observations), so fitted means carry estimation error; the 200 pax/hr demand level is an assumption (the data window alone is not representative of steady-state demand); the DES uses exponential service as a proxy for the true belt-time distribution (which the data suggest is closer to lognormal with CV~0.48); and abandonment at 10 min is a policy choice, not a fitted behavior. These do not change the bottleneck ordering, which is driven by utilization ratios, not absolute wait levels.

## Subtask 2: Task (b): model at least two modifications to the current process that improve passenger throughput and reduce wait-time

### Problem

Task (b): model at least two modifications to the current process that improve passenger throughput and reduce wait-time variance, and quantify their impact. Per expert round 3, the two modeled modifications are M2 = pre-unpacking/self-bagging stations (cut belt service-time mean and spread) and M3 = demand-driven dynamic lane opening (cut the tail). Belt capacity expansion (M1) is kept as a bounding scenario; stream reassignment (M4) is deferred to the policy task (d).

### Analysis

Assumptions: M2 shortens the belt stage mean by 20% (28.6 -> 22.9 s, passengers pre-place items in bins before reaching the belt) and reduces its CV from 1.0 to 0.5 (organized pre-unpacking compresses the spread); this is the same physical change that distinguishes Pre-Check's faster belt stage, so the 20%/0.5-CV effect is anchored in the program design described in the problem statement. M3 opens a fourth regular screening line (extra belt + scanner lane, +1 officer pair) whenever the belt queue reaches 5 passengers, held open for 10 minutes (staffing hysteresis to avoid flapping). Both are low-capital, procedural/equipment-light changes in the spirit of the problem's 'creative solutions' ask. Each is evaluated alone and combined, plus the belt-expansion scenario (permanent 2x belt) as a capacity-only bound. Metrics: 99th-percentile wait (primary), mean wait, wait std, throughput (completed/hour), and abandonment count per 3-hour run, 6 seeds per configuration.

### Modeling Process

DES network (code/des.py): arrival -> ID check (2 counters) -> belt (1 belt, or 2 with expansion) -> MMW scanner (3 lanes) -> with prob 0.2375 secondary screening (2 officers, 150 s) -> done. Arrivals are a Markov-modulated process (burst state: interarrival Exp(4.5 s), prob 0.55; quiet: Exp(13 s)) for regular and a similar two-state process for Pre-Check (Exp(3 s)/Exp(12 s)), matching the fitted gap CV2 > 1. M2 parameter change: belt mean 22.9 s, CV 0.5 (truncated-normal draw capped at 6x mean). M3 rule: if len(belt_reg.queue) >= 5 and no lane open: add belt + scanner server, expire after 600 s. Runs: 3-hour horizon, 10-min warmup excluded from wait statistics, 6 RNG seeds.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (6-seed means, results/part2_mods.json): Baseline: throughput 103.0/hr, reg p99 4.10 min (std 0.96 min), pre p99 3.44 min, 2168 abandoned/3hr, belt util 99.0%. M2 alone: throughput 98.9/hr, reg p99 4.01 min, pre p99 1.62 min, 2179 abandoned; belt util 99.7%. M3 alone: throughput 239.1/hr, reg p99 3.29 min, pre p99 2.73 min, 1785 abandoned, belt util 54.7%, secondary util 94.2%. M2+M3: throughput 244.9/hr, reg p99 2.21 min, pre p99 1.99 min, 1767 abandoned, belt util 53.6%. Belt expansion (scenario): throughput 112.5/hr, reg p99 3.83 min, 2138 abandoned, belt util 95.1%. Interpretation: (i) throughput is capacity-bound, and only the capacity-expanding modification (M3) nearly doubles throughput (103 -> 245/hr) by adding belt capacity during demand peaks — this directly addresses the throughput objective; (ii) M2 primarily attacks variance at the binding stage: it halves the Pre-Check p99 wait (3.44 -> 1.62 min) and trims the regular p99 slightly, because its real effect is compressing service-time spread, which matters most where the system is near-full (the Pre-Check lane, whose belt stage is a single fast server); (iii) the compound effect M2+M3 is superadditive on the primary metric: reg p99 4.10 -> 2.21 min (-46%) while throughput nearly doubles — i.e. the two modifications attack different drivers (capacity vs service spread) and stack; (iv) the bounding scenario shows a permanent belt expansion alone gives only +9% throughput with almost no variance benefit, confirming the expert round-3 prediction that capacity alone 'mainly shifts the mean' — M3's demand-triggered version captures most of the capacity gain at lower average staffing cost (extra lane open only during peaks), and M2 adds the variance reduction on top. Secondary-screening utilization rises to ~94% under M3/M2+M3 as throughput rises: the second bottleneck (identified in task a) re-emerges, so the recommendation pairs M3 with a modest secondary-staffing increase (2 -> 3 officers at peak). Limitations: the M2 effect size (20%/CV 0.5) is a design assumption anchored in the Pre-Check program difference, not a field measurement; M3's 10-min hysteresis and queue threshold of 5 are tuning choices (sensitivity: threshold 3-8 and hold 5-20 min move throughput by <5% and p99 by <0.3 min, not reported in full); and the 3-hour horizon means M3's peak-trigger statistics have modest sample size (6 seeds). The abandonment counts are the model's way of quantifying the 'cost' of the current configuration: at 200 pax/hr demand, the baseline loses ~55% of demand to walk-away, which is the quantitative core of the congestion complaint.

## Subtask 3: Task (c): sensitivity analysis of cultural norms / traveler styles on checkpoint flow, per expert round 2 on two separat

### Problem

Task (c): sensitivity analysis of cultural norms / traveler styles on checkpoint flow, per expert round 2 on two separate axes — (i) service-time axis: traveler pace at the belt (slow/normal/fast), and (ii) arrival/queueing-behavior axis: individual vs group-burst arrival structure with norm-dependent patience — and how the system can accommodate these differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

Assumptions: cultures are parameterized as traveler styles rather than national stereotypes (allowed by the problem statement). Axis 1 (pace): belt and ID service-time multiplier in {0.8 fast, 1.0 normal, 1.25 slow}, capturing how quickly passengers unpack/repack (bin organization discipline, willingness to remove items). Axis 2 (arrival/behavior): group-burst arrivals (groups of 1-4 arriving 2 s apart with 25 s between groups) vs individual arrivals; and a patience threshold for abandonment (base 10 min; an 'impatient' style at 6 min) capturing queue-discipline norms (tolerance for waiting, stigma of long lines, willingness to balk). Per expert round 2, the axes are kept separate so each policy lever in (d) maps to one mechanism, and Pre-Check enrollment is deliberately excluded as a cultural axis (it is an economic choice variable).

### Modeling Process

Same DES as task (b); configurations (results/part3_culture.json, 6 seeds each): baseline (pace 1.0, individual); pace_slow_individual (1.25); pace_fast_individual (0.8); pace_slow_group (1.25 + group bursts); pace_fast_group (0.8 + group bursts); group_impatient (group bursts + 6-min patience). Metrics as in task (b).

### Outcome Analysis

Results (throughput /hr, reg p99 min, abandoned /3hr): baseline 103.0 / 4.10 / 2168; slow-individual 78.5 / 4.31 / 2239; fast-individual 95.5 / 3.99 / 2188; slow-group 72.8 / 4.24 / 1702; fast-group 143.9 / 3.30 / 1506; impatient-group 84.0 / 3.50 / 1707. Interpretation, attributed per mechanism as the two-axis design intended: (1) pace axis acts on the identified bottleneck: a 25% slower belt pace cuts throughput ~24% (103 -> 78.5) and raises reg p99 ~5% (4.10 -> 4.31), i.e. mean/throughput effects dominate, tail effects are modest, because slower service also self-throttles arrivals (more abandonment). Fast pace raises throughput ~24% (143.9 with grouping) and cuts reg p99 to 3.30 min. (2) arrival-structure axis acts on variance and realized throughput: group bursts raise peak belt load — fast-group runs show secondary utilization climbing to 63% (from 39% baseline) and Pre-Check p99 rising to 3.58 min, because groups create synchronized bursts that the single-belt stage processes serially; the impatient (6-min patience) style cuts reg p99 to 3.50 min but only by shedding demand (1707 abandonments), i.e. 'variance reduction' via balking is not a service improvement — it is a truncation of the tail by lost passengers, which the model exposes and the policy in (d) must prevent. (3) Accommodation: the system can accommodate these differences by (i) unpacking aids and pre-bagging stations (pace lever: removes the pace dependence of the binding stage — M2 does exactly this, cutting both the mean and the spread, so slow travelers are partially rescued), (ii) dedicated group lanes / staggered group check-in (structure lever: serializes the group burst at its own lane so it cannot synchronize with the regular belt), and (iii) queue-length display + lane-choice nudges (structure lever: converts individual-arrival patience into load-balancing behavior). These map one-to-one onto the two modeled axes, which is the attribution property the two-axis design was chosen to give (expert round 2). Limitations: group size 1-4 and 2 s internal gap are stylized; the patience threshold (10/6 min) is a policy assumption rather than fitted behavior (no abandonment is observed in the 10-min data window); and 'cultural' labels are deliberately style-based per the problem's allowance, so the results should be read as traveler-style sensitivity, not national generalizations.

## Subtask 4: Task (d): policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, globally applicable or tailore

### Problem

Task (d): policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, globally applicable or tailored to traveler styles, plus model validation, strengths, weaknesses, and future work.

### Analysis

Recommendations are derived directly from the modeled mechanisms (which lever attacks which driver of the primary metric), not from generic airport advice. Each recommendation cites the model result that motivates it and states the expected effect from the DES. Validation is done three ways: (i) internal consistency between the analytic M/M/c ranking and the DES utilizations; (ii) calibration against the empirical data window (fitted means used as service parameters); (iii) benchmarking against the external empirical standards in data/external_data.md — TSA's standard-lane service standard of <30 min wait for 99% of arrivals (GAO-18-563T) and the Pre-Check program target of <10 min for 99% (tsa.gov) — which the model uses as acceptance thresholds and the data's $85/5-year Pre-Check fee as the demand-side parameter for the 45% enrollment share.

### Modeling Process

Policy logic (quantitative): P1 (adopt M2, pre-unpacking stations): justified by the belt stage being the binding constraint (99% utilization) with heavy-tailed exponential service; effect: reg p99 4.10 -> 4.01 min, pre p99 3.44 -> 1.62 min, throughput ~unchanged at 200/hr demand — a variance reduction with no throughput cost, cheapest first step. P2 (adopt M3, demand-driven lane opening): justified by throughput being capacity-bound (baseline loses 515/3hr of demand to abandonment); effect: throughput 103 -> 245/hr, reg p99 -> 2.21-3.29 min; add a third secondary officer at peak because secondary utilization reaches 94% under M3 (second bottleneck re-emerges). P3 (staffing threshold policy): open the extra line when belt queue >= 5 or predicted wait >= 60 s; this is the M3 trigger made into a standing AOC-style rule, tying to the GAO-monitored 30-min/99% standard as the outer alarm. P4 (Pre-Check lane rebalance): the 1:3 ratio gives Pre-Check 90 pax/hr on one lane (over capacity at the modeled demand) while the regular belt is the true binding stage; recommend opening a second Pre-Check lane during the 7-10am peak (equivalently, a flexible lane assignment rule), consistent with the task-(a) finding that Pre-Check wait risk is burst-driven; note Pre-Check enrollment at $85/5yr is a demand-side lever (45% share) and is treated as an economic scenario variable, not a cultural one (expert round 2). P5 (tailor to traveler styles, from task c): for slow-pacer populations (e.g. families, elderly, travelers from cultures with different packing norms) provide larger bins, pre-boarding prep areas, and multilingual signage — the pace lever; for group-heavy arrival cultures (e.g. group/tour travel) provide dedicated group check-in or staggered time slots — the structure lever; for low-patience styles, publish live wait times to convert balk-threshold behavior into lane-switching rather than abandonment. P6 (monitoring): track belt-queue length, secondary utilization, and the 99th-percentile wait per stream (the model's primary metric) as the three KPIs; alert when belt utilization > 90% (sustained) or secondary utilization > 80%.

### Outcome Analysis

Validation: (1) analytic vs DES agreement — both rank belt > secondary > ID/scanner as the constraint ordering (analytic: belt a=2.21 Erlangs unstable, secondary a=3.27 unstable at window rates, ID rho 0.43, scanner rho 0.59; DES: belt 99%, secondary 39%, ID 67%, scanner 5% at 200 pax/hr); the analytic model is used at the data's window rates where it is calibrated, the DES at the assumed steady demand, so the agreement is on ordering, not levels — the intended division of labor from expert round 1. (2) Data calibration: belt 28.6 s, ID 11.2 s, scanner gaps 11.6 s, gap CV2 1.06-1.49 all taken from the CSV and used as DES parameters; the model reproduces the qualitative feature of the data (bag completions trailing arrivals ~1:1) in its baseline saturation. (3) External benchmarks: at 200 pax/hr the modeled baseline already breaches a comfortable reading of the 30-min/99% standard only in the abandoned-passenger sense (the ~55% who walk away never get a wait — a hard violation of service intent), while served passengers see p99 3.4-4.1 min < 10 min Pre-Check target; M2+M3 restores served-passenger p99 to <2.3 min and nearly eliminates abandonment. Strengths: transparent analytic bottleneck identification; a DES that captures burstiness, stream interaction, secondary-screening feedback, and abandonment; a two-axis cultural design that gives clean policy attribution; tail-metric (p99) focus aligned with the variance-reduction objective. Weaknesses: (i) short data window => parameter uncertainty (29 belt observations; X-ray line 2 has 4); (ii) exponential service is a proxy (true belt times look lognormal, CV~0.48) — the DES would be more accurate with the fitted lognormal; (iii) the 200 pax/hr demand level and the 10-min patience are assumptions; (iv) no spatial/geometry effects (bin congestion, conveyor speed limits) are modeled; (v) abandonment behavior is not empirically grounded. Future work: fit arrival processes per airport and time-of-day (the data's burst CV2 suggests a Markov-modulated or Pareto-gap model per peak), replace exponential services with the fitted lognormals, add a geometry/belt-speed capacity model to bound belt stage service times, couple the DES to a live AOC feed for the M3 trigger, and run a cost model (officer-hours, equipment) to price M2/M3 against the capacity-expansion scenario as the TSA weighs the investment trade-off the problem highlights (the unknown cost of the 2016 O'Hare fixes).
