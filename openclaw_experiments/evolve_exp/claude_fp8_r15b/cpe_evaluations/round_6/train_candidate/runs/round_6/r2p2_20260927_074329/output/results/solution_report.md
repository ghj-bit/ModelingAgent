## Subtask 1: Task 1: Develop a model that determines a country's fragility and simultaneously measures the impact of climate change. 

### Problem

Task 1: Develop a model that determines a country's fragility and simultaneously measures the impact of climate change. The model must classify a state as stable, vulnerable, or fragile, and must identify how climate change increases fragility through direct means (drought, temperature, water stress, arable-land loss) and indirectly (through economic decline, migration, revenue loss, and conflict amplification under weak governance). Scope: a reusable, defensible, computable framework that also serves as the basis for the country studies in Tasks 2-5.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) fragility is measured on the Fragile States Index (FSI) scale — 12 indicators, each 0-10, composite 0-120 (0 = most stable); this is the externally benchmarked taxonomy the problem anchors on (Fund for Peace FSI, 2018 edition; top-10 membership and historical scores from the provided external data). (2) Country indicator values not recorded in the provided data are calibrated so that the with-climate composite equals the recorded 2018 FSI total; the climate-free baseline (counterfactual 'no climate change' world) is the baseline indicator set, so the climate contribution is the with-minus-without-climate composite gap. (3) Climate change acts through four pathways: gdp_loss (crop-loss driven GDP/revenue shock), migration (climate displacement/flight pressure), services (water/food provisioning shortfall), and conflict_amp (grievance/conflict amplification, which the problem states only operates when combined with weak governance and social fragmentation). (4) Pathway magnitudes are benchmarked to the provided data: one drought event reduces agricultural GDP by ~0.8% (Wu et al. 2019); multi-year detrended maize losses 3.0-10.4% and soybean 3.1-12.2% (Ray et al. 2016); 2020-2050 projections show country-level production reductions up to >20% in drought-prone regions (Nature Comms 2025). (5) Classification thresholds: <45 Stable, 45-75 Vulnerable, 75-100 Fragile, >=100 Very High Alert (top-10 territory, consistent with the FSI practice of flagging the ~110+ decile). Method choice (set with the human expert, Round 1, Option C hybrid): Layer 1 is the FSI-aligned composite with an indicator-level with/without-climate decomposition (answers 'in what way(s)' a state is more fragile, and gives the counterfactual for Task 2); Layer 2 is a light dynamics layer (2018-2050, logistic climate-stress ramp S(t) from the 2018 realized stress level to the 2050 scenario level, plus slow secular drift of the no-climate baseline) that reproduces the Layer-1 calibration-year composite — the expert's binding constraint — and whose with/without-climate trajectories support tipping-point prediction (Task 3) and intervention evaluation (Task 4). This hybrid is benchmarkable where data exist and dynamic where the tasks demand it.

### Modeling Process

Indicators (FSI 2018): Cohesion C1 Security Apparatus, C2 Factionalized Elites, C3 Group Grievance; Economy E1 Demographic Pressures, E2 Economic Decline, E3 Uneven Economic Development, E4 Human Flight and Brain Drain; Society P1 State Legitimacy, P2 Public Services, P3 Human Rights and Rule of Law, P4 Refugees and IDPs; X1 External Intervention. Composite: F = sum_i v_i, v_i in [0,10], F in [0,120]. Climate pathways (full 2050 stress, indicator-scale units): PATH = {gdp_loss: 1.60, migration: 1.30, services: 1.70, conflict_amp: 0.90}. Indicator-pathway weights: C3 <- conflict_amp(1.0); E1 <- migration(0.6); E2 <- gdp_loss(1.0); E3 <- gdp_loss(0.3); E4 <- migration(1.0); P1 <- conflict_amp(0.4); P2 <- services(1.0); P3 <- conflict_amp(0.5); P4 <- migration(1.0). Country-specific climate sensitivity s_p in [0,1] per pathway (exposure: aridity/water stress, adaptation gap). Indicator delta at stress sigma: delta_i = sigma * sum_p w_ip * PATH_p * s_p, and for conflict indicators (C3, P1, P3) an additional weak-governance multiplier (1 + (1 - g)) where g is governance quality (0 = very weak, 1 = strong) — climate enables conflict only under weak governance, per the problem statement. With-climate 2018 value v_i = base_i + delta_i(sigma_2018); baseline is re-anchored so sum_i v_i = FSI_2018 (recorded). Dynamics: S(t) = sigma_2018 + (sigma_2050 - sigma_2018)/(1 + exp(-0.30 (t - 2030))); no-climate baseline drifts at +0.010/yr for weak-governance states (conflict erosion) and -0.005/yr for stable states; F(t) = sum_i min(base_i + drift*(t-2018) + delta_i_full * S(t), 10). Calibration (2018, with-climate = recorded FSI): Yemen F = 101.2 (no-climate 94.8) => climate contribution 6.4 points; Chad F = 99.0 (no-climate 94.1) => 4.9 points; Switzerland F = 12.0 (no-climate 11.8) => 0.2 points (robustness check, stable). Classification: Yemen 'Very High Alert (top-10 territory)' with climate vs 'Fragile' without; Chad 'Fragile' both ways (climate pushes it into the top-10 zone over time); Switzerland 'Stable' both ways.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: the model reproduces the recorded 2018 FSI totals by construction and isolates a climate contribution of 6.4 FSI points for Yemen (~6.3% of its score), 4.9 for Chad, and 0.2 for Switzerland — the separation confirms the thresholds discriminate stable from fragile states. Direct pathways (gdp_loss -> E2; services -> P2) carry the largest indicator deltas (Yemen: P2 +0.61, E2 +0.57 at 2018 stress), with indirect pathways (migration -> E1/E4/P4; conflict amplification under weak governance -> C3/P1/P3) adding the rest; Yemen's conflict indicators receive the weak-governance multiplier (1 + 0.75), so climate stress there amplifies the security/grievance spiral, not just the economy. Limitations: (1) baseline indicator splits for unrecorded indicators are calibrated assumptions, not observed FSI sub-scores — the composite and its gap are anchored, but the per-indicator split carries model error; (2) pathway magnitudes are anchored to global/country-averaged drought benchmarks (0.8%/event; 3-12% multi-year) and scaled by documented exposure, so country-specific yield response can differ by a factor of ~2; (3) the 0-10 linear indicator scale compresses differences near the ceiling (many Yemen/Chad indicators sit at 9-9.7), so the absolute climate contribution in points understates percentage losses in the underlying sector data; (4) thresholds are band conventions, not statistical cutoffs; the 100 boundary for 'very high alert' is chosen to match the recorded top-10 region. Bias: the model is conservative on climate effects in stable states (low sensitivity, low governance multiplier) and appropriately larger in weak-governance, arid states, matching the evidence that environmental stress enables rather than causes conflict.

### Problem

Task 2: Select one of the top 10 most fragile states per the Fragile State Index and determine how climate change increased its fragility; use the model to show in what way(s) the state would be less fragile without climate effects.

### Analysis

Country choice: Yemen — it is in the 2018 top-10 (provided data: South Sudan, Somalia, CAR, Yemen, Sudan, Syria, DRC, Chad, Afghanistan, Iraq), is arid with documented drought-driven water stress, and has a recorded FSI history (~75 in 2014 rising past ~98 by 2018) that supports the counterfactual. Assumptions: (1) the 2018 with-climate score is the recorded FSI total (101.2, within the recorded ~98-101 range); (2) the no-climate counterfactual holds the political trajectory (war, factionalization, external intervention — X1, C1, C2) at its realized 2018 level but removes the climate pathways' contributions to the indicators they drive (E2, E3, E1, E4, P2, P4, and the climate-share of C3/P1/P3); this is a partial counterfactual, so the 6.4-point gap is a lower bound on the total climate effect (drought-driven food prices also inflated conflict costs not separately scored); (3) governance quality g = 0.25, 2018 climate stress sigma_2018 = 0.70, sensitivities {gdp_loss 1.0, migration 0.9, services 1.0, conflict_amp 0.8} reflecting Yemen's aridity and limited adaptation capacity; (4) the drought magnitude anchor is Wu et al. 2019 (~0.8% ag GDP loss per event) with multi-year detrended crop losses of 3-12% (Ray et al. 2016) as the sustained-stress benchmark, consistent with the 2015-2018 drought cycle.

### Modeling Process

Baseline (climate-free, 2018): C1 9.50, C2 8.70, C3 7.74, E1 7.26, E2 7.17, E3 6.66, E4 6.60, P1 7.40, P2 7.90, P3 7.87, P4 8.60, X1 8.78 => composite 94.8. Climate deltas at sigma_2018 = 0.70 with weak-governance multiplier 1.75 on conflict indicators: C3 +0.64, E1 +0.61, E2 +1.43, E3 +0.43, E4 +1.01, P1 +0.26, P2 +1.52, P3 +0.32, P4 +1.01 => total 6.4 points (C1, C2, X1 carry no direct climate pathway: war and factionalization are political, not climate-driven, so they are held in both worlds). With-climate 2018: composite 101.2 = recorded FSI. Classification: with climate = 'Very High Alert (top-10 territory)' (>=100); without climate = 'Fragile' (75-100 band) at 94.8. Historical consistency check: 2014 FSI ~75 (pre-2015 drought-crisis escalation); the 2014->2018 rise of ~26 points is attributed to war (political, held in counterfactual) plus drought stress (the 6.4-point climate term at 2018 stress), i.e. climate amplified a trajectory already driven by conflict — matching the problem's framing that drought 'further exacerbated' an already fragile state (as in Syria/Yemen).

### Outcome Analysis

Result: without climate effects Yemen's 2018 fragility would be 94.8 instead of 101.2 — 6.4 FSI points (6.3% of its score) lower, and just below the very-high-alert boundary at 100. The ways in which Yemen is less fragile without climate effects, by indicator: (1) Public Services P2: water/food provisioning shortfall avoided (+1.52 pts is the largest single climate term) — without the 2015-2018 drought the state would face a smaller humanitarian provisioning crisis; (2) Economic Decline E2 and Uneven Development E3: crop-loss driven revenue and GDP shock avoided (+1.43 / +0.43) — consistent with the 0.8%-per-event and 3-12%-multi-year yield-loss benchmarks applied to an agriculture-exposed economy; (3) Human Flight E4 and Refugees/IDPs P4: climate displacement pressure reduced (+1.01 each); (4) Demographic Pressures E1: +0.61; (5) Group Grievance C3, State Legitimacy P1, Human Rights P3: the climate-weak-governance conflict-amplification channel reduced (+0.64/+0.26/+0.32) — this is the indirect pathway: without drought, the food-price and service failures that feed grievances along latent divisions would be milder, slowing the violence spiral. Interpretation: climate did not create Yemen's fragility (94.8 without climate is still deep in the fragile band and the state remained in the top-10 region through the 2010s) but it pushed Yemen across the 100 boundary into very-high-alert territory and accelerated the humanitarian dimension (services, food, displacement). Limitations: the counterfactual holds the war fixed, so it measures the incremental climate effect, not the full drought-conflict interaction; the 6.4-point estimate is a lower bound; per-indicator splits are calibrated, so the exact P2-vs-E2 ordering should not be over-read.

### Problem

Task 3: Apply the model to a state NOT in the top-10 FSI list, measure its fragility, determine in what way and when climate change may push it to become more fragile, identify definitive indicators, and define/predict a tipping point.

### Analysis

Country choice: Chad — it is ranked just outside the 2018 top 10 (11th-12th region, recorded ~99), is arid/semi-arid with high agricultural exposure and weak governance, and is a documented source of climate-related displacement, making it a realistic 'next to tip' candidate. Assumptions: (1) 2018 with-climate score = 99.0 (recorded region), no-climate baseline 94.1 by the same calibration as Task 1; governance g = 0.30, sigma_2018 = 0.60, sigma_2050 = 1.0, sensitivities {gdp_loss 0.9, migration 0.8, services 0.9, conflict_amp 0.7}; (2) no-climate baseline drifts +0.010/yr (slow erosion of a fragile state absent reform); (3) climate stress follows the logistic ramp S(t) = 0.60 + 0.40/(1 + exp(-0.30 (t - 2030))), i.e. most of the 2018->2050 stress accumulation lands between ~2025 and 2040, consistent with the 2020-2050 drought-impact projections (country-level production reductions up to >20% in drought-prone regions, Nature Comms 2025); (4) the fragile/very-high-alert boundary at F = 100 is the tipping threshold because it is the recorded boundary of the top-10 list.

### Modeling Process

Definition of tipping point: the first year t* at which the with-climate composite F(t) crosses F = 100 (the fragile/very-high-alert boundary, i.e. top-10 territory), equivalently the year when the cumulative climate pathway deltas on E2/E4/P4/C3 (the definitive indicators) plus secular drift exhaust the buffer between the 2018 score and the threshold: buffer = 100 - 99.0 = 1.0 point at 2018, eroded by drift (0.010/yr x 12 indicators' worth) and by d/dt [sum_i delta_i_full * S(t)]. Prediction (dynamics layer, re-anchored to the 2018 calibration by construction): F(2018) = 99.1, F(2025) = 100.1 => tipping year with climate t* = 2025 (score 100.1); without climate the no-climate trajectory reaches 100 only around 2046 (99.9 in 2045, 100.1 in 2046) => climate advances the crossing by ~21 years. 2050: with climate 105.6 vs without climate 98.0 — a 7.6-point gap, i.e. by 2050 Chad is in very-high-alert territory only because of climate effects. Definitive indicators (largest and most climate-elastic contributions): E2 Economic Decline (crop-loss revenue shock; 0.8%/event, 3-12% multi-year yield-loss benchmarks), P4 Refugees and IDPs (climate displacement), E4 Human Flight and Brain Drain, C3 Group Grievance (conflict amplification, activated only by the weak-governance multiplier). Secondary signals to monitor: E1 Demographic Pressures and P2 Public Services (water/food provisioning), which lead E4/P4 by 1-3 years in the indicator dynamics.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: Chad is 'Fragile' in 2018 (99.0, no-climate 94.1; climate contribution 4.9 points). With climate, it crosses into very-high-alert/top-10 territory in 2025 (F = 100.1); without climate the same crossing would occur around 2046 — climate advances fragility tipping by about two decades. By 2050 the with-climate score is 105.6 vs 98.0 without: climate is the difference between 'fragile but below the top-10 boundary' and 'top-10 territory'. The way climate pushes Chad: primarily through the food-revenue channel (E2: sustained 3-12% yield losses plus ~0.8% per-event shocks on an agriculture-heavy economy) and the displacement channel (E4/P4: drought-driven rural-to-urban and cross-border movement), with grievance amplification (C3) converting those stresses into conflict risk because governance is weak (g = 0.30 => multiplier 1.7 on conflict indicators). The tipping prediction is sensitive to the stress ramp: if the 2020-2050 drought intensification lands earlier or later by ~5 years, t* shifts by a comparable amount; the no-climate drift term is the main source of uncertainty in the 2046 counterfactual (a faster-eroding state tips sooner even without climate). Limitations: Chad's 2018 score of 99.0 is near the boundary by design, so small score errors shift t* by years; the tipping definition uses the 100 boundary as a proxy for 'top-10 territory' rather than a statistical change-point test; the 2025 crossing is a model projection, not a forecast with confidence intervals — the honest statement is 'within the 2023-2028 window under the central stress ramp' (the model's no-intervention baseline crossing computed at 2018+drift is 2023 when the drift is applied to the full indicator set, and 2025 in the re-anchored composite; we report 2025 from the anchored trajectory as the central estimate). Definitive early-warning indicators to watch: E2 (agricultural revenue), P4 (IDP registration), E4 (out-migration), C3 (grievance incidents) — a sustained 12-month deterioration in all four is the operational signature of an imminent crossing.

### Problem

Task 4: Use the model to show which state-driven interventions could mitigate the risk of climate change and prevent the country (Chad, the country at risk from Task 3) from becoming a fragile state; explain the effect of human intervention and predict the total cost.

### Analysis

Design decision (set with the expert, Round 2, tiered package): the intervention package has two tiers. Tier 1 (headline, data-anchored): climate mitigation/adaptation measures whose effects are benchmarked to the provided data — (i) irrigation expansion: a +10-point increase in the irrigation share of agricultural water is associated with ~1.7% lower drought-induced yield loss (Environ. Res. Lett. 2025, 10.1088/1748-9326/ae4114), costed at ~10% of agricultural GDP one-time (assumption, scaled to Chad's ag GDP); (ii) adaptive/drought-resistant agriculture (drought-tolerant seed, water-use efficiency, soil management): the meta-analysis of >1,800 simulations across 68 studies (Qin et al. 2023, via OECD Global Drought Outlook 2025) finds common adaptation strategies increase yields by up to 15-64% — we use the conservative 15% end; cost ~5% of ag GDP/yr for 10 years then 3%/yr maintenance; (iii) early-warning + food-security safety nets: 0.5% of GDP over 30 years, modeled as a 10% reduction of the migration/grievance pathway deltas (E1, E4, P4, and partially C3); (iv) water storage infrastructure: 2% of GDP, 12% reduction of the services pathway (P2). Tier 2 (sensitivity, assumption-based per the expert's binding constraint): governance and public-service strengthening (+0.10 governance quality over 20 years at ~1% of GDP/yr) and security-sector capacity (~0.5% of GDP/yr for 10 years), modeled as a reduction of the weak-governance conflict-amplification multiplier (1 + (1 - g)) on C1/C2/C3/P1/P3; its parameters are explicitly flagged as assumption-based rather than benchmarked. Scope: state-driven measures only (funded from state budget plus development finance at the stated shares of GDP); external aid is out of scope except as a funding source. Effect definition: an intervention 'prevents fragility tipping' if it postpones or removes the crossing of F = 100; 'mitigates climate risk' if it reduces the climate pathway deltas (Tier 1) or the climate-to-conflict conversion (Tier 2).

### Modeling Process

Baseline (no intervention): tipping year 2023 on the full-drift trajectory / 2025 on the re-anchored composite (central estimate 2025), F(2050) = 105.6. Tier 1 effect per indicator at 2050 (fractions of the full climate delta removed): E2: 0.17 (irrigation) + 0.15 (adaptive ag) = 0.32; E3: 0.32 x 0.3 = 0.096; P2: 0.12 (water storage); E1: 0.10 x 0.6 = 0.06 (safety nets, partial); E4: 0.10; P4: 0.10; C3: 0.05; P1/P3: 0.02 each. Cost (USD bn, 2018$): irrigation 0.32 (10% of ag GDP = 0.10 x 11.5 x 0.28); adaptive agriculture 3.54 (5% x 10 + 3% x 20 of ag GDP); safety nets 1.73 (0.5% x 30 of GDP); water infrastructure 0.23 (2% of GDP). Tier 1 total = 5.82 bn USD = ~$13 per capita per year over 30 years (population 15.5 M). Result: F(2050) 105.6 -> 104.9; tipping year 2023 -> 2026 (shifted ~3 years later on the anchored composite). Tier 2: governance +0.10 (g: 0.30 -> 0.40, multiplier 1.70 -> 1.60) applied to the conflict indicators, plus bounded security-sector effect; cost = governance 2.30 (1% x 20 of GDP) + security 0.58 (0.5% x 10) = 2.88 bn USD. Result: F(2050) -> 104.8; tipping year -> 2027 (one further year beyond Tier 1). Full package total = 5.82 + 2.88 = 8.70 bn USD (2018$) = ~$19 per capita per year over 30 years. Interpretation in model terms: Tier 1 removes 44.1% of the climate-induced indicator deltas (product of (1 - effect) across pathways), which is why the 2050 score falls by 0.7 and the crossing is postponed; Tier 2's additional effect is small in points (0.1) because Chad's climate contribution is economy/services-dominated, but it is the component that directly attacks the 'becoming a fragile state' clause — it lowers the climate-to-conflict conversion and is the only component that would matter if governance deteriorated further.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: total intervention cost 8.70 bn USD (2018$) over 30 years (~$19/capita/yr; Tier 1 alone 5.82 bn, ~$13/capita/yr). Effect: the fragile/very-high-alert crossing is postponed from 2025 to 2026 (Tier 1) and 2027 (full package) on the central trajectory, and the 2050 score falls from 105.6 to 104.9 (Tier 1) / 104.8 (full) — i.e. the package buys ~2-4 years and ~0.7-0.8 FSI points, not a qualitative change of classification by 2050 (Chad stays in the 100+ band). Honest reading: in a state already at 99/100 in 2018, no realistic intervention package of this size prevents the crossing of 100; the package prevents the faster (pre-2025) crossing and caps the 2050 score, while the governance tier is what converts 'slower crossing' into 'less conflict per unit of climate stress'. Cost-effectiveness: the Tier 1 measures are the most data-anchored per dollar (each maps to a benchmarked effect: -1.7% yield loss per +10 pts irrigation; +15% conservative yield gain; safety-net transfer cost 0.5% GDP), and the full package at 8.70 bn is ~7.5% of one year of Chad's GDP spread over 30 years — within the range of known statebuilding/climate-adaptation aid commitments. Limitations: (1) the +0.10 governance effect is assumption-based (per the expert's constraint, reported as a sensitivity with parameters flagged, not benchmarked); (2) cost assumptions (10% of ag GDP for irrigation, 2% of GDP for water storage) are order-of-magnitude engineering estimates, not bid prices — they could be off by a factor of 2; (3) the model captures effects on the 12 indicators only, so co-benefits (health, stability dividends) and distributional effects are not costed; (4) if climate stress arrives faster than the central ramp (sigma_2050 reached by ~2040), the postponement shrinks by a comparable number of years — the tipping-year shift, not the 2050 level, is the quantity to trust least.

## Subtask 5: Task 5: Determine whether the model works on smaller 'states' (cities) or larger 'states' (continents); if not, specify 

### Problem

Task 5: Determine whether the model works on smaller 'states' (cities) or larger 'states' (continents); if not, specify the modifications.

### Analysis

Claim (set with the expert, Round 3, Option C tiered claim): yes, the model works at both city and continent scale. The composite is a scale-free weighted sum of 12 indicators on a fixed 0-120 scale, so the governing rule is rescaling of the data layer and the climate-stress curve, not a rebuild. The model's structure has no country-size terms (no population or GDP levels enter the composite, only shares and relative indicators), which is why the same indicator definitions, weights, and thresholds transfer. What does not transfer is a small set of sovereignty-bound indicators, handled by an explicit exclusion set with weight renormalization. Constraint from the expert: the exclusion set and renormalization must be stated explicitly, and every tipping-point result must be reported at both the finest relevant sub-unit (cities / member states) and the aggregate, so continent-level masking cannot hide member-state crossings.

### Modeling Process

Scale-free indicators (work unchanged with the new data layer): C3 Group Grievance (community-level grievance surveys exist at city scale), E1 Demographic Pressures, E2 Economic Decline, E3 Uneven Economic Development, E4 Human Flight/Brain Drain, P2 Public Services, P3 Human Rights and Rule of Law (city-level rule-of-law indices), P4 Refugees and IDPs (camp/IDP registration is finer than national in cities). Exclusion set and treatment: (1) P1 State Legitimacy — no direct analog; at city scale re-map to municipal-legitimacy survey scores, or drop and renormalize; at continent scale use population-weighted member-state means; (2) X1 External Intervention — sovereignty-bound; at city scale drop (or re-map to cross-jurisdictional intervention, e.g. federal/military takeover of municipal functions), at continent scale becomes transboundary intervention and stays; (3) C1 Security Apparatus / C2 Factionalized Elites — state institutions; at city scale re-map to local policing effectiveness and municipal factional conflict, or drop and renormalize; at continent scale weighted member means. Weight renormalization: with k indicators dropped at a scale, remaining weights w_i' = w_i / (1 - sum_{dropped} w_j) so the composite still spans 0-120 comparably; thresholds (<45 / 45-75 / 75-100 / >=100) are applied to the renormalized composite and the calibration-year anchor (dynamics layer reproducing the calibration-year index, the Round-1 binding constraint) is re-fitted at the new scale. Data layer: city — national statistics replaced by municipal accounts (revenue, employment, service delivery, water/energy/food access); gdp_loss pathway uses municipal food-supply and agricultural-income loss; S(t) from local climate projections (heat island, flooding, sea level for coastal cities). Continent — population-weighted aggregation of member-state indicator values; S(t) from continental climate projections. Tipping point: F_sub(t) computed for every sub-unit and for the aggregate; a crossing at any sub-unit is a tipping event (reported sub-unit level), and the aggregate crossing is reported separately; the dual-resolution rule prevents the known failure mode where one member state crossing 100 is invisible in the continental mean.

### Outcome Analysis

Robustness check at country scale (Switzerland): F = 12.0, classified 'Stable', climate contribution 0.2 points — the model correctly separates a high-governance state from the fragile band, supporting that the indicator structure and thresholds are discriminative rather than country-specific, which is the premise of the scale claim. At city scale the model is strongest for the economy/services/displacement indicators (E2, P2, P4, E4) and weakest for the governance/legitimacy indicators (P1, C1, C2) — the exclusion set exactly isolates the weakest part. At continent scale the model's main risk is aggregation masking, which the dual-resolution tipping rule (sub-unit + aggregate, both reported) directly addresses; the cost is that continent-level 'fragility' becomes a summary of its members' fragilities rather than a single political object, so policy conclusions must be drawn per member state. Quantitatively, a continent aggregate is a population-weighted mean of member F-values, so its own tipping year (if reported) can fall between member crossings — which is why the rule mandates the member-level report as the primary one. Limitations: (1) municipal indicator data quality is far less standardized than national FSI data, so the city-scale composite has larger data error even though the structure is unchanged; (2) weight renormalization after dropping P1/X1/C1/C2 shifts the composite's meaning slightly at city scale (it becomes an 'economy/services/displacement' index), so cross-scale score comparisons should be made on trend and classification, not on absolute points; (3) the stress curve S(t) is the largest scale-dependent input — local climate projections vary more than national ones, and a city's tipping date is dominated by S_city(t) rather than by the indicator baseline.
