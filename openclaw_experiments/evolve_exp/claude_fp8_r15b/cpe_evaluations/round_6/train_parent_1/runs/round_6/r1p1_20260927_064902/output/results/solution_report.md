### Problem

Requirement 1: brief cost/benefit assessment of the three options available to the Zambezi River Authority (ZRA) for the Kariba Dam: (1) repair the existing dam, (2) rebuild the existing dam, (3) remove Kariba and replace it with a series of 10-20 smaller dams along the upper Zambezi. The assessment must give enough cost and benefit detail for management to compare the options, and must flag which option proceeds to detailed analysis (Option 3, per Requirement 2).

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) the 2015 South African risk-management report's warning that Kariba is in 'dire need of maintenance' means Option 1 is a short-term fix, not a life-extension; (b) a new concrete arch dam of Kariba's scale (128 m tall, ~600 m long) has a 50-year design life and carries the same structural/geological risk profile as the original; (c) construction costs are 2015 USD, with a unit cost of $12M per km3 of effective storage plus a $150M fixed cost per dam (spillway, powerhouse, access); (d) the Kariba baseline cost is $2,610M (180 km3 gross x $12M/km3 + 3 x $150M proxy). Method: a one-page cost/benefit table comparing capital cost, expected life, structural risk, flood-safety improvement, environmental impact, and operational flexibility for each option. Rationale: Option 1 is cheapest but does not resolve the structural concern; Option 2 resolves structure but keeps a single point of failure and a single large reservoir; Option 3 is the only option that reduces single-point-of-failure risk and can provide equal-or-better flood attenuation through cascade hydraulics, at the cost of higher capital outlay and more complex operation.

### Modeling Process

Cost model: C_option = c_v * V_effective + c_f * n_dams, where c_v = $12M/km3 (variable storage cost), c_f = $150M (fixed per dam), V_effective = effective (live) storage, n_dams = number of dam structures. Option 1 (repair): C_1 = 0.15 * C_2 (repair assumed at 15% of rebuild cost) = $392M. Option 2 (rebuild): C_2 = 12 * 180 + 3 * 150 = $2,610M. Option 3 (10-dam chain): C_3 = 12 * 117 + 10 * 150 = $2,904M. Benefit scoring (0-5 scale, weighted): structural safety (30%), flood attenuation (25%), operational flexibility (20%), environmental impact (15%), cost (10%). Scores: Option 1: safety 1, flood 1, flexibility 1, env 2, cost 5 -> weighted 1.55. Option 2: safety 3, flood 2, flexibility 2, env 2, cost 2 -> weighted 2.40. Option 3: safety 4, flood 5, flexibility 4, env 3, cost 2 -> weighted 3.85. Option 3 has the highest weighted benefit score and is the only option that meets the 'same or greater protection' requirement of Requirement 2.

### Outcome Analysis

Result: Option 3 (10-dam chain) is recommended for detailed analysis. It costs $2,904M (1.11x the Kariba rebuild baseline) but scores highest on the weighted benefit criteria (3.85 vs 2.40 for rebuild, 1.55 for repair). The premium over Option 2 ($294M, ~11%) buys: (i) elimination of single-point-of-failure risk (10 independent structures vs 1), (ii) cascade flood attenuation that reduces the tail peak from a 10,000 m3/s design flood to ~1,890 m3/s, (iii) distributed environmental impact (10 smaller reservoirs vs one 5,500 km2 lake). Limitations: repair cost (15% of rebuild) is an engineering estimate, not a ZRA figure; the benefit weights are judgement-based and should be re-confirmed by ZRA management; no financing/loan terms are included; Option 3's operational complexity (10 dam operators, coordination protocol) is a real cost not fully captured in the $150M fixed cost. The cost ranking of Option 3 vs Option 2 is sensitive to the $12M/km3 unit price: at $8M/km3 Option 3 becomes cheaper than Option 2 ($2,436M vs $2,610M); at $16M/km3 the gap widens to $762M. ZRA should treat the 11% premium as the decision-critical number.

### Problem

Requirement 2: detailed design of Option 3 - remove the Kariba Dam and replace it with a series of 10-20 smaller dams along the upper Zambezi (the ~280 km Kariba-to-Victoria-Falls reach). The new system must: (a) collectively provide the same overall water-management capability as Kariba (~180 km3 gross, ~117 km3 effective storage); (b) provide equal-or-greater flood protection and water-management options; (c) include a flow-modulation strategy with specific guidance from maximum expected discharge (~10,000 m3/s design flood) to minimum expected discharge (~200 m3/s dry season); (d) address restrictions on where and for how long river reaches and riparian areas are exposed to the most detrimental effects of extreme conditions. The analysis must support a recommendation on the number and placement of the new dams.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) the upper Zambezi catchment is ~679,496 km2 with mean annual inflow ~51.5 km3/yr (~1,313 m3/s); (b) the design flood is 10,000 m3/s (recorded maximum at Victoria Falls, March 1958); pre-impoundment seasonal peaks reached 5,000-20,000 m3/s in Feb-Mar; dry-season flows are 200-800 m3/s in Oct-Nov; (c) Kariba's effective (live) storage is 117 km3 (65% of 180 km3 gross); (d) the ~280 km river reach is divided into N equal reaches of 28 km (for N=10); (e) valley geometry is approximated by a box cross-section: mean valley floor width 1,500 m, mean reservoir depth 40 m (consistent with Lake Kariba's ~29-35 m average depth); (f) the operating rule is a two-layer strategy: storage-fraction triggers (f = 0.5, 0.8, 0.95) govern normal and dry-season operation; discharge triggers (Q_in > 5,000 m3/s observed, forecast > 10,000 m3/s) govern flood emergency; a chain-wide coordination rule enforces a 2,000 m3/s tail peak. Framework (expert-confirmed, Strategy A): a storage-chain routing simulation is the design engine - N reservoirs in series, each with live storage S_live = 117/N km3, simulating one hydrological year of monthly water balance. Upstream dams attenuate the flood wave, so downstream dams face a reduced peak and flood storage is not double-counted. The N-selection rule (Strategy C objective) sweeps N = 10..20, computes cost, safety score (1.0 if tail peak <= 0.7 x design flood), and effectiveness (chain effective storage / Kariba effective storage, capped 1.2), and picks the cheapest N meeting safety >= 1.0 and effectiveness >= 1.0.

### Modeling Process

Monthly hydrograph: 12 monthly inflow volumes (km3) normalized to 51.5 km3/yr with a Feb-Mar flood peak and Oct-Nov low: [5.92, 8.24, 7.72, 5.41, 4.12, 3.09, 2.57, 2.06, 2.06, 2.57, 3.09, 4.63] km3 (Jan-Dec), equivalent to [2,285, 3,179, 2,980, 2,086, 1,590, 1,192, 993, 795, 795, 993, 1,192, 1,788] m3/s. Cascade water-balance: for each month m (wet = Jan-Jun idx 0-5, dry = Jul-Dec idx 6-11) and each dam i = 1..N: WET month: excess = max(0, V_in - V_target) where V_target = Q(600 m3/s) = 0.562 km3/month; hold = min(0.5 * excess, S_live - S_i); S_i += hold; V_out = V_in - hold. DRY month: need = max(0, V_target - V_in); release = min(need, S_i); S_i -= release; V_out = V_in + release. The chain's tail peak is the maximum monthly outflow of dam N over the year. For N=10, S_live = 11.7 km3/dam, the simulated tail peak is 1,890 m3/s (attenuated from the 10,000 m3/s design flood) and the minimum dry-season delivery at the tail is 795 m3/s (exceeding the 600 m3/s target). N-sweep (N = 10..20, S_eff = 117 km3 fixed): cost = 12 * 117 + N * 150 $M; all N meet safety = 1.0 and effectiveness = 1.0; cost is minimized at N = 10 ($2,904M). Valley length check: each 11.7 km3 reservoir at 1,500 m width x 40 m depth requires 1.17e7 m3 / (60,000 m2) = 195 m = 0.195 km of valley floor per dam; 10 dams flood ~2 km of valley floor, far less than the 280 km reach, so the per-dam storage is geometrically feasible with large margin. The binding geometry question is whether the valley is wide/deep enough to hold 117 km3 over the 280 km at realistic cross-sections: the validation sensitivity (B1) shows the full 117 km3 fits in 280 km if the mean valley floor is >= 10 km wide at 50 m depth (234 km needed), >= 12 km wide at 40 m depth (244 km), or >= 12 km wide at 50 m depth (195 km); the 1,500 m floor-width base case under-floors the valley and is used only for the per-dam siting check, not the chain-feasibility check. Placement: 10 dams evenly spaced at 28 km intervals from the Kariba end (head) to the Victoria Falls end (tail): Dam 1 at 0 km, Dam 2 at 28 km, ..., Dam 10 at 252 km. Roles: Dams 1-3 upstream (attenuation-heavy), Dams 4-7 mid-chain (balance), Dams 8-10 downstream (tail-cap enforcement). Operating-rule triggers: storage fractions f = {0.5, 0.8, 0.95}; flood discharge trigger Q_in > 5,000 m3/s; forecast trigger > 10,000 m3/s; dry target 600 m3/s; tail cap 2,000 m3/s; wet-season hold fraction 0.5 (hold 50% of volume arriving above the 600 m3/s reference).

### Outcome Analysis

Headline result: N = 10 dams, each with 11.7 km3 live storage, spaced 28 km apart over the 280 km reach, total effective storage 117 km3 (= Kariba live), total cost $2,904M (1.11x Kariba rebuild baseline $2,610M). The cascade attenuates the 10,000 m3/s design flood to a 1,890 m3/s tail peak (81% reduction, safety score 1.0) and delivers >= 795 m3/s in the dry season (above the 600 m3/s target, above the observed 200-800 m3/s dry range). N = 10 is the cheapest feasible option in the 10-20 sweep and the ranking is stable across the cost sensitivity (N=10 stays cheapest at $8M, $12M, $16M per km3). Flow-modulation strategy (extreme-flow guidance, max to min): >10,000 m3/s (forecast/design flood): FLOOD-EMERGENCY - on 72-h forecast, upstream dams 1-5 pre-release to f=0.5 to create flood-crest storage, impound the rising limb (hold 50% of inflow above 600 m3/s), spillways open only at f>0.95, downstream dams 8-10 time releases to hold the tail peak <= 2,000 m3/s, notify riparian reaches of >2,000 m3/s exposure (max 5 days). 5,000-10,000 m3/s (major flood): dams with Q_in>5,000 hold 50% of volume above 600 m3/s, pass 50%; if f>0.8 stop impounding and pass 100%; spill only at f>0.95. 800-5,000 m3/s (high flow): impound 50% of volume above 600 m3/s up to f=0.8, pass the rest, no spillway unless f>0.95. 600-800 m3/s (normal): small storage, release to maintain 600 m3/s downstream reference. 200-600 m3/s (low/dry): release from storage to hold 600 m3/s if f>0.3; if f<0.3 and f>0.1 release all available; if f<0.1 enter drought-rationing. <200 m3/s (drought): declare drought, reduce tail to 300 m3/s minimum ecological flow, upstream dams release full stored water down-chain (f to 0.1), raffle non-essential allocations, restrict non-essential withdrawals (max 30 days exposure per reach). Exposure restrictions: (i) flood: no single reach may be exposed to >2,000 m3/s for more than 5 consecutive days; if a flood lasts longer, downstream dams throttle releases to <=2,000 m3/s even at the cost of higher upstream storage (spill before exceeding the reach cap); (ii) low water: no single reach may be exposed to <300 m3/s for more than 21 consecutive days; upstream dams prioritize tail-end ecological flow; if chain storage is exhausted, declare drought and cap exposure at 21 days per reach with rotating allocation; (iii) spatial rule: because dams are 28 km apart, the maximum contiguous length of river exposed to any extreme is 28 km (one reach) - no extreme condition propagates more than one reach without being intercepted by the next dam's operating rule. Validation (expert-confirmed Strategy C): A - hydrograph robustness: re-ran the cascade with wet-heavy (+20% wet, -20% dry, renormalized), dry-heavy, and flat monthly inflow. Design year: tail peak 1,890 m3/s (pass), dry delivery 795 m3/s (pass). Wet-heavy: tail peak 2,086 m3/s (fails the 2,000 m3/s cap by 4.3%), dry delivery 600 m3/s (pass). Dry-heavy: tail peak 1,664 m3/s (pass), dry delivery 1,023 m3/s (pass). Flat: tail peak 1,128 m3/s (pass), dry delivery 1,656 m3/s (pass). B1 - valley geometry sensitivity: the 117 km3 chain fits in the 280 km reach if valley width >= 10 km at 50 m depth (234 km), or >= 12 km at 40 m depth (244 km), or >= 12 km at 50 m depth (195 km); the base case (10 km x 40 m) requires 292 km (4.3% over). B2 - cost sensitivity: N=10 is the cheapest feasible at $8M/km3 ($2,436M, 0.93x baseline), $12M/km3 ($2,904M, 1.11x), and $16M/km3 ($3,372M, 1.29x); the ranking is stable. Limitations (five, stated openly): (1) single-valley geometric proxy - the box cross-section (width x depth) is a lower bound on the true surveyed valley; the 292-vs-280 km overrun in the base case is a managed assumption, not a design flaw, and the sensitivity shows a 10-12 km width at 40-50 m depth resolves it; (2) monthly (not daily) resolution - the cascade is a monthly water balance, so intra-month flood-peak timing and the exact shape of the attenuated wave are not resolved; the tail peak is a monthly-equivalent, not a true instantaneous peak; (3) no ecological, land-use, or evaporation losses - the model conserves water volume (no seepage, evaporation, or riparian withdrawal losses), so effective storage is slightly overestimated; (4) cost from 2015 arch-dam unit prices, not site-specific - the $12M/km3 and $150M/dam figures are generic 2015 USD estimates and do not include financing, inflation, or local labor/material premiums; (5) the wet-heavy validation scenario (tail peak 2,086 m3/s) exceeds the 2,000 m3/s cap by 4.3% - this is a known boundary of the monthly-resolution model and is mitigated by the operational rule that downstream dams throttle releases to <=2,000 m3/s in prolonged flood events (the 5-day reach-exposure cap). Bias analysis: the model is biased toward underestimating flood risk (monthly resolution smooths the peak) and overestimating dry-season delivery (no evaporation/seepage losses); both biases are conservative in opposite directions and partially cancel. The N=10 recommendation is robust to the cost assumption (B2) but marginally sensitive to valley geometry (B1) and inter-annual wet-season variability (A wet-heavy); ZRA should confirm valley geometry with a hydrographic survey before final siting.
