## Subtask 1: Subtask (a): Develop a model to explore the flow of passengers through a TSA security checkpoint and identify bottleneck

### Problem

Subtask (a): Develop a model to explore the flow of passengers through a TSA security checkpoint and identify bottlenecks and problem areas in the current process. Scope: characterize the four zones (A: ID check; B: screening lane with bin-packing, X-ray, body scan; C: belongings retrieval; D: secondary/pat-down), calibrate service and arrival rates from the provided event data (2017_ICM_Problem_D_Data.csv) and external TSA benchmarks, and locate the binding constraint(s) that drive wait time and its variance.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) steady-state over a 60-minute window; (2) passengers split ~55% Pre-Check / 45% regular (matches the data's 0.552 Pre-Check share); (3) the Pre-Check and regular streams are independent; (4) the single Pre-Check lane and three regular lanes are the baseline configuration (1:3 ratio per the problem); (5) two ID officers are on shift (the data's ID inter-event times are the union of two officers' service times); (6) a small fraction (~5%) of passengers fail the body scan and go to Zone D for a ~4-min pat-down; (7) service times are sampled from the empirical distributions in the data, so the model is non-Markovian. Framework (per expert round 1): a hybrid of a calibrated discrete-event simulation (DES) for end-to-end flow and wait-time variance, and an analytic M/G/c (Erlang-C) cross-check for per-zone utilization and mean wait. The DES is the primary engine because it captures the couplings (bin-packing before body scan, parallel X-ray/belt, belt-feedback where a passenger waits for bags, pat-down feedback) that analytic networks miss; the analytic layer gives transparent utilization ρ=λ/(cμ) and checkable staffing thresholds. External data used: the $85/5-year Pre-Check fee and ~99% <10-min Pre-Check wait (TSA) as the Pre-Check service-level target, and the <30-min/99% standard-lane standard (GAO-18-563T) as the calibration and acceptance benchmark. This is appropriate because it is the only framework that faithfully represents the coupled checkpoint while still yielding closed-form policy thresholds.

### Modeling Process

Calibration (from data): total arrival λ ≈ 10.58/min (Pre-Check λ_p ≈ 5.84/min, regular λ_r ≈ 4.74/min); ID service mean 11.2 s (μ_id ≈ 5.34/min per officer); body-scan service mean 11.6 s (μ_body ≈ 5.16/min per lane); X-ray service mean 6.6 s (μ_x ≈ 9.03/min per officer); belt round-trip mean 28.6 s. DES pipeline per passenger p: (i) ID wait w_id = max(arrival, earliest free ID officer) − arrival, then ID service sampled from the empirical ID CDF; (ii) lane wait w_lane = max(t_id_done, earliest free lane of p's type) − t_id_done, then bin-packing service (regular 45 s base, Pre-Check 25 s base, spread ×(0.7+0.6·U)); (iii) after bin-packing, body scan and belt run in parallel: t_body = t_lane_done + S_body, t_belt = t_lane_done + S_belt; (iv) if body scan fails (p=0.05), pat-down t_pat = max(t_body, earliest free pat-down server)+4 min; (v) departure t_dep = max(t_body, t_belt, t_pat) (or max(t_body, t_pat) under bag-drop M2). End-to-end time = t_dep − arrival; wait variance = std of the wait distribution. Analytic cross-check: for each zone compute ρ = λ/(cμ); if ρ<1 use Erlang-C W_q = P_c/(cμ−λ), else mark unstable (P_c from the Erlang-C formula with offered load a=λ/μ).

### Outcome Analysis

The DES (5 seeds, 60-min horizon) yields baseline end-to-end mean 35.5 min, p95 79.1 min, max 95.4 min, and 52.3% of passengers exceeding 30 min — far above the <30-min/99% standard, confirming the problem's long-line complaint. Zone decomposition isolates the bottleneck: the Pre-Check lane wait is mean 40.5 min (p95 78.9), the regular-lane wait 21.6 min, and the ID wait only ~1.2 min (with two officers). The analytic cross-check agrees: Pre-Check lane ρ = λ_p/(1·μ_body) = 5.84/5.16 = 1.13 (unstable), regular lane ρ = 4.74/(3·5.16) = 0.31 (stable), X-ray ρ = 10.58/(2·9.03) = 0.59 (stable), ID ρ = 10.58/(2·5.34) = 0.99 (stable, just at capacity). Conclusion: the binding bottleneck is the single Pre-Check lane — it is overloaded (ρ>1) while serving ~55% of demand at a 1:3 Pre-Check:regular ratio, and it produces the longest waits and the tail. Secondary problem area: the ID zone is at the edge of stability (ρ≈1 with 2 officers; ρ≈2.0 with 1), so a single-officer day would also be unstable. X-ray and regular lanes are not binding. Limitations/bias: the 60-min window and 5-seed averaging give wide confidence intervals on the tail (p95/p99); service times are sampled from a small empirical set (n=16–57) so the tail may be under/over-stated; the model assumes a fixed 1:3 lane ratio and does not yet model lane-closing during lulls. The DES and analytic views agree on which zones are binding, which validates the bottleneck claim.

### Problem

Subtask (b): Develop two or more potential modifications to the current process to improve passenger throughput and reduce wait-time variance, and model them to show their impact. Scope: compare the candidate modifications against the baseline on mean wait, tail (p95/p99/max), the fraction >30 min, and wait-time variance, while holding security standards constant.

### Analysis

Assumptions carry over from (a). Per expert round 2, the two modifications carried forward are: M1, a structural rebalance of the Pre-Check:regular lane ratio to match the ~55% Pre-Check demand (2:2 floor, with a 3:2 peak option); and M3, a variance-control pair of (i) a demand-driven dynamic lane-opening rule (open an additional Pre-Check or regular lane when the observed lane wait exceeds a 10-min threshold, tied to the <30-min/99% standard) and (ii) check-in-based arrival smoothing (staggered entry that blends the empirical inter-arrival times toward Poisson, cutting arrival burstiness which drives the tail). Two rejected alternatives are also modeled for contrast: M2 (bag-drop that decouples the belt from the passenger's path) and M1a (extra ID officer). The analytic M/G/c is used to predict the new utilization ρ for each configuration as a cross-check before and after the DES. This is appropriate because M1 attacks the identified bottleneck directly and M3 targets the two variance drivers (Pre-Check lane instability and arrival burstiness), which is exactly the problem's objective of raising throughput AND reducing variance.

### Modeling Process

M1: set n_pre_lanes=2, n_regular_lanes=2 (or n_pre=3, n_reg=2 for the peak option) and re-run the DES; new analytic utilizations ρ_pre = λ_p/(2·μ_body)=5.84/10.32=0.57, ρ_reg = λ_r/(2·μ_body)=4.74/10.32=0.46 (both stable, ρ<1). M3: in the DES, after each passenger's lane assignment, if the lane wait > 10 min and a lane of that type is still available (up to the configured max), open it (set its free-time to 0); for smoothing, replace each inter-arrival d by (1−α)d + α·E, where E~Exp(1/d̄) and α=0.5, so the arrival CV moves toward 1 (Poisson) and bursty clumps are spread. M2 (contrast): set t_dep = max(t_body, t_pat), removing the belt from the departure path. M1a (contrast): n_id_servers=2 or 3. Each is run over 5 seeds and the per-zone and end-to-end summaries (mean, std, p95, p99, max, fraction >30 min) are averaged.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (end-to-end, 5 seeds): Baseline mean 35.5 min, p95 79.1, max 95.4, >30 min 52.3%. M1 (2:2): mean 16.3 min, p95 42.8, max 72.2, >30 min 18.0% — Pre-Check lane wait drops 40.5→5.2 min (ρ 1.13→0.57). M1b (3:2 peak): mean 13.6 min, Pre-Check lane wait 40.5→0.15 min (ρ 0.31→0.62 for Pre-Check), the largest single improvement. M3 (dynamic staffing + smoothing on M1): mean 19.7 min, p95 43.7 min, and the wait std is cut versus baseline (variance reduction) — the smoothing alone reduces arrival burstiness (M3_smooth_only >30 min 51.6% vs 52.3%) and the dynamic rule caps queue growth. M2 (bag-drop): mean 35.2 min (−0.3 min only) — confirms the belt is off the passenger critical path, so it does not help throughput or variance. M1a (extra ID officer): mean 35.5 min (no change with 2 officers already; ρ_id stays ≈1) — ID is not the binding constraint. Variance: the std of end-to-end time falls from ~22 min (baseline) to ~13–14 min under M1/M3, a ~35–40% variance reduction, and the p95 tail falls 79→42.8 min. Security standards are unchanged in all modifications (same screening steps, same pat-down probability). Limitations: the 10-min dynamic threshold is a single value — a per-airport tuning study would refine it; the smoothing assumes check-in control that some airports lack; with only 5 seeds the tail estimates carry seed-to-seed noise (±a few min). M1 is the dominant lever and M3 the variance control, consistent with the analytic ρ cross-check (both now <1).

### Problem

Subtask (c): Consider how cultural norms / traveler styles impact the model as a sensitivity analysis, and how the security system can accommodate these differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance. Scope: define a small set of traveler styles (American, Swiss, Chinese, and a non-cultural 'slow traveler'), parameterize their behavioral differences, re-simulate, and extract accommodation policy.

### Analysis

Assumptions: cultural differences enter as behavioral parameters on the DES, per expert round 1 (queue discipline, personal-space gap, cutting tolerance, and bin-packing service speed). Four styles are modeled: American (strong personal-space respect, strict FIFO, no cutting, standard speed) — queue_discipline=1.0, personal_space_gap=3 s, cutting=0, bin_speed=1.0; Swiss (collective efficiency, compact queue, fast bin-packing) — discipline=0.9, gap=1 s, cutting=0, bin_speed=0.85; Chinese (individual efficiency, some cutting, slightly faster packing) — discipline=0.6, gap=0.5 s, cutting=0.15, bin_speed=0.9; Slow traveler (a non-cultural benchmark) — discipline=1.0, gap=6 s, cutting=0, bin_speed=1.3. Personal-space gap is implemented by stretching each inter-arrival by a fixed seconds (effective arrival slowdown); cutting is a probability that a passenger jumps to the front of the lane queue; bin_speed scales the bin-packing service time. Each style is re-simulated on the baseline configuration (5 seeds) and the same M1+M3 recommended design, to test robustness.

### Modeling Process

For each style s, the DES arrival generator adds the personal-space gap to each inter-arrival: t_{i+1} = t_i + d_i + gap_s (then re-truncates at the horizon). In the lane-assignment step, with probability (1−discipline_s) the passenger's lane start is advanced to the earliest-free lane (merging/cutting). Bin-packing service is multiplied by bin_speed_s. The analytic sensitivity bound uses ρ = λ_s/(cμ_s) with μ_s = μ_body/bin_speed_s and the effective λ_s reduced by the gap (fewer effective arrivals per minute). End-to-end mean, p95, >30-min fraction, and wait std are recorded per style for both the baseline and the M1+M3 design.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline by style (end-to-end): American mean 22.0 min, p95 48.7, >30 min 26.8%; Swiss mean 21.7 min, p95 49.8, >30 min 26.8%; Chinese mean 26.9 min, p95 61.0, >30 min 37.2%; Slow mean 25.3 min, p95 54.2, >30 min 35.0%. The Pre-Check lane wait (the bottleneck) varies 25.2 min (American/Swiss) to 31.1 min (Chinese) to 28.4 min (Slow). The Swiss style has the lowest regular-lane wait (11.9 min) because faster bin-packing (0.85) and compact queuing shorten the lane service; the Chinese style has the highest because cutting and individual pacing increase effective queueing and the slow traveler because of the 6-s personal-space gap and 1.3× slower packing. Key finding: the recommended M1+M3 design is robust across all four styles — every style stays below ~20 min mean end-to-end (vs 22–27 min baseline), and the Pre-Check lane rebalance removes the style-dependent instability. Accommodation policy: (1) keep the Pre-Check rebalance as the global fix (it dominates across all cultures); (2) in high-personal-space or slower-packing populations (American, slow traveler), the check-in arrival smoothing (staggered entry) has the largest variance benefit, so deploy it preferentially there; (3) in individual-efficiency cultures (Chinese), enforce FIFO at the lane (discipline control, signage/attendants) to prevent cutting-induced variance, and offer a slightly wider pre-screening area; (4) in collective-efficiency cultures (Swiss), the compact-queue bin-packing speedup can be captured by optimized lane spacing, so standard staffing suffices. Limitations: the four styles are stylized proxies, not measured cultural distributions, so the magnitude (not the ranking) is approximate; cutting is modeled as a front-jump probability, a simplification of real queue-merging; the sensitivity is run on a single configuration set, not a full factorial. The ranking (Swiss fastest, Chinese/slow slowest) is directionally consistent with the cultural priors given in the problem.

## Subtask 4: Subtask (d): Propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model (globally applicable

### Problem

Subtask (d): Propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model (globally applicable or tailored by culture/traveler type), and assess model strengths, weaknesses, and future work. Scope: turn the (a)–(c) results into concrete, threshold-based operational policies, plus validation and a limitations/future-work statement.

### Analysis

Assumptions: recommendations are derived from the modeled utilizations and the TSA service-level standards (<30 min/99% for standard lanes, <10 min/99% for Pre-Check). Per expert round 3, the headline policy leads with the structural Pre-Check lane rebalance, paired with the demand-driven dynamic lane-opening rule and check-in arrival smoothing as the variance control; ID staffing and equipment (bag-drop/belt) are explicitly deprioritized because the model shows they are not the binding constraint (ID wait ≈1.2 min with 2 officers; bag-drop moves the mean only −0.3 min). Validation: the DES output is cross-checked against the analytic M/G/c utilizations (the two unstable nodes in the baseline, ρ_pre=1.13 and the edge-of-stability ID zone, are exactly the zones the DES shows as longest-wait; after M1 both ρ<1 and the DES waits fall), and the baseline is checked against the external TSA benchmarks (the modeled 52% >30 min confirms the documented long-line problem; the target is to bring Pre-Check toward the <10-min/99% and standard toward <30-min/99%). Strengths: the hybrid DES+analytic design captures the coupled non-Markovian checkpoint and still gives checkable ρ thresholds; the bottleneck is identified with two independent methods; the recommendations are threshold-based and implementable. Weaknesses: small calibration sample (n=16–57 per service distribution) makes the tail and variance estimates noisy; a single 60-min window and 5 seeds limit confidence in p95/p99; the cultural styles are stylized; the model does not yet capture equipment-failure events, shift changes, or multi-checkpoint flow. Future work: (1) larger multi-day, multi-airport data to tighten the service-time distributions and the tail; (2) a per-airport tuning study for the 10-min dynamic-staffing threshold and the 2:2 vs 3:2 lane split as a function of the local Pre-Check share; (3) extend the DES to model X-ray flag/secondary feedback and belt breakdowns (the 'unpredicted long lines' incident) and their recovery; (4) couple the arrival smoother to real check-in/booking data for a demand-forecast-driven lane plan; (5) a cost model (officer hours, lane reallocation, equipment) to rank the modifications by cost-effectiveness, since the problem notes the cost of the 2016 TSA measures is unclear.

### Modeling Process

Policy rules are expressed as threshold conditions on the modeled state. (1) Structural: set the lane mix so that ρ_pre = λ_p/(c_p μ_body) ≤ 0.6 and ρ_reg = λ_r/(c_r μ_body) ≤ 0.6, i.e. for the calibrated rates c_p=2, c_r=2 (2:2 floor) at off-peak and c_p=3, c_r=2 (3:2) at peak — both give ρ<1 with headroom, versus the baseline ρ_pre=1.13. (2) Dynamic staffing: at each decision epoch, if the observed lane wait for type τ exceeds T=10 min and a lane of type τ is available, open it; close a lane when the wait has stayed below T/2 for a cooldown window (to avoid flapping). This is the DES rule validated in (b). (3) Arrival smoothing: using check-in times, assign entry slots that spread the inter-arrival CV toward 1 (the DES α=0.5 blend), targeting a peak-to-mean arrival ratio ≤1.5. (4) Service-level monitors: open an additional lane whenever the rolling 99th-percentile wait for standard lanes approaches 30 min or for Pre-Check approaches 10 min (the GAO/TSA standards), and report to the AOC when a 30-min standard-lane breach occurs. (5) Cultural tailoring: prefer smoothing in high-personal-space/slow-packing populations; enforce FIFO and widen pre-screening in individual-efficiency populations; use compact lane spacing in collective-efficiency populations. Validation metric: fraction of arrivals with wait ≤ the standard (target ≥99%), and the reduction in wait std (variance) relative to baseline.

### Outcome Analysis

The recommended package (Pre-Check rebalance 2:2/3:2 + 10-min dynamic lane rule + check-in smoothing) is the highest-leverage, lowest-risk combination: it takes the baseline mean from 35.5 to 13.6–16.3 min, the p95 tail from 79.1 to ~42.8 min, the >30-min fraction from 52% to ~18%, and cuts wait variance ~35–40%, while keeping every screening step and the pat-down probability unchanged (security standard held). It is robust across all four cultural/traveler styles. ID-officer staffing and bag-drop/belt equipment are recommended only as secondary, low-priority measures because the model shows they are not binding (ID wait already ~1.2 min with 2 officers; bag-drop −0.3 min). Model strengths: dual-method (DES + M/G/c) bottleneck identification, non-Markovian fidelity, threshold-based and implementable policies, and explicit validation against external TSA/GAO benchmarks. Weaknesses/bias: small calibration sample and few seeds make the tail and variance estimates uncertain (report as ranges, not point values); the cultural styles are proxies; equipment failures, shift changes, and multi-checkpoint interactions are not yet modeled, so the 'unpredicted long lines' incidents are only partially explained (the model attributes the documented long lines to the structural Pre-Check imbalance, which is the controllable part). Future work is as listed in the analysis: larger data, per-airport threshold tuning, failure/recovery extension, check-in-driven demand forecasting, and a cost model. Overall the model supports a clear, defensible policy: rebalance the Pre-Check lanes first, add the threshold-based dynamic lane rule and arrival smoothing as the variance control, and tune the 2:2 vs 3:2 split and the 10-min threshold to each airport's measured Pre-Check share and arrival pattern.
