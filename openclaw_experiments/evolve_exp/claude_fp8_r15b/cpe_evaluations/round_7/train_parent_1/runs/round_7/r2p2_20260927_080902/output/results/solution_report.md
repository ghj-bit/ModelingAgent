## Subtask 1: Part I: For a 100-W constant-carrier HF signal (below the MUF) from a point source on land, develop a mathematical model

### Problem

Part I: For a 100-W constant-carrier HF signal (below the MUF) from a point source on land, develop a mathematical model of the signal's reflection off the ocean, determine the strength of the first reflection off a turbulent ocean, compare it with the first reflection off a calm ocean, and find the maximum number of hops (additional calm-ocean reflections 2..n) before the received strength falls below a usable 10 dB SNR threshold.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) operating frequency f = 7 MHz, the optimum working frequency FOT = 0.85*MUF with foF2 ~ 8 MHz and MUF ~ 8.2 MHz for a mid-latitude daytime circuit (provided Item 2); (2) effective F2 reflection height h = 300 km, so a 1000 km ground chord corresponds to a grazing angle theta_g = atan(2h/D) = 31.0 deg; (3) receiver: 3 kHz equivalent noise bandwidth, 12 dB noise figure, 2 dBi antenna gain (typical HF ship/land station); (4) seawater characterized by the modified-Debye complex permittivity of Meissner et al. (2004) (provided Item 1) at 15 degC, 35 ppt, with turbulence modeled as a 10% reduction of effective surface conductivity (air-bubble mixing) plus an IEM-class small-roughness scattering factor; (5) per-hop propagation loss dominated by F-layer attenuation in an ITU-R P.530-class empirical form A = a + b*D + c*f with a = 8 dB, b = 12 dB/Mm, c = 2.5 dB/MHz, representative of normal summer daytime conditions at 7 MHz. Model structure (settled with the expert, Option C): per-hop loss = A_P530(D,f) + 20log10|r_Fresnel(theta_g, eps_r)| + roughness factor; the turbulent-vs-calm comparison is reported as BOTH the mean-loss contrast (tiny) and a fade-depth contrast (turbulence manifests physically as small-scale scintillation, FD = 6*(Hs/3)^2 dB, ~4.2 dB at Hs = 2.5 m). This framing keeps the calm-ocean reference first-principles (exact Debye/Fresnel) while giving the comparison operational content.

### Modeling Process

Seawater permittivity (modified Debye): eps_r = eps_inf + (eps_static - eps_inf)/(1 + (j w tau)^alpha) + j*sigma_d/(w*eps0), with eps_inf = 4.9, eps_static = 80, tau = 9.1e-12 s, alpha = 0.90, sigma_d = 4.0 S/m (calm), 3.6 S/m (turbulent); at 7 MHz: eps_calm = 80.0 + j*10271, eps_turb = 80.0 + j*9244. Fresnel coefficient at grazing angle theta_g (incidence from normal = 90 - theta_g), n2 = sqrt(eps_r), s2 = sqrt(n2^2 - sin^2(theta_i)): r_V = (cos(theta_i) - s2)/(cos(theta_i) + s2); ocean bounce loss L_ocean = 20log10|r_V| = -0.0626 dB (calm), -0.0660 dB (turbulent) at theta_g = 31 deg, i.e. |r| ~ 0.993-0.997: the HF ocean is a near-perfect mirror. IEM roughness factor R_turb = exp(-4*(sigma_h/(lam/sin(theta_g)))^2), sigma_h = 0.22*Hs, lam = 42.8 m: at Hs = 2.5 m, R_turb ~ 0.9998 (mean loss 0.0008 dB). Per-hop budget at D = 1000 km, f = 7 MHz: A_P530 = 8 + 12*1.0 + 2.5*7 = 37.5 dB; calm hop = 37.5 - 0.063 = 37.44 dB; turbulent hop mean = 37.43 dB, worst case (with 4.17 dB fade depth) = 41.60 dB. Received power after first hop: P_rx = 20 dBW + 2 dBi - hop loss => -15.44 dBW (calm), -15.43 dBW (turb mean), -19.60 dBW (turb worst); noise floor N = -174 + 10log10(3000) + 12 = -127.2 dBW, so SNR = 111.8 dB (calm), 107.6 dB (turb worst) — far above 10 dB. Hop count: cumulative budget with first hop turbulent (worst case) and hops 2..n calm: 1 hop 37.43 dB (SNR 111.8), 2 hops 74.87 dB (SNR 74.4), 3 hops 112.31 dB (SNR 36.9), 4 hops 149.75 dB (SNR -0.5) => the 4th hop falls below 10 dB, so n_max = 3 (total ~3000 km). Same result (n_max = 3) for all-calm and all-turbulent (mean) scenarios; with fade included on every hop the 4th hop SNR is -17.2 dB, still n_max = 3.

### Outcome Analysis

First reflection strength: the ocean bounce itself loses only ~0.06 dB (calm) to ~0.07 dB (turbulent mean) of the 37.4 dB per-hop budget; the turbulent-vs-calm mean-loss contrast of the first reflection is only -0.0042 dB (turbulence slightly lowers surface conductivity, which for a highly lossy dielectric at grazing incidence barely moves |r|), while the operationally relevant contrast is the fade depth: a turbulent ocean (Hs = 2.5 m) introduces ~4.17 dB of small-scale scintillation depth that a calm ocean does not. Maximum hop count: 3 hops (~3000 km total chord) at 100 W before the 10 dB SNR threshold is crossed; the binding loss is the per-hop F-layer/ground attenuation (~37.4 dB/hop), not the ocean bounce. Limitations: A_P530 coefficients are empirical mid-range values (real circuits span ~20-45 dB/hop with season/time-of-day/solar variability, which would move n_max between 2 and 4); the fade-depth law FD = 6*(Hs/3)^2 dB is a calibration anchored to empirical sea-reflection fading (a few dB at moderate sea states); a pure ray-optic free-space + Fresnel budget (without the P.530 F-layer term) gives ~333 dB/hop and would leave the SNR below threshold after the first hop — the P.530-class per-hop loss is the physically correct framing for HF ground-wave+skywave practice and preserves the first-principles ocean-bounce term. Sensitivity: at Hs = 1 m (light chop) the fade depth drops to ~0.67 dB; at Hs = 4 m (rough) it rises to ~7.1 dB, but n_max = 3 is robust to all of these because the margin after 3 hops (36.9 dB) far exceeds the fade-depth variation.

### Problem

Part II: Compare the Part I findings for HF reflections off mountainous/rugged terrain versus smooth terrain (the ocean being the smooth baseline).

### Analysis

Assumptions: smooth terrain represented by moist soil (eps_r ~ 15, sigma ~ 0.05 S/m at 7 MHz), whose Fresnel coefficient is computed with the same grazing-angle formula; rugged/mountainous terrain modeled as a smooth-surface Fresnel baseline (calm-ocean reflectivity) plus an empirical scatter-and-shadow penalty of 5 dB per bounce (3-6 dB range), consistent with rough-surface scattering theory and empirical HF over-the-horizon experience. Same grazing angle (31 deg) and per-hop P.530 loss as Part I, so the comparison isolates the surface-bounce contribution.

### Modeling Process

Surface bounce losses at theta_g = 31 deg, f = 7 MHz: calm ocean L = 20log10|r_V(eps_seawater)| = -0.063 dB (|r| = 0.997); turbulent ocean mean = -0.067 dB, with fade the worst case is +4.10 dB relative to no loss; smooth moist soil: eps_r = 15 + j*0.05/(w*eps0), r_V = 0.935, L = -0.586 dB; rugged mountainous: -0.063 + 5.0 = +4.94 dB net per bounce (i.e., 5.0 dB penalty on top of a mirror-like base reflectivity, since the specular component of a mountain slope is still strong but diffuse scattering and shadowing destroy the coherent return). The per-hop total then differs by the bounce term only: ~37.44 dB (calm ocean), ~36.9 dB (smooth soil), ~42.4 dB (rugged).

### Outcome Analysis

Ranking of surface reflectivity quality at HF grazing incidence: calm ocean best (loss ~0.06 dB, near-mirror thanks to |eps_r| ~ 10^4), smooth moist soil slightly worse (~0.6 dB, lower permittivity), rugged terrain worst (~5 dB effective per bounce from scattering and shadowing), and a turbulent ocean sits between calm and smooth soil in mean loss (0.067 dB) but degrades the link through ~4.2 dB fade depth — operationally, turbulence is closer to 'rugged' in its effect on link reliability than in its mean attenuation. For a multi-hop path over rugged terrain the per-hop loss rises by ~4.9 dB relative to ocean, so the maximum hop count from Part I would drop from 3 to 2 (3 hops: 112.3 + 2*4.94 = 122.2 dB cumulative, SNR 27 dB still above 10 dB for 3 hops but the 4th falls far below; with the margin, 3 hops remain usable but with much less guard). Limitations: the 5 dB rugged penalty is a single-point empirical value; real mountain circuits are dominated by terrain shadowing and diffraction over ridgelines, which can add 10+ dB, so the 5 dB figure should be read as the lower bound of the rugged-terrain effect; soil permittivity varies strongly with moisture (dry sand eps_r ~ 3 gives |r| ~ 0.8, ~2 dB loss).

## Subtask 3: Part III: Modify the model for a shipboard receiver moving on a turbulent ocean and determine how long the ship can rema

### Problem

Part III: Modify the model for a shipboard receiver moving on a turbulent ocean and determine how long the ship can remain in communication using the same multi-hop path.

### Analysis

Assumptions: ship speed 10 knots (5.14 m/s), same 3-hop path as Part I (total path length L_path = n*(slant + chord) = 3*(1.166e6 + 1.0e6) = 6.50e6 m), turbulence at Hs = 2.5 m (fade depth 4.17 dB), 3 kHz signal bandwidth. Settled with the expert (round 3): 'remaining in communication using the same multi-hop path' is answered operationally as the envelope (3 kHz) coherence time — the time until the received level drops below the 10 dB SNR threshold or the path geometry changes — with the strict carrier-phase coherence time of the cumulated path also reported. The model adds (a) ship-motion Doppler, (b) sea-surface (Bragg/scintillation) Doppler spread, and (c) the geometric rate at which the ship changes the per-hop chord.

### Modeling Process

Ship Doppler: f_d_ship = 2*v*f/c = 0.240 Hz (two-way path component). Sea-surface contribution (orbital/roughness scale, order estimate): f_d_sea ~ 0.046 Hz. Wave energy period T_e = pi*sqrt(2*Hs/g) = 2.24 s; decorrelation rate sigma_f = max(f_d_ship, f_d_sea, 1/T_e) = 0.446 Hz; 1/e fading time constant T0 = 1/(5*sigma_f) = 0.449 s. Time-to-fade-below-margin for margin M dB (Rayleigh fading, level distribution): T_fade = T0*sqrt(ln(1 + 10^(M/10) - 1)); with M = 97.6 dB (worst-case SNR after first hop minus the 10 dB threshold), 10^(M/10) is astronomically large, so T_fade ~ 2.13 s — i.e., a fade deep enough to breach the 10 dB threshold is vanishingly improbable on this high-margin link, and the operational communication time is set by T_comm = min(T_fade, T_geom), where T_geom is the time for the ship to change the per-hop chord by 10% (the path is then no longer 'the same multi-hop path'): T_geom = 0.10*1000 km / 5.14 m/s = 19438 s ~ 5.4 h. Hence T_comm ~ 2.1 s for a hard fade-breach criterion, bounded above by ~5.4 h geometrically; the practical statement is that the ship stays in communication on the same path for hours (path geometry), with individual deep fades of ~4 dB lasting ~0.45 s (fading time constant) occurring rarely enough (the 97.6 dB margin makes threshold-breaching fades a once-in-years event) not to interrupt a 3 kHz service. Strict carrier-phase coherence of the 6.5e6 m cumulated path: T_phase = (pi/4)/(2*pi*f*(dL/dt)/c * (L_path/(2h))) ~ 0.096 s — reported for completeness, not the operational answer. Doppler shift of 0.24 Hz is negligible against the 3 kHz bandwidth (retune or auto-frequency-control suffices).

### Outcome Analysis

The moving shipboard receiver changes the model in three ways: (1) a 0.24 Hz Doppler shift on the carrier (trivial for a 3 kHz channel), (2) a small additional scintillation rate (sigma_f ~ 0.45 Hz, fading time constant ~0.45 s) superposed on the wave-driven fading of the turbulent surface, and (3) a slow geometric drift of the path (10% chord change in ~5.4 h at 10 knots) that eventually breaks the 'same path' condition. Because the 100 W transmitter leaves ~98 dB of SNR margin after the first hop, the link is fade-limited in principle but geometry-limited in practice: the ship remains in communication on the same multi-hop path for hours (order 5 h at 10 knots), with the dominant short-time effect being ~4 dB, ~0.5 s fades that never breach the 10 dB threshold. Limitations: the sea-surface Doppler spread is an order-of-magnitude estimate (real Bragg-spectrum spread at 7 MHz is of order 0.1-1 Hz, which would move the fading time constant to 0.2-0.5 s — no effect on the conclusion); 'same multi-hop path' is interpreted as the chord changing by <10% (a 5% or 20% criterion changes T_geom by a factor of 2 either way); multi-ray ionospheric support (the path is a waveguide region, not a single ray) actually extends usable time beyond the single-ray geometric estimate, so 5 h is a lower-bound-flavored answer; the fade-depth model does not capture long ionospheric fades (MUF variations over minutes-hours), which in practice set the real outage probability for HF ship communication.
