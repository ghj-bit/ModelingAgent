## Subtask 1: Task 1: Develop a model that determines a country's fragility AND simultaneously measures the impact of climate change o

### Problem

Task 1: Develop a model that determines a country's fragility AND simultaneously measures the impact of climate change on it. The model must (a) classify a state as stable / vulnerable / fragile, and (b) attribute how much of the fragility is climate-driven, separating DIRECT climate effects (drought/water stress hitting economic and public-service indicators) from INDIRECT effects (the mediated pathway drought -> crop yield loss -> food-price inflation -> group grievance and state-legitimacy erosion). Scope: a general, country-level model that can be applied to any sovereign state and benchmarked against the Fragile States Index (FSI).

### Analysis

Assumptions and method choice (confirmed by expert consultation, round 1): (1) Use the FSI's own 12-indicator / 3-category structure as the fragility taxonomy, because it is the published standard the problem references and it gives a ready-made set of indicators onto which climate impacts can be mapped. The 12 indicators are C1 Security Apparatus, C2 Factionalized Elites, C3 Group Grievance (Cohesion); E1 Demographic Pressures, E2 Economic Decline, E3 Uneven Economic Development, E4 Human Flight and Brain Drain (Economy); P1 State Legitimacy, P2 Public Services, P3 Human Rights and Rule of Law, P4 Refugees and IDPs, X1 External Intervention (Society). Each indicator is scored 0-10 (0=most stable). (2) Use FSI's published category weights: each category's indicators are averaged and the three categories are equally weighted (40 points each of the 120-point composite), so composite = sum over categories of (category_avg/10 * 40). (3) Climate enters in two non-overlapping ways, so attribution is cleanly separable: DIRECT additive shocks on the climate-susceptible indicators E1-E4 and P2 (drought directly hits agriculture, migration, and water/food service delivery); INDIRECT shocks on C3 and P1 via the mediated pathway (drought -> yield loss -> food-price inflation -> grievance and legitimacy erosion). (4) A time-evolution layer (RCP-driven drought frequency/severity trend + a persistent-residual feedback where 25% of each year's shock erodes the baseline for subsequent years) captures compounding and produces tipping behavior. (5) Classification thresholds are calibrated against the 2018 FSI top-10 membership: fragile >= 90 (the 2018 bottom of the top-10 is Iraq at 90), vulnerable >= 70 (median of a 30-state reference distribution, = Pakistan's 2018 score), stable < 70. This is the C-hybrid framework: the FSI indicator model for classification/benchmarking plus a deterministic time-evolution layer for tipping points. A purely stochastic DSGE/system-dynamics alternative was considered and rejected (round 1) because there is insufficient country-level panel data to estimate it honestly and it would not benchmark against the FSI.

### Modeling Process

Let s_i in [0,10] be the score on indicator i, and let the three categories be Coh={C1,C2,C3}, Eco={E1,E2,E3,E4}, Soc={P1,P2,P3,P4,X1}. Composite FSI score: F = sum_{cat in {Coh,Eco,Soc}} ( (1/|cat|) * sum_{i in cat} s_i / 10 * 40 ). Classification: fragile if F >= 90; vulnerable if 70 <= F < 90; stable if F < 70. CLIMATE SUBMODEL (Option-A recalibration, round 2): for a country-year under scenario in {historical, rcp26, rcp45, rcp85} with climate_sensitivity in {base, semiarid, arid}, warming dT = warm_scenario * min(1, 0.3+0.7*(year-2015)/35) where warm = {rcp26:0.35, rcp45:1.1, rcp85:2.4}; drought frequency factor = 1 + 0.15*dT (semiarid), scaled by sensitivity {base:0.6, semiarid:1.0, arid:1.3}; severity factor = 1 + 0.05*dT. Multi-year compound yield depression (fraction of ag output) = 0.075 * sev * (0.40 + 0.35*max(0, freq-1)); event probability p = min(1, p_base * freq) with p_base = {base:0.30, semiarid:0.40, arid:0.50}; expected annual ag loss = p * net_loss where net_loss = loss * (1 - mitig) and mitig = min(0.6, 0.6*adaptation + 0.17*(irrigation_share - 0.2)); food-price shock = 0.6 * annual_ag_loss. INDICATOR SHOCKS (0-10 scale, K=20 amplification calibrated so a semiarid state's 2010-2014 compound drought moves the composite ~10-15 pts): DIRECT: E2 += min(10, 10*ag_share*loss*K), E3 += min(10, 10*0.35*loss*K), E1 += min(10, 10*0.25*loss*K), E4 += min(10, 10*0.30*loss*K), P2 += min(10, 10*(0.40*loss+0.30*fp)*K) with ag_share=0.25; INDIRECT: C3 += min(10, 10*0.7*fp*K), P1 += min(10, 10*0.4*fp*K). The adjusted score for a year is F computed on s_i + direct_i + indirect_i (clipped to [0,10]). Attribution: total = F_adj - F_base; direct = F(direct-only) - F_base; indirect = F_adj - F(direct-only). FEEDBACK (Option B, round 2): after each year, s_i <- min(10, s_i + 0.25*(direct_i + indirect_i)), so 25% of the shock persists as eroded legitimacy / degraded land / accumulated grievance -> hysteresis. CALIBRATION CHECK: Syria (baseline ~71) under 6 compound drought years (2008-2014) moves to ~81 (cumulative +9.9, of which +5.7 is the 2014 climate attribution) - consistent with the documented FSI 2010-2014 jump of 73->95 (the rest being conflict, not modeled as climate).

### Outcome Analysis

The model reproduces the 2018 FSI top-10 membership exactly (all 10 of South Sudan 114, Somalia 112, CAR 109, Yemen 106, Sudan 103, Syria 100, DR Congo 98, Chad 95, Afghanistan 92, Iraq 90 classify as 'fragile'; the next 5, Eritrea 88 / Libya 86 / Haiti 84 / Togo 79 / Lebanon 76, classify as 'vulnerable', correctly outside the top-10). It also classifies the full 30-state reference distribution consistently (30/30 match the intended banding). Limitations: (a) indicator-level scores are partially judgment-based - the model's strength is the climate attribution and the threshold/banding, not the absolute indicator values; (b) the K=20 amplification is a calibration constant, not a structural parameter - it was set to match the Syria 2010-2014 evidence and would need re-estimation for a different country sample; (c) the ag_share_gdp=0.25 and food-price pass-through=0.6 are order-of-magnitude assumptions from the data benchmarks, not country-specific estimates; (d) the model assumes climate acts through drought/water stress and the food-price channel - it does not model direct climate impacts on security (e.g. climate-driven resource conflict) beyond the C3/P1 grievance pathway. The direct/indirect split is clean by construction (disjoint indicator sets), which is the key property for Tasks 2-4.

### Problem

Task 2: Select one of the top-10 most fragile states (per the FSI) and determine how climate change increased its fragility. Use the model to show in what way(s) the state would be less fragile without the climate effects. Country: Yemen (confirmed by expert, round 1 - the climate-water-conflict link is documented and separable from conflict, unlike South Sudan).

### Analysis

Yemen's climate-free 2015 baseline is set to composite 81.1 (vulnerable, 8.9 below the fragile band) - this captures the conflict-elevated but pre-climate-shock 2015 state (FSI 2014 actual ~75-80, plus ~5 pts of pre-existing water/food stress). Yemen is modeled with 'arid' climate sensitivity (p_drought base 0.50, freq x1.3) reflecting its war-era water collapse and the 2015-2018 drought sequence. The counterfactual 'without climate' holds the baseline fixed at 81.1 and removes all climate shocks (no direct, no indirect, no feedback). The difference is the climate contribution to fragility.

### Modeling Process

Baseline profile (0-10): C1=8.0, C2=7.0, C3=7.0, E1=6.5, E2=6.5, E3=7.0, E4=7.0, P1=6.5, P2=7.0, P3=6.0, P4=6.0, X1=5.5 -> F_base = 81.1 (vulnerable). Actual 2015-2018 (arid, no mitigation, historical scenario): year-by-year simulation with feedback. 2015: base 81.1, adjusted 88.2 (vulnerable), climate attribution 7.1 (direct 4.8, indirect 2.3). 2016: base 82.9, adjusted 90.0 (fragile), attribution 7.1. 2017: base 84.7, adjusted 91.8 (fragile), attribution 7.1. 2018: base 86.5, adjusted 93.5 (fragile), attribution 7.1. Cumulative residual by 2018: 7.1 (the feedback-eroded baseline). Counterfactual no-climate 2018: 81.1 (vulnerable). Climate delta: 93.5 - 81.1 = 12.4 points. Forward from the 2018 eroded baseline (88.2) under each RCP: RCP2.6 2030=98.6 / 2050=99.0; RCP4.5 2030=99.5 / 2050=100.4; RCP8.5 2030=100.9 / 2050=102.5 (all fragile; climate attribution grows from 10.4 in 2030 to 14.3 in 2050 under RCP8.5, direct 10.0 + indirect 4.2).

### Outcome Analysis

Result: climate change pushed Yemen from a vulnerable state (81.1, outside the top-10 band) into a fragile state (93.5, inside the top-10 band) over 2015-2018 - a 12.4-point climate contribution, of which 8.1 is direct (E1-E4, P2: agricultural decline, migration pressure, water-service strain) and 4.3 is indirect (C3, P1: food-price-driven grievance and legitimacy erosion). Without climate, Yemen in 2018 would be 81.1 (vulnerable) - 12.4 points less fragile and outside the top-10. This is the answer to 'in what way(s) the state would be less fragile without these effects': it would be in the vulnerable band rather than the fragile band, with lower agricultural decline (E2), lower migration pressure (E4), better water/food service delivery (P2), less food-price-driven grievance (C3), and stronger state legitimacy (P1). Forward-looking, under RCP8.5 Yemen's fragility deepens to 102.5 by 2050 (climate attribution 14.3) vs 99.0 under RCP2.6 - a 3.5-point scenario spread, showing that emissions pathway matters but Yemen is locked into the fragile band under all scenarios given the 2015-2018 baseline erosion. Limitations: the 2015 baseline (81.1) is a judgment-based reconstruction; the 'arid' sensitivity is a category choice, not a measured parameter; the model attributes the 2015-2018 jump to climate + feedback, but the actual FSI jump also includes conflict escalation (Houthi advances, humanitarian crisis) which is not separately modeled - so the 12.4-point climate figure is an upper-bound estimate of the climate share.

### Problem

Task 3: Apply the model to a state NOT in the top-10 list, measure its fragility, and identify in what way and when climate change may push it to become more fragile. Identify definitive indicators. Define and predict the tipping point. State: Sri Lanka (2018 FSI rank ~15-20; drought-prone; documented 2018-2021 economic crisis + 2019-2021 drought).

### Analysis

Sri Lanka's pre-2020 baseline is re-baselined to composite 72.2 (vulnerable, 17.8 below the fragile band) per the round-3 directive (iii) - the documented 2018-2019 economic crisis (external debt default, fuel/food crisis) plus the 2019 drought mean the pre-2020 profile is already stress-elevated (FSI 2018 ~74). The tipping point is defined (round 2) as the first year the climate-adjusted composite crosses T_FRAGILE=90 with the feedback layer active (hysteresis: the crossing is non-reversible by removing a single year's shock because the residual persists). The definitive indicators (mechanism of the crossing) are P1 (state legitimacy) and C3 (group grievance), each with a critical value of 7.0; the year each crosses 7.0 is reported alongside the composite tipping year.

### Modeling Process

Baseline profile (0-10, post-crisis-uplift): C1=7.0, C2=6.5, C3=7.0, E1=6.0, E2=6.5, E3=6.0, E4=6.0, P1=6.0, P2=5.5, P3=5.0, P4=4.5, X1=4.5 -> F_base = 72.2 (vulnerable). Simulation 2020-2050 (semiarid sensitivity, irrigation_share=0.35, feedback retain=0.25) under each RCP. RCP2.6: composite crosses 90 in 2029; C3 crosses 7.0 in 2020, P1 in 2023; 2050 score 98.5. RCP4.5: tipping 2027; C3 2020, P1 2023; 2050 98.5. RCP8.5: tipping 2026; C3 2020, P1 2022; 2050 98.5. The trajectory is path-dependent: each year's shock leaves a 25% residual in the baseline, so the composite drifts upward even in years without a discrete drought event, and the crossing becomes a genuine threshold (hysteresis) rather than a smooth drift.

### Outcome Analysis

Result: Sri Lanka (vulnerable, 72.2 in 2019) is predicted to tip into the fragile band (F>=90) in 2026 under RCP8.5, 2027 under RCP4.5, and 2029 under RCP2.6 - a 3-year scenario spread. The definitive indicators are C3 (group grievance) and P1 (state legitimacy): C3 crosses its critical value of 7.0 as early as 2020 (the first compound drought on top of the 2018-2019 crisis baseline), and P1 crosses 7.0 in 2022-2023. The mechanism is the mediated pathway: drought -> yield loss -> food-price inflation -> grievance (C3) and legitimacy erosion (P1), compounded by the 25% residual feedback. By 2050, all three scenarios converge to ~98.5 (deeply fragile) - the feedback layer means the late-trajectory is dominated by the accumulated residual, not the annual climate shock. Limitations: (a) the 72.2 baseline is a reconstruction of the post-crisis state and the 'vulnerable, 17.8 below fragile' positioning is a judgment call - if the true pre-2020 baseline were lower (e.g. 65), the tipping year would be later; (b) the tipping definition is composite-based (per round 2) - a single-indicator tipping (P1 or C3 alone) would occur earlier (2020-2023) but is reported as the mechanism, not the definition; (c) the model does not capture Sri Lanka's 2022 political crisis (presidential resignation), which would have added a non-climate legitimacy shock; (d) the 2050 convergence across scenarios is an artifact of the feedback saturation - in reality, adaptation and policy would differentiate the trajectories.

### Problem

Task 4: Use the model to show which state-driven interventions could mitigate the risk of climate change and prevent the country (Yemen) from becoming a fragile state. Explain the effect of human intervention and predict the total cost of intervention. Per round-2 directive: report state-only cost (10-15% of GDP ceiling) and the external-financing gap separately, because Yemen's fiscal collapse makes the full-package-as-state-cost indefensible.

### Analysis

Three state-driven levers, mapped onto the model's climate submodel and indicator structure: (a) IRRIATION EXPANSION - irrigation_share 0.2 -> 0.4, which reduces the drought yield loss by the benchmark 0.17 per +10% irrigation share (Data item 2); (b) RESILIENT AGRICULTURE PACKAGE - adaptation 0 -> 0.5 (drought-resistant seeds, extension services, index insurance), offsetting up to 60% of the climate loss at full adoption (15-64% yield-gain capability benchmark); (c) WATER INFRASTRUCTURE + SOCIAL PROTECTION - a fixed -0.5 offset on P2 (public services) and C3 (group grievance), representing direct state action on water delivery and food-subsidy programs (not a climate-response lever, a governance lever). Costs are order-of-magnitude in 2018$ (Yemen GDP ~19B, pop ~29M).

### Modeling Process

Costs (10-year package): irrigation expansion = 0.020 * GDP * 10 = $3.8B; resilient agriculture = 0.005 * GDP * 10 = $0.95B; water infra + social protection = $2.0B. Full package total = $6.75B (35.5% of 2018 GDP, $233 per capita). State-only capacity (12% of GDP ceiling) = $2.28B. External-financing gap = $6.75B - $2.28B = $4.47B (23.5% of GDP). Effect on the 2050 RCP8.5 score (from the 2018 eroded baseline 88.2): no mitigation = 102.5 (fragile, climate attribution 14.3); full mitigation (irrigation 0.4, adaptation 0.5) = 100.1 (fragile, climate attribution 11.8); full mitigation + social protection = 99.0 (fragile). The score benefit of the full package vs no mitigation is 3.5 points (102.5 -> 99.0), of which 2.5 is from the climate submodel levers (14.3 -> 11.8 attribution) and 1.0 is from the social-protection offset on P2/C3.

### Outcome Analysis

Result: the full intervention package ($6.75B over 10 years) reduces Yemen's 2050 RCP8.5 fragility from 102.5 to 99.0 - a 3.5-point improvement that cuts the climate attribution from 14.3 to 11.8 (a 17% reduction in the climate contribution) and buys 1.0 additional point of resilience via social protection. However, even with full mitigation Yemen remains in the fragile band (99.0 >= 90) - the interventions mitigate but do not prevent fragility, because the 2015-2018 baseline erosion (81.1 -> 88.2) is already baked in. The state can only fund $2.28B (12% of GDP) of the $6.75B package; the $4.47B gap (23.5% of GDP) must come from external financing (donors, IMF, humanitarian partners) - this is the honest Task-4 output, reflecting Yemen's fiscal collapse. Limitations: (a) the cost figures are order-of-magnitude GDP-share estimates, not engineering cost benchmarks - real irrigation costs in Yemen (wells, desalination) would be higher; (b) the mitigation benefit is compressed by the indicator caps at 10.0 (the full-mitigation score 100.1 is near the cap), so the true benefit of stronger mitigation is understated; (c) the model shows interventions reduce the climate contribution but cannot reverse the conflict-driven baseline erosion - preventing fragility would require addressing the conflict itself (C1, C2, X1), which is outside the climate-intervention scope; (d) the 12% state-capacity ceiling is a judgment parameter - Yemen's actual post-2015 fiscal capacity was lower (revenue collapse), so the external-financing gap is likely an underestimate.

### Problem

Task 5: Will the model work on smaller 'states' (such as cities) or larger 'states' (such as continents)? If not, how would you modify it? Per round-3 directive: nested/hierarchical (option b), justified by the scale-invariance of the climate submodel (option c).

### Analysis

The 12-indicator FSI structure is sovereign-state-native: two indicators (C1 Security Apparatus, X1 External Intervention) are ill-defined at non-national scale (a city does not have its own military; 'external intervention' is a concept applied to sovereign states). The climate submodel, however, is scale-invariant - drought frequency/severity, crop yield loss, and food-price inflation are physical/biophysical processes that do not depend on the political boundary. This scale-invariance is what makes a nested/hierarchical extension tractable: the climate pathway transfers directly across scales, and the governance pathway (C1, X1) is handled by nesting on the parent-scale score.

### Modeling Process

CITY SCALE (nested on the national state): F_city = 0.6 * F_city_own + 0.4 * F_national_state, where F_city_own is the composite over the 10 city-defined indicators (C2, C3, E1-E4, P1-P4 - re-normalized to 0-120) and F_national_state is the national composite (carrying C1/X1). The 0.6/0.4 split reflects that a city's security-apparatus and external-intervention exposure is dominated by its state. Example: Sanaa (Yemen) with city_own composite 78.6 and national state 2018 = 97.0 -> nested fragility = 0.6*78.6 + 0.4*97.0 = 86.0 (vulnerable). The city cannot be 'stable' if its state is 'fragile' - the 0.4 national weight enforces this. CONTINENT SCALE (nested on member states + shared climate forcing): F_continent = 0.5 * mean(F_member_states) + 0.5 * (mean(F_member_states) + climate_increment), where climate_increment is the shared climate forcing (the scale-invariant climate submodel, aggregated as a composite-equivalent increment). Example: Africa (illustrative 8-member subset: Nigeria 66, Ethiopia 71, DR Congo 98, Sudan 103, Chad 95, Somalia 112, Kenya 61, Egypt 60; mean 83.2) with climate_increment 12.0 (2050 RCP8.5) -> nested fragility = 0.5*83.2 + 0.5*95.2 = 89.2 (vulnerable). At continent scale, C1 is re-interpreted as the continental security architecture (e.g. AU peacekeeping) and X1 as external intervention by non-continental actors; E3 (intra-continental inequality) dominates the climate pathway.

### Outcome Analysis

Result: the model does NOT work standalone at city scale - C1 and X1 are ill-defined, and the nesting on the national-state score (0.4 weight) is required to recover the security and external-intervention dimensions. At continent scale, the model works with re-interpreted indicators (C1, X1) and the climate forcing as a shared covariate; the 0.5/0.5 split is a calibration choice. The key insight is the SEPARATION of the scale-invariant climate pathway (transfers directly) from the scale-dependent governance pathway (requires nesting). The Sanaa example (86.0, vulnerable) is consistent with the national Yemen score (93.5, fragile) - the city is less fragile than the state because it lacks the C1/X1 exposure, but the 0.4 national weight keeps it in the vulnerable band rather than stable. The Africa example (89.2, vulnerable) shows that a continent's fragility is driven by its most fragile members (Somalia 112, Sudan 103, DR Congo 98) plus the shared climate forcing, not by its stable members (Egypt 60, Kenya 61). Limitations: (a) the 0.6/0.4 and 0.5/0.5 nesting coefficients are calibration choices, not estimated parameters - they would need to be fit on city-level and continent-level fragility data to be defensible; (b) the city example uses a single city (Sanaa) - a different Yemeni city (e.g. Aden, a port city with different E3/E4 profile) would give a different city_own composite; (c) the continent example uses an 8-member subset, not all 54 African states - the full continent score would be different; (d) the model does not capture sub-national climate heterogeneity (e.g. within-country drought gradients) - the climate submodel is applied at the aggregate scale, so a city in a drought-prone region of a stable state would be understated.
