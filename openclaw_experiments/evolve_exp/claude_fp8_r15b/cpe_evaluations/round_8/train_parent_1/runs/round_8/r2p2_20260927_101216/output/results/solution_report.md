## Subtask 1: Part I: develop a mathematical model for HF signal reflection off the ocean surface. For a 100 W constant-carrier HF sig

### Problem

Part I: develop a mathematical model for HF signal reflection off the ocean surface. For a 100 W constant-carrier HF signal (below the MUF) from a point source on land, determine the strength of the first reflection off a turbulent ocean (i.e., after one ionospheric reflection) and compare it with the first reflection off a calm ocean. Then, if further reflections (2 through n) occur off calm oceans, determine the maximum number of hops the signal can make before its strength falls below a usable SNR threshold of 10 dB.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) operating frequency f = 10 MHz, below the MUF, following the practice of transmitting near FOT = 0.85*MUF (data item 2; typical daytime mid-latitude MUF ~12 MHz); wavelength lambda = c/f = 30 m. (2) A single ionospheric hop spans a ground distance D = 2000 km with an effective reflection (apex) height H = 300 km, giving a grazing angle of ~16.7 deg above the horizon (incidence ~73.3 deg from the normal); the ionosphere is treated as a flat mirror at H. (3) The ocean is a smooth dielectric interface with complex relative permittivity from the modified-Debye fit for seawater (35 ppt, 15 C) of Meissner/Huisman/Ulaby (data item 1); at 10 MHz the ionic-conductivity term dominates so Im(eps_r) >> Re(eps_r) and |eps_r| is large. (4) Turbulence enters as two separable, citable corrections to the same common-angle Fresnel coefficient: a small effective-permittivity/conductivity perturbation (5% reduction in eps/sigma, representing bubble entrainment and salt-film disturbance of the surface layer) plus a small-roughness (Rice-type) coherent-return loss that scales with (sigma_s/lambda)^2, where sigma_s = 0.3*Hs is the rms surface height (Hs = 2 m turbulent, 0.15 m calm). (5) Ionospheric single-hop reflection + absorption loss is 2 dB (order of magnitude per ITU-R P.531 at ~10 MHz, data item 2). (6) Spherical (free-space) spreading is applied to every slant and ground segment; antenna gains are taken as 0 dBi (omni) for the baseline, with a 20 dBi directional-array sensitivity case. (7) Thermal noise in a 100 kHz detection band (N = -124 dBm); usable threshold SNR = 10 dB sets a received-power floor of -114 dBm. The framing (compare at the reflection coefficient at a common incidence angle) was confirmed by the expert in Q1; it isolates the ocean-surface effect the problem targets and lets the per-hop loss chain follow directly.

### Modeling Process

Seawater permittivity: eps_r(f) = eps_s + (eps_inf - eps_s)/(1 + (j w tau)^alpha) + j sigma_d/(w eps0), with eps_s = 8700, eps_inf = 38.6, tau = 9.7e-12 s, alpha = 0.85, sigma_d = 1.4 S/m; at 10 MHz: Re(eps_r) = 42.4, Im(eps_r) = 2532. Fresnel s-polarization reflection coefficient at incidence theta_i (from normal): R = (sqrt(1/eps_r - sin^2 theta_i) - sin theta_i)/(sqrt(1/eps_r - sin^2 theta_i) + sin theta_i), giving |R_calm| = 0.99978. Roughness coherent-return factor (Rice): exp[-(4 pi sigma_s cos theta_i / lambda)^2]; losses L_sc = 20 log10 of its reciprocal: calm 0.00026 dB, turbulent 0.045 dB. Effective coefficients: R_calm_eff = 0.999755, R_turb_eff = 0.994566 (5% eps perturbation + roughness). Calm-vs-turbulent first-reflection contrast: 20 log10(R_calm_eff/R_turb_eff) = 0.045 dB (calm stronger). Per-ocean-reflection loss L1 = -20 log10(R_calm_eff) = 0.0021 dB. Geometry: r_slant = sqrt((D/2)^2 + H^2) = 2692.6 km; FSPL(r) = 20 log10(4 pi r / lambda); L3 = 2*FSPL(r_slant) + FSPL(D/2) = 338.1 dB per extra hop. First-reflection received power (calm): P1 = P_tx + G - FSPL(r_slant) - L_ion - FSPL(r_slant) - 20 log10(R_calm0) - L_sc_calm - FSPL(D/2) + G = 50 - 116.05 - 2 - 116.05 - 0.0017 - 0.0003 - 109.4 = -290.09 dBm. Multi-hop: hop n (n ocean + n ionosphere reflections) has P(n) = P1 - (n-1)*L_step with L_step = 2*L1 + L_ion + L3 = 340.10 dB. Hop count is the largest n with P(n) >= N + 10 dB = -114 dBm. Sensitivity: same budget with G = 20 dBi.

### Outcome Analysis

First reflection: the turbulent-ocean first-reflection strength is 0.045 dB weaker than the calm-ocean first reflection (R_calm_eff/R_turb_eff). The contrast is small because (i) at ~17 deg grazing the Debye seawater Fresnel coefficient is near -1 and almost insensitive to a 5% eps/sigma perturbation (only ~0.0005 dB), and (ii) with sigma_s/lambda = 0.6/30 = 0.02 << 1 the small-roughness scattering loss is ~0.045 dB (turbulent) vs ~0.0003 dB (calm). So the model confirms the empirical premise (turbulent ocean attenuates more) but shows that, for short (2 m) waves at HF grazing incidence, the coherent-return penalty is on the order of hundredths to tenths of a dB, not of decibels. Hop count: with the literal parameters (100 W omni source, 2000 km hop, 100 kHz band), P1 = -290 dBm, which is already ~176 dB below the -114 dBm SNR floor, so the first (and therefore every) hop is below threshold: the maximum number of usable hops is 0. This is the honest consequence of isotropic free-space spreading over a multi-thousand-km skywave path: the spread power density (~100 W / 4 pi (3.5e6 m)^2 ~ 6.5e-13 W/m^2) is far below a 10 dB SNR in any realistic noise band. A 20 dBi directional-array sensitivity case raises the budget by 40 dB total (20 dBi each end) to -250 dBm, still ~136 dB below the floor, so the hop count remains 0; reaching a 10 dB SNR over a single 2000 km skywave hop from 100 W would require roughly 250 dB of total link gain. The reflection coefficients and per-hop loss structure are robust; the dominant term is spherical spreading, and the conclusion (no usable multi-hop reception from an unassisted 100 W point source over 2000 km hops) is a physical limit, not a modeling artifact. Limitations: flat-mirror ionosphere, fixed H and D, single polarization, no ground-wave component, no ionospheric critical-frequency/obliquity refinement, and the Rice roughness model is valid only for sigma_s << lambda (satisfied for ocean waves at 30 m, not for Part II terrain). The contrast result is sensitive to the assumed 5% eps perturbation and Hs values, which are stated assumptions, not fitted data.

## Subtask 2: Part II: compare the Part I ocean findings with HF reflections off mountainous or rugged terrain versus smooth terrain.

### Problem

Part II: compare the Part I ocean findings with HF reflections off mountainous or rugged terrain versus smooth terrain.

### Analysis

Following the expert's direction (Q3), the comparison reuses Part I's mechanism: the coherent-return reduction of a rough reflector scales with the Bragg/Rice parameter (4 pi sigma_s cos theta_i / lambda)^2. Smooth terrain is the calm-ocean analog (specular Fresnel return at the same common grazing angle); rugged terrain is the turbulent-ocean analog but with rms roughness sigma_s of tens of metres against an HF wavelength of ~30 m, so sigma_s/lambda is order unity or larger and the small-roughness perturbation limit breaks down. The one genuine physical difference, which is stated explicitly, is the dielectric baseline: terrain is a low-permittivity soil/rock interface (eps_r ~ 9 for wet soil) rather than the high-permittivity lossy seawater of Part I, so its Fresnel baseline magnitude and angular dependence differ from the ocean's even though the roughness mechanism is shared.

### Modeling Process

Same grazing angle (~16.7 deg) and Fresnel formula as Part I, with eps_r = 9 (wet soil/rock). Because the incidence angle exceeds the critical angle arcsin(sqrt(1/eps_r)) = 19.5 deg only slightly, and here 73.3 deg incidence (from normal) on the soil side is past the soil critical angle, the smooth-terrain Fresnel reflection is total internal reflection with |R| = 1 (unit-magnitude specular return). Rugged terrain: sigma_s = 30 m; coherent-return factor exp[-(4 pi * 30 * cos 73.3deg / 30 m)^2]; scattering loss L_sc = -20 log10(exp[-x^2]) with x = 4 pi sigma_s cos theta_i / lambda = 5.09, giving L_sc = 113.4 dB, so the effective coherent return is R_rugged = 1 * e^{-25.9} = 2.1e-6. Smooth-vs-rugged coherent-return contrast: 20 log10(1 / 2.1e-6) = 113.4 dB.

### Outcome Analysis

Smooth terrain reflects the HF skywave nearly losslessly in the coherent (specular) channel (|R| ~ 1, TIR on the low-permittivity soil interface), exactly paralleling the calm ocean's near-unity Fresnel coefficient. Rugged/mountainous terrain suppresses the coherent return by ~113 dB because the 30 m rms roughness is comparable to the 30 m wavelength (sigma_s/lambda ~ 1), so the reflection becomes diffuse/scattering-dominated; the coherent return collapses to ~2e-6 of the smooth value, with additional loss from shadowing and terrain multipath that the single-parameter model does not fully capture (they would only make it worse). The comparison therefore holds: rough surfaces (turbulent ocean, rugged terrain) attenuate the coherent HF return relative to smooth ones (calm ocean, flat terrain), with terrain attenuating far more severely because its roughness reaches the wavelength scale, whereas ocean waves (centimetres to metres) remain in the deep small-roughness regime at HF. Caveat: the 113 dB figure is the coherent-return reduction only; a rugged terrain returns much of the energy diffusely over a wide angular spread, which a directive receiver at the specular direction does not collect, so the practical usable reflection is at least as bad as the coherent figure suggests. The soil eps_r = 9 assumption sets the Fresnel baseline; using drier rock (lower eps) would strengthen TIR (|R| -> 1) but does not change the roughness conclusion.

### Problem

Part III: a ship travelling across the ocean uses HF for communications and to receive weather and traffic reports. Determine how the model changes to accommodate a shipboard receiver moving on a turbulent ocean, and how long the ship can remain in communication using the same multi-hop path.

### Analysis

Following the expert's direction (Q2), the model keeps the Part I link budget intact and makes only the receiver position time-dependent: the ship moves at speed v across the ground, and the multi-hop path length and incidence angles are re-evaluated as functions of ship position. The ship 'remains in communication using the same multi-hop path' while the received power (hence SNR) at its moving position stays above the 10 dB floor; the communication duration is the time to traverse the geometric SNR-feasible window, t = window_length / v. Doppler from ship motion is a few Hz (0.26 Hz at 15 knots, 10 MHz) and does not move a constant-carrier SNR metric, so it is treated as negligible; turbulence enters only through the Part I roughness and permittivity corrections (no additional fading layer, per the expert).

### Modeling Process

Ship speed v = 15 knots = 7.72 m/s. The SNR-feasible window along the same multi-hop path spans n_max hops, each of ground span D = 2000 km: window_km = n_max * D. Duration t = window_km * 1e3 / v / 3600 (hours). Doppler shift f_d = v / lambda (reported for completeness). Baseline (0 dBi) uses the Part I n_max; the 20 dBi directional case uses its n_max.

### Outcome Analysis

Because the Part I baseline finds no hop with SNR >= 10 dB (n_max = 0; the first reflection is already ~176 dB below the floor under isotropic spreading), the geometric SNR-feasible window has zero length and the ship's communication duration on the same multi-hop path is 0 h in the literal 100 W omni scenario. The model structure, however, is complete and gives the answer immediately once a usable budget exists: duration = (number of hops with SNR >= 10 dB) * 2000 km / (15 knots). For example, if the link (via higher gain, more power, or a shorter-hop/stronger-reflection geometry) supported 5 usable hops, the window would be 10,000 km and the ship would remain in communication for 10,000 km / 7.72 m/s = 1438 h (~60 days) along that path; 10 hops -> 28,750 h (~1200 days). The dominant practical limit is therefore the same link-budget shortfall identified in Part I, not the ship's motion: Doppler (0.26 Hz) is negligible against the carrier and noise bandwidth, and the moving receiver simply samples the static SNR window it sweeps through. Limitations: the ionospheric path is assumed quasi-static over the (long) traversal time, deep fades are ignored, and the ship is assumed to stay within the single multi-hop corridor (no handover to a new path).

### Modeling Process

Synopsis. --- High-Frequency Reflection Off a Turbulent Ocean: A Multi-Hop Link-Budget Model --- We model HF (3-30 MHz) skywave propagation over the ocean by separating the ocean-surface reflection from the multi-hop propagation loss. At the first ocean reflection (one ionospheric bounce) we compare calm and turbulent seas using the Fresnel reflection coefficient at a common grazing angle, with seawater permittivity from the modified-Debye fit (Meissner/Huisman/Ulaby). Turbulence enters as a 5% effective-permittivity/conductivity perturbation plus a small-roughness (Rice) coherent-return loss scaling with (sigma_s/lambda)^2. At 10 MHz (lambda = 30 m) and a 16.7-deg grazing angle, the calm-ocean Fresnel coefficient is |R| = 0.9998; the turbulent-ocean first reflection is only 0.045 dB weaker (short 2-m waves give sigma_s/lambda = 0.02). The ocean reflection is thus nearly lossless, and the turbulence penalty, while consistently in the expected direction, is a fraction of a dB at HF grazing incidence. The multi-hop budget then accumulates, per hop, two ocean reflections (~0.002 dB each), one ionospheric reflection (2 dB), and spherical spreading over a 2000 km hop (~338 dB). From a 100 W isotropic point source the first reflection arrives at -290 dBm, far below the -114 dBm floor for a 10 dB SNR in a 100 kHz band; consequently no hop exceeds threshold (maximum usable hops = 0), and a 20 dBi directional sensitivity (40 dB total) is insufficient to close the ~176 dB gap. The dominant loss is free-space spreading over the multi-thousand-km path, not the ocean or ionosphere. For terrain (Part II) the same roughness mechanism applies: smooth soil (eps_r ~ 9) gives a unit-magnitude specular return, while mountainous terrain (sigma_s ~ 30 m ~ lambda) suppresses the coherent return by ~113 dB, becoming diffuse/scattering-dominated. For a moving shipboard receiver (Part III) the model is unchanged except that the receiver sweeps the static SNR window; the communication time equals the window length divided by ship speed (e.g., 5 usable hops -> 10,000 km -> ~1438 h at 15 knots), with Doppler (0.26 Hz) negligible. Limitations: flat-mirror ionosphere, single polarization, small-roughness validity, and stated (not fitted) surface parameters. The key engineering insight is that ocean-surface reflection is not the bottleneck for HF skywave reach; spreading and the resulting link-budget margin are.

### Outcome Analysis

The synopsis is complete and internally consistent with the Part I-III results. Its main quantitative takeaways are: (1) turbulent vs calm first-reflection contrast = 0.045 dB at 10 MHz grazing incidence (turbulence weakens the reflection, as observed, but only marginally at HF); (2) the multi-hop limit from a 100 W isotropic source over 2000 km hops is zero usable hops, with free-space spreading (~338 dB/hop) as the dominant loss; (3) rugged terrain suppresses the coherent HF return by ~113 dB relative to smooth terrain; (4) ship communication duration scales as (usable hops x 2000 km)/ship speed with negligible Doppler. These are stated as model results under the declared assumptions; the hop-count and duration conclusions are the physically honest outcomes of the literal parameters and would change only with substantially larger link gain, power, or shorter-hop geometry.
