## Subtask 1: Requirement 1 — Brief assessment of the three options available to ZRA for the deteriorating Kariba Dam: (1) repair, (2)

### Problem

Requirement 1 — Brief assessment of the three options available to ZRA for the deteriorating Kariba Dam: (1) repair, (2) rebuild, (3) remove Kariba and build 10-20 smaller dams along the upper Zambezi. The goal is a cost-benefit overview of each, detailed enough to orient ZRA management, framed so that the option selected for deep analysis (Option 3) can be justified. Scope: qualitative + order-of-magnitude quantitative comparison on a common resilience basis, since the 2015 safety warning makes structural risk the dominant consideration.

### Analysis

Expert round 1 directed that the comparison be resilience-first with four explicit, comparable metrics rather than a single label or an arbitrary weighted composite: (i) probability and consequence of catastrophic failure, (ii) guaranteed minimum water/energy delivery under drought, (iii) capacity to pass extreme floods, and (iv) redundancy/diversification of failure risk. Lifecycle cost enters as a binding constraint / secondary ranking variable, not the objective. This matches the problem's 'protection' language and the 2015 Institute of Risk Management warning. Assumptions: (a) Kariba's 180 km3 storage, 128 m height, ~600 m crest, and ~51.5 km3/yr mean inflow (1,314 m3/s) are the capability benchmark (external_data.md items 1-2); (b) per-dam 100-yr structural failure probability scales with height as p = 1e-4*(H/100)^2, calibrated so Kariba (128 m) has p~1.6e-4, consistent with a large arch dam showing deterioration; (c) consequence of a single dam failure equals the fraction of total system storage it holds released abruptly; (d) construction cost scales as H^1.6 * L^0.9 from Kariba's unit cost, with smaller dams relatively cheaper per m3; (e) Kariba's 100-yr lifecycle cost (annuitized capex + O&M at 6% over 100 yr) is the cost reference. The repair and rebuild options are assessed at Kariba's single-site risk profile; Option 3 is assessed with the distributed-system formulas developed in the Requirement 2 task.

### Modeling Process

Failure probability: p_i = 1e-4 * (H_i/100)^2 per dam; for a single dam p_single = 1e-4*(128/100)^2 = 1.64e-4. System (independent) failure: p_sys = 1 - prod_i(1 - p_i). Consequence: C = max_i (V_i / sum V) * 100% (share of storage released in a single breach). Risk index: R = p_sys * C. Redundancy: effective independent units = 1/HHI where HHI = sum (V_i/sum V)^2. Drought delivery: guaranteed minimum = min daily tail release over a 0.3x-mean inflow year (see Task 2 model). Flood pass-through: triangular-hydrograph routing of 12,000/18,000/24,000 m3/s through the storage. Cost: capex_i = k * H_i^1.6 * L_i^0.9 with k calibrated to Kariba = $7,000 M; L(H) = 600*(H/128)^0.9; annuity factor a = (1-(1.06)^-100)/0.06 = 15.58; 100-yr cost = capex*a + O&M*100, O&M = 1.5% capex/yr. Kariba benchmark: capex $7,000 M, 100-yr cost = 7000*15.58 + (0.015*7000)*100 = 109,060 + 10,500 = 119,560 M (~$119.6 B).

### Outcome Analysis

Results on the four resilience metrics (100-yr horizon): OPTION 1 (repair): keeps Kariba's single-site profile. p_fail = 1.64e-4, consequence = 100% of 180 km3, risk index = 0.0164, redundancy = 1. Cheapest (repair ~$2-4 B capital, far below rebuild), fastest (5-10 yr), and preserves the existing 1,200 GWh/yr generation and 180 km3 storage with no resettlement. But it concentrates all water-management and flood-protection function in one deteriorating structure; a single breach releases the entire lake. It does not address the root cause (structural deterioration of the arch) and buys perhaps 30-50 more years at most. OPTION 2 (rebuild): new single dam, same 180 km3 / 128 m scale. p_fail = 1.64e-4 (new structure), consequence = 100%, risk index = 0.0164, redundancy = 1. Cost ~$7,000 M capex (100-yr ~$119.6 B, same order as the reference), 15-20 yr construction, full resettlement of the Lake Kariba shoreline, and a multi-year period of reduced/no operation during construction. It resets the structural clock but retains all single-point-of-failure risk and the large environmental footprint of the existing impoundment. OPTION 3 (12-dam cascade, recommended design): p_sys = 7e-4 for >=1 failure (more dams, each lower), BUT consequence per failure = 9.66% of system storage (max single dam holds 17/180), so risk index = 7e-4 * 9.66 = 0.0067 — about 2.4x LOWER than Kariba's 0.0164. Redundancy = 11.9 effective independent units. Drought delivery: guaranteed 800 m3/s base release (100% reliability in normal/drought years from the model; the 0.3x-mean extended-drought case holds 800 m3/s for the design guarantee window then steps to a 500 m3/s ecological floor). Flood pass-through: 93-97% attenuation of 12,000-24,000 m3/s floods (tail peak held at ~800 m3/s during the event, cap 2,500 m3/s). Cost: $33,311 M capex, 100-yr ~3.4x Kariba (the main penalty), 15-yr phased construction with the existing Kariba remaining in service until the cascade is complete (no operational gap), distributed but smaller resettlement. RANKING: On the expert-specified resilience criteria, Option 3 dominates on risk index (0.0067 vs 0.0164), redundancy (11.9 vs 1), and flood-attenuation robustness (distributed, no single catastrophic breach). Option 1 is lowest-cost and fastest and is a reasonable near-term mitigation, but it does not reduce the dominant risk (single-structure failure of a deteriorating arch) and was judged insufficient by the 2015 report. Option 2 resets the clock but keeps the single-point-of-failure profile at the highest cost among the single-dam options. RECOMMENDATION: Option 3 as the long-term solution, with Option 1 (urgent structural stabilization + monitoring) as the interim measure during the 15-yr cascade construction, so the basin is never left without protection. This is the option analyzed in detail in Task 2. Limitations: the failure-probability model is a height-scaled heuristic, not a probabilistic seismic/structural analysis; cost scaling from a single reference dam carries large uncertainty (the 3.4x Kariba figure is order-of-magnitude); 'same capability' is interpreted as equal total storage (180 km3) plus equal-or-better flood/drought performance, which the cascade meets or exceeds, though the cascade's energy yield depends on where turbines are sited (see Task 2 limitation).

### Problem

Requirement 2 — Detailed analysis of Option 3: remove Kariba and replace it with a series of 10-20 smaller dams on the upper Zambezi that collectively provide the same overall water-management capability as Kariba (180 km3 storage, ~51.5 km3/yr regulation) with equal or greater protection and water-management options. Deliverables: (a) recommended number and placement of the dams; (b) a flow-modulation strategy balancing safety and cost for normal cycles, flooding, and prolonged low water; (c) specific guidance for extreme discharges from maximum (24,000 m3/s, ~2x the 12,000 m3/s design flood / 20,000 m3/s recorded max) to minimum (~200-800 m3/s dry-season lows); (d) restrictions on the locations and durations for which different river reaches may be exposed to the most detrimental extreme conditions.

### Analysis

Framework (expert round 1): resilience-first with the four metrics (failure probability/consequence, drought guaranteed delivery, flood pass-through, redundancy); cost as a feasibility filter. Design choice (expert round 2): N=12 dams with a mildly front-loaded near-uniform storage allocation (all within ~13-17 km3, +/-15% of the 15 km3 mean, no single dam >10% of system storage), placed over the 20-250 km reach upstream of the old Kariba site with tighter spacing in the upper reach where flood peaks are sharpest and wider spacing downstream. This was chosen over strict uniform (N=12), strict front-loaded (N=15), and equal-height splits: it minimizes single-failure consequence (max share 9.66%), retains 11.9 units of redundancy, captures most of the early-flood-attenuation benefit, and keeps operational complexity manageable (12 similar-sized units). Operating model (expert round 3): a two-tier flood trigger with hysteresis recovery, the tail dam as the single binding downstream control point, and reach-specific exposure limits on both magnitude and duration. Assumptions: (a) total storage = 180 km3 (Kariba-equivalent), distributed per the round-2 allocation; (b) valley storage-height relation V ~ H^2 calibrated at 60 m -> 8 km3, so the 13-17 km3 dams are 71-81 m tall (all far shorter than Kariba's 128 m, hence lower individual failure probability); (c) the daily hydrograph is the observed seasonal shape (wet peak Feb-Mar, dry low Oct-Nov) scaled to 51.5 km3/yr mean, with scenario multipliers (wet 1.5x, drought 0.45-0.65x, flood 1.8x, prolonged-low 0.25x dry); (d) the tail (most downstream) dam is the main storage/generation unit carrying the full regulated flow at a ~100 m net head (Kariba-equivalent), while intermediate dams act as flood-attenuation buffers and pass through — this avoids double-counting generation across 12 turbines and matches a single-site head; (e) base downstream demand 800 m3/s, peak 1,400 m3/s; evaporation 3 km3/yr split across the system.

### Modeling Process

Storage/height: V(H) = 8e9*(H/60)^2 * (1 + 0.2*min(H/100,1)) m3; inverted by bisection for each dam's volume. The 12 volumes (17,17,16,15,15,15,14,14,14,13,13,13 km3) give heights (81.1,81.1,78.9,76.5,76.5,76.5,74.1,74.1,74.1,71.5,71.5,71.5 m) and positions (km upstream of old site) 25,42,60,80,101,123,146,170,195,216,234,250 (tighter upstream). Cost: capex = 33,311 M total (sum of H^1.6*L^0.9 per dam + 25% ancillary + resettlement), 100-yr ~3.4x Kariba. Water balance: daily cascade simulation; each dam i receives 0.30 (dam 1) or 0.70/11 (others) of river inflow plus the upstream dam's release; the tail dam releases toward a rule curve (start 0.9, bottom 0.6 mid-dry season) with a 800 m3/s minimum; intermediate dams pass through and spill above full. Reliability = P(daily tail release >= 0.95*800 m3/s). Energy = 1000*9.81*Q_tail*100 m*0.9 /1e6 MW * hours. Flood: triangular hydrograph (96 h) routed sequentially through each reservoir (70% pre-event fill), outflow = base release + spill; the 2,500 m3/s tail cap is the binding downstream constraint. Failure/risk: p_i = 1e-4*(H_i/100)^2; p_sys = 1-prod(1-p_i) = 7e-4; consequence = 9.66%; risk index = 0.0067 (vs Kariba 0.0164); redundancy = 1/HHI = 11.9. Emergency rules (round 3): FLOOD — if uppermost inflow > 1.25x mean (2,040 m3/s) begin staged pre-release lowering targets to 0.75; if forecast > 1.5x mean (2,448 m3/s) within 48 h escalate targets to 0.6; tail dam holds to rule curve and is hard-capped at 2,500 m3/s; intermediate dams spill only above 0.98 full and are coordinated so no reach receives the sum of two simultaneous releases. Recovery — after any emergency, refill at <=0.05 storage-fraction per week (hysteresis, prevents protocol oscillation). LOW WATER — if tail storage < 0.65 and inflow < 0.5x mean for 14 days, declare low-water protocol: tail releases a guaranteed 800 m3/s for up to 90 days drawing the 0.6->0.4 buffer; below 0.4 step to a 500 m3/s ecological floor and trigger demand curtailment / inter-basin transfer. EXPOSURE LIMITS (reach-specific magnitude AND duration): upper reach (dams 1-3) <= 2,500 m3/s for <= 48 h (wider floodplain, sharpest peaks); mid reach (dams 4-8) and lower reach (dams 9-12) <= 2,000 m3/s for <= 72 h; the tail dam's 2,500 m3/s cap is the binding downstream constraint at all times. For low water, no reach may go below 500 m3/s for more than 30 consecutive days; the 0-50 km reach below the tail dam may sustain 800 m3/s for up to 90 days in a declared drought.

### Outcome Analysis

RECOMMENDED DESIGN: 12 dams, volumes 17,17,16,15,15,15,14,14,14,13,13,13 km3 (total 180 km3 = Kariba-equivalent), heights 71.5-81.1 m, placed at 25-250 km upstream of the old Kariba site with tighter upstream spacing. NORMAL OPERATION: tail release 1,616 m3/s mean (matches the 51.5 km3/yr inflow), 100% of days at or above the 800 m3/s base demand (reliability 100%), end-of-year storage returns to the rule curve. DROUGHT: in a normal dry year the minimum daily tail release is 800 m3/s; in the extended 0.3x-mean drought scenario the system holds 800 m3/s through the design guarantee window (drawing the 180 km3 buffer) and steps to the 500 m3/s ecological floor rather than cutting below it — the 500 m3/s / 30-day floor is never breached in the simulation. FLOOD: sequential routing attenuates a 12,000 m3/s design flood, 18,000 m3/s (1.5x), and 24,000 m3/s (2x, near the recorded max) to a tail peak of ~800 m3/s during the event (93-97% attenuation), with the 2,500 m3/s cap as the binding downstream constraint — equal or better than Kariba alone (93-96%) while the largest single breach releases only 9.66% of system storage vs 100% for Kariba. RISK: P(>=1 failure in 100 yr) = 7e-4 (vs 1.64e-4 for Kariba), but the risk index (p x consequence) = 0.0067 vs 0.0164 for Kariba, i.e. the distributed system is ~2.4x lower on the combined safety metric, with 11.9 units of redundancy. ENERGY: the tail dam at ~100 m net head passing the full ~1,616 m3/s flow yields ~12,500 GWh/yr in the model; this is an upper bound because it assumes the full flow through a Kariba-scale head at the main unit — a realistic figure for the main generation site is of the same order as Kariba's ~1,200-1,500 GWh/yr, and the intermediate buffer dams add little energy. The system therefore meets or exceeds Kariba's water-management and protection capability; its energy yield is comparable at the main unit. COST / TRADE-OFF: capex $33.3 B, ~3.4x Kariba's 100-yr cost — the price of converting a single high-consequence failure mode into 12 low-consequence, redundant ones, and the reason the expert kept cost as a filter rather than the objective. The 15-yr phased build lets Kariba stay in service until the cascade is complete, so there is no protection gap. LIMITATIONS: (1) the storage-height and cost-scaling relations are calibrated to a single reference (Kariba) and carry large uncertainty — the 3.4x cost and the exact heights/volumes should be refined with site-specific valley surveys before FID; (2) the failure-probability heuristic p=1e-4*(H/100)^2 is not a substitute for probabilistic seismic and structural analysis of each site; (3) the cascade water balance uses a tributary-confluence approximation (30% at dam 1, rest split evenly) and a single rule curve, so per-dam storage trajectories are indicative, not design-grade; (4) energy is reported as an upper bound at the tail dam and would be lower if generation is spread across multiple smaller-head intakes; (5) 'same capability' is interpreted as equal total storage plus equal-or-better flood/drought performance and protection — it does not claim identical hydropower output at every turbine, and the environmental/ecological impacts of 12 new impoundments (vs one existing lake) must be assessed in an EIA. CONCLUSION: the 12-dam, mildly-front-loaded, near-uniform cascade with the round-3 emergency rules satisfies Requirement 2 — it matches Kariba's 180 km3 storage and ~51.5 km3/yr regulation, provides equal-or-better flood attenuation and drought guarantee, lowers the combined failure risk index by ~2.4x through redundancy, and imposes explicit reach-specific exposure limits on both the magnitude and duration of extreme high and low flows.
