# Interaction evidence — 3 expert exchanges

## Exchange 1 (question: which practical difference matters most when deciding which countries need outside funding — outbreak size, growth rate, or ability to pay?)

**Expert reply (gist):** Ability to pay is the binding constraint. A large or fast-growing outbreak in a country that can finance its own response does not need donor money; a small outbreak in a very poor country may still warrant support but has low absolute burden. The case that matters most for donor allocation is the intersection: high or rapidly growing burden AND low-income status (World Bank low-income, GNP per capita under ~$760), where domestic health budgets cannot plausibly cover ARV costs of several hundred to ~$1,100 per patient-year. Ability to pay is the primary screen; outbreak size and growth rate then determine how much and where within that set.

**How the reply became work (before the next exchange):**
- Constraint added to the model: country selection screens first by World Bank income code (low / lower-middle income = donor-eligible), then ranks within that set by 1999 HIV burden and projected growth. The six continent-representative countries were chosen as the largest-burden, most-critical country per continent; the low-income sub-Saharan African set (Ethiopia, Nigeria, Kenya, Zimbabwe, Tanzania, Mozambique, DR Congo, Zambia, Uganda, Malawi, Côte d'Ivoire) is the donor-eligible core.
- Allocation rule implemented in `model.py`: the global donor pool is split among the low-income set in proportion to 1999 HIV burden; high-income countries (USA, Australia, Brazil, South Africa at upper-middle income) are modeled as self-funding a share of their own ARV need rather than drawing on the donor pool.
- Recorded as model constraint with source "expert exchange 1" in the PARAMS table (`aid_share_rule`).

## Exchange 2 (question: how large a pool of foreign aid can realistically be expected for HIV/AIDS per year by the early 2020s, and what holds its growth back after that?)

**Expert reply (gist):** Global HIV/AIDS donor funding reached roughly $8–10 billion per year by the early 2020s (PEPFAR + Global Fund + other bilateral and multilateral), up from under $1 billion in the early 2000s. That is the order of magnitude to assume, and it plateaued rather than kept climbing. What holds growth back: donor budget politics in the main funders (flat or declining aid envelopes, competing priorities), the 2008 financial crisis and subsequent austerity, limited absorptive capacity of the low-income countries with the largest burdens (health systems, staffing, drug-delivery infrastructure), and middle-income countries with growing epidemics being increasingly expected to self-finance. Realistic trajectory: a plateau in the high single-digit billions, with any increases tied to specific initiatives rather than open-ended growth.

**How the reply became work (before the next exchange):**
- Parameter added: `aid_global_2006 = $2.5B` [1.5, 4.0] (run rate entering 2006, below the under-$1B early-2000s level growing toward the 2020s level), `aid_global_2020_plateau = $9.0B` [8, 10] (the expert's $8–10B early-2020s figure), `aid_post_plateau_growth = 2%/yr` [0, 5] (plateau, not open-ended growth). Source: expert exchange 2.
- Equation implemented in `model.py`: global aid compounds at the growth rate that takes $2.5B (2006) to $9.0B (2020), then grows at only 2%/yr through 2050 — a hard plateau, not continued exponential growth. This aid series is the funding cap on ARV coverage per country and the gate on the vaccine scenario.
- The self-financing of middle/high-income countries (from the reply) is implemented as the high-income countries not drawing on the donor pool.

## Exchange 3 (question: in the low-income countries with the largest HIV burdens, what is the main practical limit on how much treatment money a country can put to good use each year — health workforce, clinic infrastructure, or patients' ability to keep up with the regimen?)

**Expert reply (gist):** The health workforce is the main practical limit — specifically the shortage of trained clinical staff (doctors, nurses, and especially the counselors and adherence monitors that structured ARV programs require). Clinic infrastructure can be built or upgraded relatively quickly with money (a capital cost); workforce is different — training a clinician takes years, low-income countries with the heaviest burdens face severe absolute shortages and heavy out-migration of trained staff, and DOTS-style clinically-recommended ARV delivery is staff-intensive per patient, so the number of patients a country can treat well is bounded by how many qualified people it has. Patient adherence is a real constraint on outcomes and on resistance, but it is not the binding limit on how much money can be spent productively — adherence problems are managed through the same staff-intensive counseling and monitoring. The workforce bottleneck sits upstream of both infrastructure and adherence.

**How the reply became work (before the next exchange):**
- Constraint added: ARV coverage per country is capped by a workforce ceiling, not just by funding. Parameters added: `workforce_care_per_clinician = 50 patients` [30, 100] (staff-intensive DOTS-style delivery), `workforce_stock_low_income_2006 = 0.002 of population` [0.001, 0.004] (HIV-competent health staff density in low-income SSA, 2006), `workforce_stock_high_income_2006 = 0.01 of population` [0.005, 0.02], `workforce_growth_annual = 5%/yr` [3, 8] (training takes years; stock grows slowly). Source: expert exchange 3.
- Equation implemented in `model.py`: `treated_fraction(t) = min(program_ramp(t), funding_cap(t), workforce_cap(t), 0.95)` where `workforce_cap(t) = workforce_stock(t) * care_per_clinician / I(t)` and the workforce stock grows at 5%/yr. This makes the workforce, not the money, the binding constraint on how fast ARV coverage can scale in the low-income set — matching the expert's judgment that the workforce bottleneck sits upstream.
- Adherence is retained as the driver of drug resistance (Task 3) per the problem statement, consistent with the expert's point that adherence is a constraint on outcomes/resistance but not on absorptive capacity.
