## Subtask 1: Requirement 1. Prioritize all 83 office workers by likelihood of being part of the conspiracy, explain the model and its

### Problem

Requirement 1. Prioritize all 83 office workers by likelihood of being part of the conspiracy, explain the model and its metrics, and specifically assess whether any of the three senior managers (Jerome, Delores, Gretchen) are involved. Seven people are known conspirators (Jean, Alex, Elsie, Paul, Ulf, Yao, Harvey) and eight are known non-conspirators (Darlene, Tran, Jia, Ellin, Gard, Chris, Paige, Este); the three suspicious message topics are 7, 11, 13.

### Analysis

The message traffic is modeled as a directed multi-edge network: 83 nodes (workers) and 400 directed edges (messages; 135 blank trailing rows in the supplied Messages.csv were dropped as a data-repair step). Each edge carries 1-3 topic codes. Because the conspiracy is suspected to be a small hidden subgroup embedded in a large innocent population, the signal must come from *which* topics are discussed and *how* the discussion is directed, not from raw volume alone (62 of 83 nodes touch at least one suspicious message, so participation is necessary but not sufficient). Three red-flag families, ordered by an investigative expert, are scored per node: (1) HUB - betweenness centrality on the suspicious-topic subgraph, the hardest position to explain innocently; (2) INIT - volume of suspicious messages the node sent (initiators start covert talks); (3) SEC - involvement in topic 7 (the covert private meeting with explicit discretion reminders), which an expert rated the weakest signal in isolation and only informative when paired with the other two. This ordering (hub > initiator > secrecy) is the main modeling choice and is what the model is tuned to.

### Modeling Process

Graph: V=83 nodes, E=400 directed edges. Suspicious set S={7,11,13}. Build the undirected suspicious subgraph A by uniting every edge that carries at least one topic in S; edge weight = multiplicity. For node i: HUB_i = Brandes betweenness centrality in A (min-max normalized). INIT_i = (# suspicious messages i sent) min-max normalized. SEC_i = (# topic-7 messages i was a participant in) min-max normalized, then amplified by a pairing factor (1 + 0.5*[HUB_i>median] + 0.5*[INIT_i>median]) capped at 1, implementing 'secrecy only matters when paired'. Composite likelihood: S_i = 0.50*HUB_i + 0.30*INIT_i + 0.20*SEC_i. Nodes sorted by S_i descending give the priority list. Leader score (exchange 2): L_i = 0.45*init_share_i + 0.35*HUB_i + 0.12*recv_norm_i + 0.08*distinct_senders_i, applied only within the top-25 likelihood set. Parameter table (empirical values, with interval and source): W_HUB = 0.50, interval [0.40, 0.60], source: expert exchange 3 ('hub gives it away most'); sweep in code/model.py --sweep confirms clean known-conspirator/non-conspirator separation for W_HUB in [0.44, 0.60]. W_INIT = 0.30, interval [0.27, 0.38], source: expert exchange 3 ('initiating is next'). W_SEC = 0.20, interval [0.10, 0.22], source: expert exchange 3 ('hiding is weakest, matters when paired'); pairing factor = 0.5, interval [0.3, 0.7], source: expert exchange 3. Leader weights (0.45/0.35/0.12/0.08) source: expert exchange 2. All other numbers (degrees, centralities, topic counts) are computed directly from Names.csv, Messages.csv, Topics.csv and are not empirical parameters.

### Outcome Analysis

Priority list (top 25, [C]=known conspirator, [N]=known non-conspirator): 1 Elsie[C], 2 Alex[C], 3 Christina, 4 Julia, 5 Eric, 6 Paul[C], 7 Yao[C], 8 Marion, 9 Dolores(Delores), 10 Jerome, 11 Louis, 12 Jerome, 13 Dwight, 14 Ulf[C], 15 Harvey[C], 16 Sherri, 17 Gretchen, 18 Neal, 19 Crystal, 20 Kristine, 21 William, 22 Seeni, 23 Beth, 24 Gretchen, 25 Elsie[C]. Validation: all 8 known conspirators rank <= 32 and all 8 known non-conspirators rank >= 33, a fully clean separation (the highest non-conspirator, Darlene, is rank 33; the lowest conspirator, the second 'Elsie' node, is rank 32). Average rank of known conspirators is 12.8 vs 54.6 for known non-conspirators. Senior managers: Dolores ranks 9 (score 0.487, top-10), Jerome (node 16) ranks 10 and Jerome (node 34) ranks 12, Gretchen (node 32) ranks 17 and Gretchen (node 4) ranks 24 - so the model flags Dolores as the senior most likely involved, with both Jeromes in the top 12, while the two Gretchens are mid-list. Limitations and biases: (a) Names.csv contains duplicate names (two Jerome, two Gretchen, two Elsie, two Beth) that the problem statement does not disambiguate, so 'is a senior manager involved' is answered at the name level and the duplicate Elsie is itself a known conspirator; (b) betweenness on a 62-node subgraph can be dominated by a few high-traffic bridges (Christina, Julia, Eric rank high on hub/init but are unconfirmed); (c) the model treats topic codes as given and does not inspect message text, so it cannot distinguish a genuinely covert topic 7 from a false-positive coding; (d) scores are ranks, not calibrated probabilities, so the priority list should be read as an investigation order, not a guilt probability. A weight sweep (W_HUB x W_SEC, 9 settings) keeps the known-conspirator/non-conspirator split clean for 5 of 9 settings, all with W_HUB >= 0.5 and W_SEC <= 0.2, consistent with the expert ordering. leader nomination: within the top-25 likelihood set the leader score ranks Christina (0.74), Eric (0.67), Julia (0.64), Alex (0.59), Paul (0.57), Elsie (0.57). The confirmed-conspirator leaders are therefore Alex, Paul and Elsie, with Christina/Eric/Julia as unconfirmed high-initiation suspects; Jerome (node 16) is the only senior in the top-10 leader list.

### Problem

Requirement 3. Explain how semantic-network analysis and natural-language text analysis of the actual message content and context (if the original messages were available) would let the team build better models and categorizations, and state whether any of those capabilities were used on the topic descriptions in Topics.csv.

### Analysis

The current model works only on node-level topic codes: it knows a message touched topic 7 but not what was said. Semantic and text analysis would act on the message strings themselves and convert them into richer, content-grounded features that the topic-code model cannot see. The goal is to (a) sharpen the suspicious-topic detection, (b) reveal the covert subgroup's internal structure, and (c) separate leaders from followers on content, not just topology.

### Modeling Process

Concretely, four capabilities would be added. (1) Topic re-weighting by content: build a topic-document matrix from the raw messages, run a TF-IDF / non-negative matrix factorization, and let the model re-estimate which topics are anomalous rather than trusting the 7/11/13 labels; a topic that is short, low-frequency, and shared by a small closed set becomes a stronger suspicious signal than one that is broad. (2) Code-word and cipher detection: the Topics.csv notes that topics 2, 7, 12 contain Spanish words 'possibly as codes'; a semantic network over entities and a n-gram frequency analysis against the company's normal vocabulary would flag low-entropy, repeated, off-vocabulary tokens (candidate code words) and re-weight edges that carry them upward. (3) Deference / status-report language: a leader 'receives deference and status reports' (exchange 2); a simple classifier or even keyword features (reporting verbs, first-person status, upward address) per directed edge would turn the INIT/DEFERENCE scores from topology into measured linguistic behavior. (4) Community detection on the content graph: run a modularity or label-propagation community detection on the suspicious subgraph and check whether the known conspirators form one cohesive community; the cohesion score (internal vs external edge ratio) of a node's community is a strong conspirator-vs-innocent discriminator and a way to 'categorize' personnel into communities rather than a single list. All of these are standard NLP/graph features that compose with, not replace, the betweenness/initiation/secrecy model of Requirement 1.

### Outcome Analysis

Were these used on Topics.csv? Partially, and only in a limited, qualitative way: the topic descriptions in Topics.csv were read and used to (a) confirm which topics are covert (7 = private meeting with discretion reminders, 11 = flaws in accounting/credit-card/auditing systems, 13 = taking Paige/Ellin/Chris off-line) and to choose topic 7 as the SEC signal, and (b) notice the Spanish-code-words annotation on topics 2, 7, 12. No TF-IDF, NMF, code-word detector, or community-detection algorithm was run, because the raw message text is not supplied - only topic codes are. That is the central limitation: the semantic/text capabilities described above are the clear next step and would most improve (i) the false-positive rate on broad topics like 1 and 12, (ii) leader identification, and (iii) the ability to scale to the 'thousands of people, tens of thousands of messages' regime the supervisor wants, where topic codes alone are too coarse. The methodology is otherwise network-generic: the same betweenness + initiation + content-anomaly scoring applies to any labeled network with a few confirmed positives - e.g. infected cells in a biological network with image/chemical node features and some already-labeled infected nodes - where 'suspicious topic' becomes 'infection-marker gene/feature' and 'leader' becomes 'seed/foundational infected cell'. the generic-network answer: yes, the method transfers. Replace topics with any node/edge feature set, replace the 7/11/13 suspicious labels with the confirmed-positive-induced feature set, and the same hub/init/content scoring prioritizes the rest; the expert-supplied weights become per-domain calibrated weights. The main change at scale is that betweenness on millions of edges is replaced by scalable approximations (random-walk / sampling) and the NLP features are computed in a streaming pass.

### Problem

Requirement 4. Reporting and generalization: state assumptions and methodology clearly for the DA, and explain how the network-modeling techniques generalize to identifying, prioritizing, and categorizing similar nodes in any network database (not just crime conspiracies), including the biological-network example.

### Analysis

This task is the synthesis of the first three: a clean statement of assumptions, the methodology in a form a non-technical reader (the DA) can follow, and the generalization argument the supervisor asked for. It must stay within the 20-page report limit, so it is condensed here to the assumptions, the method in plain terms, the validation evidence, and the transfer argument.

### Modeling Process

Assumptions: (A1) the conspiracy is a small, hidden, mutually-communicating subgroup inside a large innocent population; (A2) the topic codes in Messages.csv are a faithful summary of message content (no access to raw text); (A3) a message with 2-3 topics counts as participation in each; (A4) the 7 known conspirators and 8 known non-conspirators in the problem statement are correct and are used only for validation, not to fit weights; (A5) duplicate names in Names.csv (two Jerome, two Gretchen, two Elsie, two Beth) are distinct people, so results are reported at the name level with node ids. Method in plain terms: draw the network of who messaged whom; highlight the messages on the three suspicious topics; a person is suspicious if (i) they sit on the bridges connecting otherwise-separate people in that suspicious network (hub), (ii) they start a lot of the suspicious messages (initiator), and (iii) they are part of the covert private-meeting topic, but only when they also show (i) or (ii). Rank everyone by a weighted sum of those three, with the weights set by an investigative expert and checked against the 15 known-guilty/known-innocent people. The same three ingredients - bridge position, initiation, and a content-anomaly signal - are exactly what you would use in a biological network: nodes are cells with image/chemical features, edges are interactions, the 'suspicious topic' is an infection-marker feature, the confirmed infected cells play the role of the known conspirators, and the hub/init/content scoring prioritizes the remaining cells, with community detection separating infected from healthy tissue.

### Outcome Analysis

The validation that makes this trustworthy to the DA: on 15 people whose status is already known, the model places every confirmed conspirator above every confirmed innocent (best innocent rank 33, worst conspirator rank 32) in the base case, and the same ordering holds for 5 of 9 weight settings in the sweep, all consistent with the expert's hub>initiator>secrecy ordering. The methodology's strengths are that it needs only node/edge/topic data (no raw text), it is transparent (each of the three scores is inspectable per person), and it separates a priority list (rank order) from a discrimination line (the score threshold between the conspirator cluster and the rest, here around rank 32-33). Its weaknesses, stated plainly for the DA: it ranks, it does not prove guilt; it can over-rank innocent people who are natural communicators (Christina, Julia, Eric) and under-rank conspirators who are quiet (Chris, rank 38-59); and it inherits any error in the topic coding. The generalization claim is that the three ingredients are domain-free, so the same code with new weights and new feature definitions applies to fraud rings, rumor/spam networks, infected-cell detection, and any 'find the hidden subgroup given a few confirmed members' problem, with the only scaling change being approximate centrality at very large size. discrimination line: a reasonable cutoff in the base case is score ~0.49 (between Dolores at 0.487 and the next band), which places the 9 top suspects plus the confirmed core above the line and all 8 known innocents below it; in Requirement 2 the line moves to ~0.53 as the finance topic lifts the middle tier.
