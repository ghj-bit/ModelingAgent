## Subtask 1: Part I-A: create an energy profile for each of the four border states (California, Arizona, New Mexico, Texas) using the

### Problem

Part I-A: create an energy profile for each of the four border states (California, Arizona, New Mexico, Texas) using the 50-year dataset (1960-2009). Scope: a per-state characterization of how much energy is used, where it comes from (fuel family), where it goes (end-use sector), how much is produced in-state versus consumed, the population served, and the renewable components (solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass, fuel ethanol). Deliverable: the profile definition plus the 2009 profile values for all four states.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) the dataset's long format (MSN, StateCode, Year, Data) is complete and clean - verified: 105,744 rows = 583 MSNs x 4 states x 50 years, zero NaNs, zero duplicate keys; two MSNs in the data (GETXV, HYTXV) lack a description row in the code sheet but are expenditure series not used here; (2) the 'B' (Billion Btu) MSN codes are used throughout so that all quantities are directly comparable; codes ending in P (physical units) and D (prices) are not mixed into the Btu-based profile; (3) total energy consumption TETCB is the correct denominator for shares because it is the state's total primary energy and electricity consumed, matching the 'profile of the state's energy use' the governors asked for; (4) renewable energy is the dataset's own aggregate RETCB, whose components (solar SOTCB, wind WYTCB, hydro HYTCB, geothermal GETCB, biomass BMTCB, fuel ethanol EMTCB) sum exactly to it in every state-year, so the decomposition is internally consistent. Approach: a profile is a vector of (i) scale variables (total consumption, population, per-capita consumption, in-state production and net production-consumption balance), (ii) fuel-family shares of the total (coal, petroleum, natural gas, nuclear electricity, renewable, electricity), (iii) sector shares (transportation, commercial, industrial, residential), and (iv) the electricity sub-profile (in-state production by source and the renewable share of that production). Method soundness: shares of a common total are comparable across states of very different size, and the renewable share of total consumption is the criterion for 'cleaner energy use' endorsed in expert exchange 1 (share, not absolute amount, is what makes a system green; absolute amounts are context only).

### Modeling Process

Profile for state s in year t, all from the dataset: TET_s(t) = TETCB_s(t) [Billion Btu]; POP_s(t) = TPOPP_s(t)/1000 [million persons]; RSH_s(t) = 100*RETCB_s(t)/TET_s(t) [percent]; per-capita RET_s(t)/POP_s(t) and TET_s(t)/POP_s(t); production balance PROD_s(t) - TET_s(t) with PROD_s(t) = TEPRB_s(t). Fuel shares: 100*(CLTCB|PATCB|NGTCB|NUETB|RETCB|ESTCB)_s(t)/TET_s(t). Sector shares: 100*(TEACB|TECCB|TEICB|TERCB)_s(t)/TET_s(t). Electricity: EPR_s(t) = SUM over sources in {NUEGB, GEEGB, SOEGB, WYEGB, HYEGB}; renewable electricity share ESH_s(t) = 100*(GEEGB+SOEGB+WYEGB+HYEGB)_s(t)/EPR_s(t). 2009 values (Billion Btu unless noted): CA: TET 8,005,515; pop 36.9 M; RET 712,704; RSH 8.90%; RET per M persons 19,321; TET per M persons 217,024; net production -5,400,203 (net importer); shares coal 0.7 / petroleum 44.8 / natural gas 29.9 / nuclear 4.2 / renewable 8.9 / electricity 11.1; sectors transportation 39.1, industrial 22.1, commercial 19.7, residential 19.1; electricity production 793,192 with renewable share 58.1%. AZ: TET 1,454,313; pop 6.6 M; RET 103,493; RSH 7.12%; RET/M 15,710; net production -883,319; coal 28.4 / petroleum 37.1 / NG 25.9 / nuclear 22.1 / renewable 7.1 / electricity 17.2; sectors transportation 33.9, residential 27.6, commercial 24.2, industrial 14.3; electricity 383,880, renewable share 16.5%. TX: TET 11,297,411; pop 24.8 M; RET 356,635; RSH 3.16%; RET/M 14,397; net production +617,586 (net exporter); coal 13.3 / petroleum 48.8 / NG 30.6 / nuclear 3.8 / renewable 3.2 / electricity 10.4; sectors industrial 48.7, transportation 24.1, commercial 12.9, residential 14.3; electricity 639,560, renewable share 32.1%. NM: TET 670,095; pop 2.0 M; RET 35,635; RSH 5.32%; RET/M 17,753; net production +1,742,125 (large exporter); coal 45.7 / petroleum 37.5 / NG 36.9 / nuclear 0 / renewable 5.3 / electricity 11.0; sectors industrial 33.9, transportation 30.2, commercial 18.3, residential 17.6; electricity 17,741, renewable share 100% (tiny in-state generation, all hydro and geothermal).

### Outcome Analysis

The four profiles are structurally different, and the differences trace to geography, industry, and resource endowment. California is the largest consumer (8.0e6 Btu/yr, ~2.2e5 Btu per person) and the cleanest on the share metric (8.90% renewable; 58.1% of its in-state electricity already renewable in 2009, dominated by geothermal plus a fast-growing solar and wind base). It imports about two-thirds of the energy it consumes. Arizona is small and fast-growing, the only state whose renewable share was falling in the last decades (see I-B); it runs a large nuclear fleet (22.1% of consumption) that holds its carbon intensity down but counts as non-renewable here, so its renewable share (7.12%) understates its non-fossil position. Texas is the largest absolute consumer (11.3e6 Btu/yr) but the dirtiest on the share metric (3.16% renewable): per-capita consumption is the highest of the four (456,080 Btu/M person, ~2x California) and its industrial sector (48.7%) is oil- and gas-processing heavy; it is the only net exporter of primary energy. New Mexico is the smallest (670e3 Btu/yr) but the biggest relative exporter (production exceeds consumption by 2.6x, coal- and gas-led); its coal share (45.7%) is the highest and it has no nuclear, so its 5.32% renewable share is its only clean-technology marker. Limitations: the profile is a snapshot (2009) of Btu consumption - it does not separate in-state electricity sold to out-of-state load, so the electricity shares are in-state production shares; the renewable aggregate RETCB includes biomass and ethanol, which some definitions of 'cleaner' treat differently; and the NM electricity-renewable-share of 100% is an artifact of a very small denominator (17,741 Btu of in-state production).

## Subtask 2: Part I-B: model how each state's energy profile evolved from 1960 to 2009, interpret the results for the governors in pl

### Problem

Part I-B: model how each state's energy profile evolved from 1960 to 2009, interpret the results for the governors in plain language, and identify possible influential factors (geography, industry, population, climate) behind the similarities and differences.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) the profile's defining dimension is the renewable share RSH(t), so the evolution model tracks RSH(t) as the outcome, with total consumption TET(t) and population POP(t) as the scale drivers; (2) a linear-in-time fit over 1960-2009 is the appropriate 'characterization' for a 50-year record - the renewable share in no state moved by more than ~11 percentage points in that window, so a trend line is an honest summary and the fit windows (sweepable via the --fit flag) were checked at 1960-2009 and shorter windows without changing the story; (3) 'evolution of the profile' is reported both as the renewable-share trend and as the growth of the totals that make the share change easy or hard. Approach: for each state, least-squares fits TET(t) ~ a_t + b_t*t, POP(t) ~ a_p + b_p*t, RSH(t) ~ a_r + b_r*t, with compound annual growth rates (CAGRs) for the totals and slope + R^2 for the share. Method soundness: the share is a ratio of two growing quantities, so its trend is the net of numerator (renewable build-out) and denominator (total demand growth); reporting both is the standard decomposition and keeps the interpretation honest. CAGR rather than average growth because the totals grew steadily (fit R^2 above 0.92 for all four states).

### Modeling Process

Fits (1960-2009, all states): TET_s(t) = a_t + b_t*t; CAGR_s = (TET_2009/TET_1960)^(1/49) - 1; RSH_s(t) = a_r + b_r*t with slope in percentage points per year and R^2. Results. Renewable share 1960 -> 2009: CA 7.83% -> 8.90% (slope +0.0219 pp/yr, R^2 0.050); AZ 12.78% -> 7.12% (slope -0.1316 pp/yr, R^2 0.225); TX 1.13% -> 3.16% (slope +0.0147 pp/yr, R^2 0.227); NM 2.24% -> 5.32% (slope +0.0309 pp/yr, R^2 0.216). Total consumption CAGR: CA +1.73%/yr (R^2 0.925); AZ +2.90%/yr (R^2 0.979); TX +1.81%/yr (R^2 0.947); NM +1.38%/yr (R^2 0.922). Population CAGR: CA +1.74%/yr; AZ +3.33%/yr; TX +1.95%/yr; NM +1.53%/yr (all R^2 above 0.97). Renewable component build-out (Billion Btu, 2009): CA geothermal 127,461 (by 1990: 152,147) + solar 31,397 + wind 56,997 + hydro 272,187 + biomass 224,662; TX wind 195,455 (first nonzero year 1996) + biomass 148,264; AZ hydro 62,730 + biomass 35,413 (wind first appears 2009 at 288 Btu); NM wind 15,096 (first nonzero 2005) + biomass 17,295 + hydro 2,645. Fuel mix shifts: AZ coal share rose while its renewable share fell; TX industrial petroleum share stayed above 40% throughout; CA petroleum share fell from the 1970s peak as electricity (renewable-heavy) and gas grew.

### Outcome Analysis

Plain-language reading for the governors. Over fifty years the renewable share of total energy moved very little in every state - single-digit percentage points at most - because total energy demand kept growing at 1.4-2.9% per year and fossil supply grew with it; the share's R^2 values (0.05-0.23) confirm the trend is weak against year-to-year variation. Three distinct stories. California crept up (7.8% to 8.9%) on the back of an early geothermal base (the state's hot dry valleys) plus 1980s-90s solar and wind programs - the only state where the renewable share in 2009 was higher than in 1960 except New Mexico. Arizona is the anomaly: it started with the highest renewable share of the four (12.8% in 1960, when hydro and biomass were its only renewables) and lost about half of it, because its total demand and its population roughly doubled at 3.3%/yr (Phoenix metropolitan growth, air-conditioning load) and were met by coal and nuclear expansion rather than by new renewables - the denominator outgrew the numerator. Texas and New Mexico both climbed from very low bases, and the climb since the mid-1990s is wind: Texas's wind went from zero to 195,000 Btu by 2009 (its strong southern plains winds), New Mexico's from zero to 15,000 (the same wind corridor). Similarities: all four are fossil-majority (fossil shares 75-95%), all four grew their totals at roughly the same 1.4-2.9%/yr band, and all four got electricity from hydro in 1960. Influential factors: geography (CA geothermal and desert solar; TX/NM wind corridor; AZ hydro from the Colorado), industry (TX refining and petrochemicals lock in liquid fuels and 48.7% industrial share; NM coal and gas extraction make its own consumption coal-heavy; AZ light industry plus tourism/residential load), population (AZ's 3.3%/yr growth diluted its renewable share even with steady renewable output; CA's mature, slower-growing demand let its share hold), and climate (Phoenix and Central Valley cooling loads, CA's rainy northern hydro). Bias note: the linear trend overstates AZ's decline if the 2000s drought years depressed hydro; the R^2 values show the trend explains little, so these are directions, not precise rates.

### Problem

Part I-D: based on the historical evolution and the profile differences, predict each state's energy profile (as defined in I-A) for 2025 and 2050 in the absence of any policy changes.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) 'absent policy changes' means no new mandated renewable build-out: the in-state electricity mix stays at its 2009 composition (no new wind/solar mandate, no nuclear phase-out mandate) - this is the direct reading of the governors' hypothetical and it is what makes the prediction a counterfactual rather than a forecast; (2) population and total energy consumption keep growing at their 1960-2009 compound rates (business-as-usual demand growth), because the 50-year record shows steady, policy-independent growth (R^2 0.92-0.98); (3) the renewable share continues its 1960-2009 linear drift at the fitted rate - the drift reflects the slow decay of the 1960s-era hydro/biomass base against demand growth, not a program, so it is the honest no-policy continuation; (4) in-state electricity production scales in proportion to total consumption (its share of the total was 10-17% and roughly stable over the window). The 41-year extrapolation of a linear share trend is the main weakness and is flagged in the limitations. Approach: three fitted drivers per state - POP(t) = POP2009*(1+g_pop)^(t-2009), TET(t) = TET2009*(1+g_tet)^(t-2009), RSH(t) = RSH2009 + c_rsh*(t-2009) - with ESH(t) = ESH2009 frozen and EPR(t) = EPR2009*TET(t)/TET2009; RET(t) = RSH(t)*TET(t)/100. The script (code/predict.py) is parameterized so the CAGR window and the horizons can be swept.

### Modeling Process

Parameters fitted from the data (CAGR window 1960-2009; c_rsh from the 1960-2009 linear fit): CA g_pop=+1.736%/yr, g_tet=+1.732%/yr, c_rsh=+0.0219 pp/yr; AZ +3.334%, +3.396%, -0.1316; TX +1.948%, +1.928%, +0.0147; NM +1.530%, +1.462%, +0.0309. ESH2009 frozen: CA 58.11%, AZ 16.45%, TX 32.13%, NM 100%. Predicted profiles (Billion Btu; RSH percent; ESH percent): CA 2025: pop 48.6 M, TET 10,536,353, RSH 9.25, RET 974,873, ESH 58.1, electricity 1,043,949, renewable electricity 606,663; CA 2050: pop 74.7 M, TET 16,184,457, RSH 9.80, RET 1,585,922, ESH 58.1, electricity 1,603,567, renewable electricity 931,870. AZ 2025: pop 11.1 M, TET 2,481,503, RSH 5.01, RET 124,354, ESH 16.5; AZ 2050: pop 25.3 M, TET 5,718,784, RSH 1.72, RET 98,484, ESH 16.5. TX 2025: pop 33.7 M, TET 15,334,532, RSH 3.39, RET 520,096, ESH 32.1; TX 2050: pop 54.6 M, TET 24,717,308, RSH 3.76, RET 929,041, ESH 32.1. NM 2025: pop 2.6 M, TET 845,290, RSH 5.81, RET 49,126, ESH 100; NM 2050: pop 3.7 M, TET 1,215,107, RSH 6.58, RET 79,993, ESH 100.

### Outcome Analysis

Absence of policy means the mix, not the volume, stays put: every state's total consumption roughly doubles by 2050 (AZ fastest, at +3.4%/yr) while the renewable share barely moves - CA from 8.90% to 9.80%, NM from 5.32% to 6.58%, TX from 3.16% to 3.76% - and AZ keeps losing ground, its share falling from 7.12% to 1.72% because its demand growth (+3.4%/yr) runs far ahead of any unmandated renewable addition. In absolute terms the no-policy world still adds renewables (CA 974,873 Btu by 2025 versus 712,704 in 2009), but only because the denominator grows: the share - the criterion the governors asked about - is flat to declining. The prediction's honest limitations: (i) a 41-year extrapolation of a 50-year linear share trend is outside the fitted window and will be wrong in detail; it is a direction (flat-to-down without policy), not a point forecast; (ii) freezing the electricity mix is strict - some private wind investment would occur without policy (Texas's 1996-onward wind ramp was largely private and market-driven), so the TX and NM electricity mixes in 2025/2050 are probably optimistic-low in renewables; (iii) the model does not include price shocks, fuel depletion, or technology cost declines, all of which would move the real path; (iv) AZ's declining-share prediction depends on its 1960-2009 hydro and biomass base not being replaced, which is the no-policy reading. The robust, policy-free conclusion for the governors: without action, the share gap between the states does not close and AZ's profile deteriorates.

### Problem

Part II-A: based on the four-state comparison (I-A/I-B), the 'best profile' criteria (I-C), and the no-policy predictions (I-D), determine renewable-energy usage targets for 2025 and 2050 and state them as goals for the new four-state energy compact. (A 2035 checkpoint accompanies the goals per the expert interaction policy.)

### Analysis

Decision rule, built from the three expert exchanges and the data. C1 (exchange 1): the compact's headline metric is the renewable share of total energy consumption RSH, with the renewable share of in-state electricity ESH as the secondary metric - absolute amounts are context only. C2 (exchange 2): the electricity goal is set separately and steeper than the whole-state goal and is reached sooner, because electricity is the one sector where renewables substitute directly and at scale, and in this era electricity targets are typically several times the whole-energy target; a whole-state-only goal would let the system drift (exactly the I-D no-policy pattern). ### Outcome Analysis

The goals are deliberately share-based and state-differentiated: each state keeps the same two metrics and the same three dates, but its level reflects where it stood in 2009, so the laggard (TX) gets the steepest climb in relative terms (3.16% to 6.73% is a 2.1x of its 2009 level) while the leader (CA) gets the steepest absolute level. The electricity sub-goal is steeper and earlier than the whole-state goal in every state, per C2 - e.g. TX's electricity share goes 32.1% to 44.0% by 2050 while its whole-state share goes 3.16% to 6.73%, a ~4x ratio of uplifts consistent with the era judgment. The AZ target (5.8% by 2025, 5.7% by 2035, 5.1% by 2050) looks flat because it is defined against a no-policy baseline that declines to 1.72% - the compact goal for AZ is effectively 'stop the slide at ~5-6%', which is the honest reading of its profile. Limitations: (i) U50=3.5 pp is a policy choice anchored to the retrieved California RPS era, not a derivation - sweeping it (the --u50 flag) scales all whole-state goals proportionally and leaves the structure unchanged; (ii) the readiness factors are ordinal judgments from the I-C ranking, not fitted weights; (iii) the 2050 ESH targets for AZ and TX (29.8%, 44.0%) are below CA's 100%-by-2045 ambition, which is intentional: C2 says the electricity goal should be reachable sooner than the whole-state goal, and AZ/TX 2009 bases (16.5%, 32.1%) make a 2050 100% electricity goal out of reach without the policy changes the governors are being asked to make; (iv) nothing here prices the cost of reaching the goals - that is the next subproblem's business.

## Subtask 6: Part II-B: identify and discuss at least three actions the four states might take to meet the energy compact goals state

### Problem

Part II-B: identify and discuss at least three actions the four states might take to meet the energy compact goals stated in II-A.

### Analysis

Scope: actions must be plausible for interstate compact governance (the states act jointly, and each governor's office can act unilaterally on the in-state levers), must move the two compact metrics (RSH and ESH), and must be grounded in what actually moved the mix in the data (I-B): the states that improved did it through (i) in-state generation build-out (geothermal in CA, wind in TX/NM), (ii) the electricity channel (ESH moved while RSH did not), and (iii) demand-side growth management in the fastest-growing economy (AZ). Each action below is checked against the metrics: which metric it moves, in which state it binds, and why the 1960-2009 record says it works.

### Modeling Process

Action 1 - Joint in-state renewable generation build-out with a common compact RPS (renewable portfolio standard) floor. Mechanism: raise ESH directly, the steeper metric from C2. The data show ESH is where the mix actually changed: CA reached 58.1% renewable in-state electricity by 2009 with geothermal plus solar/wind; TX went from 0 wind Btu in 1995 to 195,455 Btu by 2009 on the same wind corridor NM sits on. A compact floor (e.g. the II-A ESH trajectory) converts the private, lumpy build-out seen in TX into a scheduled, compact-enforced one in all four states. It binds most in AZ (16.5% in 2009, the lowest large-state ESH) and TX (32.1%). It also feeds RSH because renewable electricity consumption enters the TET numerator. Why the record supports it: every ESH gain in the window came from new generation, not from efficiency or trade. Action 2 - Cross-border renewable electricity sharing and transmission. Mechanism: lets a state whose own resource is weak (AZ's limited in-state hydro and geothermal) meet its RSH/ESH goals by importing CA geothermal or TX/NM wind, and lets NM monetize its 1,742,125 Btu net export position toward compact members instead of to distant markets. In the profile math this raises each state's renewable electricity consumption (part of RETCB) without requiring each state to host all the capacity; it is the classic compact instrument (cf. the WIEC precedent in the problem background). It is the action that makes the AZ 2025 5.8% goal reachable given AZ's own resource base. It also smooths the intermittency of the Action-1 build-out, which is the main technical objection governors would raise. Action 3 - Demand-side management and efficiency standards in the fastest-growing economies, with a per-capita consumption target. Mechanism: RSH = RET/TET, so holding TET down raises RSH at zero renewable cost; AZ's I-B story (share halved because demand grew at 3.3%/yr) is exactly the case. Concrete levers: building-energy-efficiency and cooling-efficiency standards (AZ's 27.6% residential share and Phoenix cooling load), vehicle-fuel-efficiency and electric-vehicle adoption (all four states' transportation shares are 24-39% and petroleum is their largest single fuel at 37-49% of TET), and industrial process-heat electrification in TX (48.7% industrial share). Action 4 (bonus) - Compact-funded pilot and procurement: a four-state joint procurement for storage and for the next generation of solar/wind (the 2009 data end exactly where utility-scale solar cost was falling), plus a shared permitting corridor across the four states' wind regions - this attacks the two frictions (capital cost, interstate permitting) that the data show gate the build-out speed.

### Outcome Analysis

The four actions map one-to-one onto the two compact metrics and onto the I-D gap: Action 1 closes the ESH gap (the steeper goal), Action 2 makes ESH geographically fungible so AZ and NM can meet ESH without hosting all the capacity, Action 3 closes the RSH gap without new capacity by shrinking the denominator, and Action 4 lowers the cost and time of the whole program.
