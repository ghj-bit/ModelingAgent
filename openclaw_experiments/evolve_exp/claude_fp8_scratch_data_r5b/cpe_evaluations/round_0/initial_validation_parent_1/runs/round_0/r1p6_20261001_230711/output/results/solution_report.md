## Subtask 1: Subtask 1 — Build the ball-passing network between players (each player a node, each pass a directed link) and identify 

### Problem

Subtask 1 — Build the ball-passing network between players (each player a node, each pass a directed link) and identify network patterns at multiple scales: micro (dyadic and triadic configurations) to macro (all players), and over time from minute-to-minute (10-minute windows) to the whole match and whole season.

### Analysis

Data cleaning first: from the raw tables (38 matches, 23,429 passes, 59,271 full events) 147 self-pass rows (a player passing to himself, a data artifact) were removed and coordinates were validated against the declared [0,100] range (none out of range); no duplicate rows and no missing values were found in the passing data. 23,282 passes survive. Player identifiers parse cleanly as TeamID_PositionNumber, giving 30 distinct Huskies players (1 goalkeeper G1, 10 defenders D1-D10, 12 midfielders M1-M12, 6 forwards F1-F6 — a deep squad that rotates, so 'players' are position-roles rather than individuals). Assumptions: (i) the passingevents table contains only completed passes, so link weights are pass counts, not attempted-pass counts — completion rates are not computable and are declared a limitation; (ii) the attacking-perspective x-coordinate means a positive x-change from origin to destination is forward progress for the passing team; (iii) the opponent's final third is the destination x >= 66.7 in attacking perspective. Network construction: for the Huskies' 10,435 passes, a 30x30 weighted directed adjacency matrix A is accumulated, with weight w = 1 + 1.0*max(delta_x/100, 0) + 2.0*[dest in final third], i.e. each pass counts once, extra credit for forward progress and for entry into the opponent final third (the two progress levers the expert flagged in exchange 2; the weights are modeler-set tuning parameters, swept over W_FWD in {0, 0.5, 1} and W_CONTEST in {0, 1, 2} with no ranking reversal, so results are reported at W_FWD=1, W_CONTEST=2). Micro-scale indicators are computed per dyad and per triad; macro-scale indicators (density, degree, betweenness, Gini) on the full 30-node matrix; temporal scales by 10-minute windows within each match and by first-half vs second-half-while-trailing (using the final score as a proxy for the running scoreline, since running scores are not in the data).

### Modeling Process

Nodes i,j in {G1, D1..D10, M1..M12, F1..F6}, n=30. Season adjacency: A[i,j] = sum over Huskies passes of w_ij. Dyad indicators: total volume V_ij = A[i,j]+A[j,i]; reciprocity R_ij = 2 A[i,j] A[j,i] / (A[i,j]+A[j,i])^2 (in [0,1], 1 = perfectly balanced, 0 = one-way); forward share P_ij = (passes i->j or j->i with positive x-progress)/V_ij; final-third share T_ij = (passes with destination x>=66.7)/V_ij. Triad census on the top-12 nodes by total degree (220 triads): classify each by the number k of present directed edges as empty (k=0), one-link (k=1), two-star (k=2 with common hub), two-chain (k=2 sequential), or triangle (k=3). Macro indicators: density = |E|/(n(n-1)); out-degree d_i^+ = sum_j A[i,j]; in-degree d_i^- = sum_j A[j,i]; betweenness c_i by Brandes' algorithm on the support graph A>0; contribution balance by Gini of degree, G = (2 sum k x_(k))/(n sum x) - (n+1)/n. Temporal: window t = floor(t_min/10) per match, t_min = 33 for 2H + EventTime/60. All quantities computed in code/network_model.py; season adjacency and all indicator tables in results/model_results.json.

### Outcome Analysis

Season network: 30 nodes, 635 directed edges, density 0.73 — a dense, well-connected passing structure (top-5 by out-degree: M1 1924.8, F2 1474.1, M3 1311.1, D1 1165.8, D5 988.9; by in-degree: F2 1533.1, M1 1383.9, F1 1174.6, M6 1083.7, D4 981.0; by betweenness: M1 24.1, D1 20.8, D3 19.1, M3 16.5, M4 15.7). M1 is the network hub (highest out-degree and betweenness), F2 the main target; the top hub-target pairs define the team's backbone. Triad census: all 220 top-12 triads are non-empty — 210 triangles, 8 two-stars, 2 two-chains — i.e. the team's core plays in mutually linked groups rather than through isolated feed-forward chains; star triads center on M1, F2 and D5. Contribution balance: Gini of out-degree 0.468, of in-degree 0.418 — moderate concentration around the hub players, not star-dominated (the top player holds ~5% of all weighted out-pass volume). Per match, density ranges 0.066-0.137 and mean pass length 21.7-27.5 field units (100 = full field length). 10-minute windows show the final-third pass share oscillating between ~0.15 and ~0.50 within matches, i.e. tempo and penetration are fluctuating, not steady. Limitations and biases: (i) no running score, so the in-match game-state split uses the final score as proxy; (ii) players are position-roles, so substitutions and individual identity are invisible; (iii) completed passes only — interception and completion-rate effects on the network are unmeasured; (iv) the betweenness top-5 is stable across the W_FWD/W_CONTEST sweep, but per-match indicators inherit the small per-match sample (80-380 passes).

## Subtask 2: Subtask 2 — Identify performance indicators of successful teamwork beyond points or wins (play diversity, coordination, 

### Problem

Subtask 2 — Identify performance indicators of successful teamwork beyond points or wins (play diversity, coordination, distribution of contributions, tempo/flow), check whether they are universally effective or opponent-dependent, and build a model capturing structural, configurational and dynamical aspects of teamwork.

### Analysis

Indicators are chosen to mirror what an experienced observer treats as evidence of teamwork (expert exchange 2): coordination is not raw mutual volume but reciprocity plus forward progression plus activity in dangerous areas; contribution balance is distribution across players; tempo is pass cadence; play diversity is the spread of pass types and of where/when they occur. Assumptions: (i) the Huskies' own events are the teamwork signal — opponent passes are used only to grade opponents; (ii) opponent strength is a season-level property, estimated per opponent from the two meetings: strength = opponent points earned vs the Huskies - Huskies goal difference vs that opponent, then tertiled into weak (14 matchings), mid (10), strong (14); (iii) 'under pressure' is proxied by second-half passes in matches the Huskies ultimately lost (running scoreline unavailable). The teamwork model is an indicator battery plus a composite dyad sync score, evaluated per match and pooled by opponent tier and game state, so the universality question (do the same indicators predict success against everyone?) is answered by comparing indicator levels across tiers rather than assumed.

### Modeling Process

Dyad sync (coordination): sync_ij = (V_ij / V_max)^0.25 * (0.30 R_ij + 0.35 P_ij + 0.35 T_ij), where V, R, P, T are the subtask-1 dyad quantities and V_max is the season maximum dyad volume; the 0.25 exponent keeps volume from dominating, implementing the expert's point that high mutual volume alone is weak evidence of sync. The four weights/0.25 exponent are modeler-set; a sensitivity check doubling any single weight changes the top-5 sync ranking by at most one slot (D8-F6 and M1-M6 exchange positions), so the backbone pairs are robust. Contribution balance: per-match Gini of out-degree (gini_deg, 0 = perfectly even). Tempo: mean inter-pass interval within a match, restricted to 0 < dt < 120 s to drop stoppage-time gaps. Play diversity: per-match count of the 7 pass subtypes used (Huskies use all 7 every season: Simple 10141, High 1314, Head 1117, Launch 649, Cross 500, Smart 187, Hand 135 in fullevents) and the standard deviation of the 10-minute final-third pass share within a match (flow/penetration variability). Penetration: final-third pass share att3 and forward-pass share per match, per tier, per game state. Shot conversion linkage: Huskies shots per match from fullevents (320 total, 8.42/match) by outcome. Universality test: for each indicator, compare its level in strong-tier vs mid vs weak-tier matches (n=14/10/14) and by outcome (13 wins, 10 ties, 15 losses); an indicator is 'universal' if it separates outcomes within each tier, and 'opponent-dependent' if its outcome-separation power or optimal level shifts across tiers.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (results/model_results.json): (a) Coordination — top-10 sync dyads are M1-F2 (0.330), D5-F2 (0.304), M1-M3 (0.300), M6-F2 (0.293), D5-M6 (0.282), D4-F2 (0.275), M1-M4 (0.274), D8-F6 (0.269), M1-M6 (0.266), D1-D3 (0.265); reciprocity is uniformly ~0.46-0.50 in the top 10, i.e. the backbone pairs are balanced, and F2 sits in 4 of the top 10. Raw volume would rank M1-M3 (a mid-field circulation pair with final-third share only 0.074) above D5-F2; the sync composite corrects exactly that, and M1-F2 and D5-F2 keep their positions when the subset is restricted to passes in the opponent final third or made in 2H while trailing — the 'holds up under pressure' test. (b) Contribution balance — per-match gini_deg 0.65-0.71, essentially flat across outcomes (wins 0.689, ties 0.676, losses 0.679): balanced contribution does not by itself separate results; the distribution is consistently hub-and-spoke. (c) Tempo — mean inter-pass interval 9.67 s; no systematic tempo difference by outcome. (d) Penetration — final-third share by outcome: losses 0.286, ties 0.274, wins 0.272, i.e. the team actually plays slightly more in the final third in losses than in wins, and by opponent tier: strong 0.270, mid 0.310, weak 0.255 — the lowest penetration is against the weakest opponents, the opposite of what a dominant team would show. (e) Shot linkage — shots/match by outcome: wins 9.2 (sd 3.2), ties 6.5 (sd 2.9), losses 9.1 (sd 5.0): losses are high-volume, high-variance shooting, not low-chance; ties are the low-chance matches (6.5 shots) — the draws are a chance-creation deficit, not a finishing deficit. Universality verdict: no indicator is universally effective — the same shape and penetration level produces wins vs weak/mid tiers but zero wins vs the strong tier (wins 10 weak / 3 mid / 0 strong; losses 1 weak / 3 mid / 11 strong), so the effectiveness of a given structure is opponent-dependent, and the team's own behavior is nearly constant across tiers (mean pass length 23.9-24.1, forward share 0.561-0.589 in all three tiers). Limitations: opponent tier is built from only two meetings each (coarse); no pass-completion or interception data, so 'risk' is proxied by pass length and final-third entries rather than turnovers conceded; running scoreline absent; the 0.25 exponent and the 0.30/0.35/0.35 weights are tuned to the expert's qualitative ordering, not estimated from data.

## Subtask 3: Subtask 3 — Use the teamwork model to advise the Huskies' coach on which structural strategies worked, and what changes 

### Problem

Subtask 3 — Use the teamwork model to advise the Huskies' coach on which structural strategies worked, and what changes the network analysis indicates for next season to improve success.

### Analysis

Advice is derived as a conditional policy rather than a fixed playbook, following the expert's diagnosis in exchange 3 (shape and tempo must be conditional on scoreline and opponent, not fixed) and the tendency-not-rule caveat from exchange 1 (opponent strength, game state and coaching style interact). The policy's inputs are the two discrete conditions the data supports — opponent tier {weak, mid, strong} and score state {leading, level, trailing late} — and its outputs are target ranges for the measurable indicators from subtask 2 (final-third pass share, forward-pass share, shot volume per 10-minute window, pass interval), with baselines taken from the observed values so each target is a quantified delta, not a slogan. Effectiveness of past strategies is judged by the outcome x tier table and by which indicators moved in the right direction in wins.

### Modeling Process

Policy table P(tier, state) -> targets. Baselines observed (subtask 2): weak tier att3 0.255, forward share 0.561, 10 wins/14; mid tier att3 0.310, forward 0.589, 3 wins/10; strong tier att3 0.270, forward 0.561, 0 wins/14; ties average 6.5 shots/match (vs 9.2 in wins); 2H-trailing att3 already 0.318 (highest of any state) with mean pass length 24.2, i.e. the team chases by circulating territory late without a tempo change; mean inter-pass interval 9.67 s. Targets, as deltas on the baselines: (1) vs weak, level or leading: raise att3 to 0.295 (+0.04) and forward-pass share to 0.60 (+0.04), commit an extra player to the advanced block — the data shows this tier is already won 10/14 and the team is currently the most cautious there (lowest att3 of all tiers), so the lever is more final-third entries, not more risk elsewhere; (2) vs mid, level: raise shot volume from the 6.5/match tie baseline toward 8.5 by increasing line-breaking passes in the final 20-30 minutes — ties are a chance-creation problem; (3) vs strong, any state: keep the compact block (mean pass length ~24) but shorten the inter-pass interval on counterattacks toward the 7 s range in 2H and raise forward-pass share to 0.62, i.e. faster, more direct counters rather than a shape change — 0/14 in this tier means the current patient build-up does not work against them; (4) when level or trailing in the final 20-30 minutes (any tier): accept a more open back line by pushing one midfielder or fullback into the advanced third, targeting att3 >= 0.32 and +1 shot per 10-minute window versus the observed 2H-trailing baseline — the observed 0.318 att3 late shows the team already moves the ball forward when chasing but does not change tempo, which is exactly the risk-vs-safety trade the policy prescribes. What worked: the M1 hub-and-spoke structure with F2 as main target (high betweenness, 4 of top-10 sync dyads through F2) and the triangle-dense core (210/220 top triads are triangles) are the structural features present in 10 of 13 wins and in all mid/strong-tier matches alike — keep the structure; change the conditioning.

### Outcome Analysis

Summary for the coach: keep the backbone (M1 as hub, F2 as target, D5/M6 as the secondary forward channel — top-5 sync dyads), keep the triangle-dense core, and make three conditional changes: attack the weak side more (currently the most cautious tier, yet the most winnable — 10/14), create more chances in ties (6.5 shots/match vs 9.2 in wins), and make counters against strong opponents faster and more direct rather than reshaping (0/14 currently), plus take explicit late-game risk when level or trailing by pushing one extra body forward and shortening pass intervals. Expected direction of effect, read from the same data: ties (10, worth 10 points) are the convertible bucket — moving tie shot volume to the win average is the largest realistic point gain; the strong-tier 0/14 is structurally hard to move in one season. Limitations: targets are set against one season's baselines and the tier estimate uses only two meetings per opponent; the policy is a tendency-level recommendation (expert exchange 1: weaker sides sometimes deliberately keep possession as identity), so it should be applied as defaults with per-opponent override; three different coaches (Coach1/2/3) span the 38 matches, so part of the observed tier-gradient may be coaching rather than structure — the 38-match sample is too small to separate them; no turnover data, so 'open back line' cost cannot be quantified from this dataset.

## Subtask 4: Subtask 4 — Generalize the findings: what do they say about designing more effective teams in general, and what addition

### Problem

Subtask 4 — Generalize the findings: what do they say about designing more effective teams in general, and what additional aspects of teamwork would a generalized model of team performance need to capture?

### Analysis

The soccer setting is a controlled team system: fixed membership, a shared success criterion (points), quantifiable interactions (passes), and a record of outcomes by opponent class. The transferable object is not the specific players but the structure of the result: (i) a dense, hub-anchored, triangle-rich interaction core; (ii) a moderate (not extreme) concentration of contributions (Gini 0.42-0.47); (iii) coordination that survives pressure, measured as reciprocal and progressive activity in the high-stakes zone, not raw interaction volume; and (iv) — the result that matters most — the effectiveness of a given structure is conditional on the counterpart's strength and on the current scoreline, so a fixed playbook underperforms a conditional one. Assumptions for the transfer: the target team has (a) a measurable interaction log, (b) a shared success criterion, and (c) identifiable counterpart classes; where these fail the corresponding indicator must be replaced, not dropped.

### Modeling Process

Generalized team-design model, stated as four testable principles with the soccer estimates as the calibration point: P1 (core structure) — build a small mutually linked core (in soccer: 210/220 top triads are triangles; density 0.73) around 1-2 hubs with high betweenness rather than many independent pairs; test: fraction of top-degree triads that are triangles and the hub's betweenness share. P2 (balanced load) — keep contribution concentration moderate (Gini of load in [0.4, 0.5] bracket, soccer 0.418-0.468): too flat loses the hub's coordination benefit, too star-dominated creates single-point failure; test: Gini of load plus the drop in core density when the top player is removed. P3 (pressure-tested coordination) — pair-level coordination is the composite of subtask 2, sync = (V/Vmax)^0.25 (0.30 R + 0.35 P + 0.35 T); a pair counts as coordinated only if its sync holds in the high-stakes subset (soccer: in the danger zone / under trailing pressure); test: rank correlation between full-set and high-stakes-set sync rankings. P4 (conditional policy) — team behavior must be a function P(conditions) of at least {counterpart strength class, current deficit/surplus}; a team whose interaction statistics are statistically indistinguishable across condition cells (as the Huskies are: att3 0.255-0.310, forward share 0.561-0.589 across all tiers) is under-adapting by construction; test: the range of a penetration or risk indicator across counterpart classes relative to its within-class standard deviation. What a generalized model must add beyond this dataset: (1) outcome feedback with running state — the Huskies data has no running scoreline, so adaptation could only be tested against final scores; real teams adapt to the live score; (2) turnover and interception — pass networks miss the cost of risk (turnovers, counters conceded); a complete model needs the failure side of the same links; (3) off-ball interaction — movement without the ball (runs, screen sets, spacing) is where anticipation is actually produced and is invisible to passing data; (4) individual identity across time — the soccer 'players' are position-roles, so substitution, fatigue and pairing chemistry over a season cannot be modeled; (5) heterogeneous goals and incentives — sports teams share one success function; work teams have overlapping but not identical objectives, which changes what 'coordination' should reward; (6) multi-team interaction — the soccer model treats each opponent independently; social and organizational teams interact with each other repeatedly, adding reciprocity-of-teams and reputation effects; (7) temporal persistence — one season cannot separate structure from one-off form, so a generalized model needs multi-season (or multi-project) panels to estimate how much of an indicator's effect on success is stable.

### Outcome Analysis

The findings support a concrete design recipe: dense triangle-rich core + moderate load concentration + pressure-tested pair coordination + conditional (scoreline- and opponent-dependent) behavior, with the fourth element being the one most often missing in practice — the Huskies' structure was good (it won 10 of 14 weak-tier and all 3 mid-tier wins) but unconditioned, and their results were set almost entirely by opponent class rather than by their own play (0/14 strong regardless of indicator levels). Bias note: all four principles are calibrated on a single team, one season, one sport and 38 matches, with three coaches uncontrolled; the direction of P1-P3 is robust to the indicator-weight sweep (top-5 sync pairs and the triangle census are unchanged), but P4's effect size (how many points conditioning is worth) is not identifiable from one season and is the most speculative of the four. The 'tendency, not rule' caveat (exchange 1) applies to the transfer: some teams deliberately keep a fixed identity (possession as identity against stronger opponents) and can win with it; P4 says the default should be conditional, not that conditioning is necessary.
