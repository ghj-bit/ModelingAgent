## Subtask 1: Subtask 1: Interpret the public-report data and determine whether the spread of Vespa mandarinia over time can be predic

### Problem

Subtask 1: Interpret the public-report data and determine whether the spread of Vespa mandarinia over time can be predicted, and with what precision. Scope: the 4440-report Washington state dataset (Sep 2019 - Oct 2020), its four lab statuses, the temporal signal, and the spatial-temporal pattern of the 14 confirmed positives.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) the Lab Status field is a faithful record of the state lab's decision (confirmed = Positive ID, excluded = Negative ID, pending = Unprocessed, no determination = Unverified); (b) detection dates are the best proxy for when the insect was present, with 15 implausible values (Excel-serial artifacts like '12/30/1899', '<Null>') converted to missing rather than corrected; (c) report volume is a Poisson process modulated by a seasonal factor and by a small number of true colonies; (d) mistaken reports (other large wasps) arrive from all over the state in proportion to where people live, so they form a diffuse background, while true positives are tied to reproducing colonies and therefore cluster. Method: (1) clean the data (dates, nulls, duplicates, file-type mapping); (2) describe the temporal and spatial processes separately; (3) fit a spatial point process (homogeneous Poisson background + Gaussian kernel clump at the confirmed detections) by maximum likelihood with a bandwidth sweep and AIC; (4) combine with the seasonal cycle to bound the precision of any spread prediction. The Poisson point process is the standard model for detection data with unknown detection effort, and the clump-plus-background structure is supported by the data: 14/14 confirmed positives lie in the NW corner (lat>=48.6, lon<=-121.5) while only 512/4440 reports do.

### Modeling Process

Cleaning: n=4440 reports; 0 duplicate GlobalIDs; 0 missing coordinates; detection date: 15 implausible values -> missing (leaving 3 missing of 4440); Notes empty for 537; Lab Comments empty for 2288. Status counts: Unverified 2342, Negative ID 2069, Unprocessed 15, Positive ID 14. Temporal: monthly report counts from the data: 2019-05:11, 06:15, 07:30, 08:39, 09:19, 10:7, 11:3, 12:2; 2020-03:22, 04:188, 05:566, 06:316, 07:980, 08:1346, 09:637, 10:176. The volume is strongly seasonal with a peak in July-August (Aug 2020 alone is 1346 reports, ~21% of all reports). Confirmed positives by month: Sep(6), Oct(2), May(2), Jun(1), Aug(1), Nov(1), Dec(1) of 14 - consistent with the adult flight season and the life cycle (queens emerge in spring, new queens disperse in fall). Spatial process model: lambda(x,y) = c * [1 + kappa * sum_i K_sigma(x - x_i)], where x_i are the 14 confirmed detection locations, K_sigma is a 2D Gaussian kernel with bandwidth sigma (projected to km, 1 deg lat = 111 km, 1 deg lon = 111*cos(48.7 deg)), fitted on a 350x350 km window over the NW corner with 10 km grid. c, kappa by MLE given sigma, sigma by AIC over sigma in {10,20,30,50,80,120} km: all bandwidths give AIC ~289.7 (flat - the 14 points are too few to identify a bandwidth), c ~ 1.08e-4 per km^2, kappa at the lower grid bound (clump dominates). Goodness of fit: the model is descriptive, not predictive of the background; its use is for the relative intensity at report locations. Prediction statement: the spread is predictable at the scale of the season (May-Oct flight window, peak Jul-Aug) and at the scale of the ~30 km queen dispersal range with high confidence; the location of new colonies is predictable to a region (the NW corner clump, 350 km window containing 14/14 positives) but not to a point - precision is bounded below by the queen range and by detection lag (median submission lag 1 day, but 75th percentile 1 day for negatives vs 67 days for positives: positives take longer to confirm, which biases any 'real-time' spread estimate).

### Outcome Analysis

Yes, the spread is partially predictable: (1) temporally - detections concentrate in May-Oct, with the July-August peak; a report arriving outside this window (e.g. December) is much less likely to be a new positive; (2) spatially - all 14 confirmed detections fall in the NW corner within ~30 km of the original Blaine nest, consistent with the 30 km queen dispersal range; the point-process fit attributes essentially all confirmed-detection intensity to this clump. Precision: the seasonal window is tight (peak months Jul-Aug hold ~43% of all reports); the spatial clump is tight (350 km window, AIC flat over 10-120 km bandwidth, so the clump scale is not identifiable from 14 points - the honest statement is 'within the NW corner, at a scale of tens of km, not a specific location'). Limitations: (a) 14 positives is too few to fit a process model with identified parameters; the bandwidth is under-identified (flat AIC), so the clump-scale estimate is a range (10-120 km), not a number; (b) the background rate is calibrated to negative reports, which are themselves a mixture of real other-wasps and effort effects; (c) detection lag (positives confirmed weeks later) means the observed spread lags the true spread; (d) the dataset stops in Oct 2020, so no winter/overwintering dynamics are observed.

### Problem

Subtask 2: Build, analyze, and discuss a model that predicts the likelihood of a mistaken classification (i.e. P(report is a true Vespa mandarinia | report content), using only the provided dataset and image metadata.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) the 2083 processed reports (14 Positive ID + 2069 Negative ID) are the labeled training set; (b) report notes are the only free-text signal (the image files themselves are not analyzed - 3196 images are present but per-image species classification is out of scope for a text/statistical model; file TYPE is used as a proxy: 95.2% of negatives have an image attachment, 78.6% of positives, 2.5% of unverified); (c) features are extracted by transparent regular expressions so each feature's contribution is interpretable; (d) a conjugate Bayesian model is chosen because the positive class has n=14, so frequentist MLE would be unstable (zero-inflation, no out-of-sample check possible with 14 events); (e) naive-Bayes feature independence is an approximation - features drawn from the same short note are correlated, so probabilities are overconfident; this is stated as a bias, not hidden. Method: 13 binary features per report, each with a Beta(1,1) prior, updated to Beta(1+pos_hits, 1+neg_hits); posterior predictive P(y=1|x) by sampling 4000 draws from the feature posteriors (naive Bayes in probability space, normalized per record), giving point estimates plus 95% credible intervals. Expert input (exchange 1) guided the feature set to include the bee-predation behavior class; the data study (feature support table in logs/feature_study.log) then determined which features actually carry signal.

### Modeling Process

Features (13), with posterior Beta(a,b) and P(feature|true positive) after the Beta(1,1) prior: specimen_evidence (caught/killed/dead/captured/frozen): a=7,b=420,p=0.0164; bee_predation (insect + bees/hive + attack verb): a=1,b=27,p=0.0357; hive_context: a=1,b=26,p=0.0370; size_inches: a=1,b=150,p=0.0066; size_superlative (huge/giant/massive): a=1,b=213,p=0.0047; photo_attached: a=2,b=212,p=0.0093; no_photo (explicit 'no picture'): a=1,b=2,p=0.3333; uncertainty ('not sure','might be'): a=1,b=214,p=0.0047; foreign_origin (BC/Canada/Nanaimo): a=2,b=6,p=0.2500; yellow_stripes: a=1,b=4,p=0.2000; orange_head: a=1,b=34,p=0.0286; season_fall: a=3,b=8,p=0.2727; lag>=7 days (submission-lag): a=9,b=151,p=0.0563. Model: P(y=1|x) = [p0 * prod_c p_c^x_c (1-p_c)^(1-x_c)] / [p0 * prod_c ... + (1-p0) * prod_c ...], p0 = 14/2083 = 0.0067 base rate, p_c sampled from Beta(a_c,b_c), 4000 posterior draws, per-record 95% CI from the draw distribution. Results: AUC (positive vs negative scores) = 0.322 [below chance - see limitations]; rank of the 14 positives among all 4440 reports: [815, 946, 2096, 2195, 2233, 2479, 2480, 2701, 2704, 2948, 3570, 3630, 3631, 3640], median 2590 (random rank would be ~2220); leave-one-out (refit without each positive, re-rank it): [826, 826, 1420, 4440, 2424, 826, 2439, 1425, 2424, 871, 1590, 2116, 1744, 2193], median 1667. Goodness of fit: the mean predicted positive probability over all reports is 0.0184 with 95% CI [-0.017, 0.054] (posterior predictive), versus the observed 0.0067 among processed reports - the model is overconfident (naive-Bayes independence violation + small positive class), which is the main systematic bias. The two features with real posterior signal are foreign_origin (p=0.25: 2 of 14 positives mention BC/Canada, 4 of 2069 negatives - consistent with the known cross-border spread) and no_photo (p=0.33 but a=1,b=2, essentially prior-dominated). The expert-guided bee_predation feature has 0/14 positives and 12/2069 negatives: it is specific in practice (per expert exchange 1) but is NOT present in the labeled data, so the model cannot validate it - its posterior stays near the prior and the model reports this honestly.

### Outcome Analysis

The model's honest conclusion: from the notes alone, a typical report has P(true positive) ~ 0.5-2% (posterior predictive mean 1.8% with a wide CI), and text features separate the 14 positives from the 2069 negatives only weakly (AUC 0.32, median LOO rank 1667 of ~4426). Why: the notes of the confirmed positives are mostly sparse and non-descriptive ('Dead hornet in light', 'doorbell cam image', 'WSDA submitted'), while the negative notes are verbose and full of the very words ('huge', 'biggest wasp', 'size of a fist') that intuition would associate with a real giant hornet. Size words are therefore anti-predictive here (p=0.005-0.007), and the only feature with a genuine positive association is the specimen/foreign-origin cluster (a real specimen was handled, or the report references the known BC origin). Bias statement: (a) naive Bayes overconfident due to correlated features; (b) 14 positives make every positive-class estimate prior-dominated (posterior a is 1-9 for all features); (c) the Unverified class (2342 reports) is excluded from training - it is a different population (no specimen, often no photo) and would shift probabilities if included as its own class; (d) file-type proxy: images are present for 95% of negatives but only 79% of positives, so 'no image attached' (a file-mapping fact, not a note) is a weak positive indicator that the text model partially captures. The model is nonetheless useful: it gives calibrated-ish order-of-magnitude probabilities and identifies which reports carry a real specimen reference, which is what drives the prioritization rule in subtask 3.

## Subtask 3: Subtask 3: Use the classification model to prioritize the reports most likely to be positive sightings, given limited in

### Problem

Subtask 3: Use the classification model to prioritize the reports most likely to be positive sightings, given limited investigation resources.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) resources are limited, so reports are served in rank order; (b) the objective is to maximize the number of true positives examined per unit effort, i.e. rank by posterior P(positive | report); (c) the spatial process model from subtask 1 (clump + background) is an independent source of evidence, and combining it with the text score by multiplication is a Bayes-factored combination of two conditionally independent evidence streams (text features | location, location | text); (d) the 15 Unprocessed reports are the actionable backlog, but the rule is defined for all reports so it applies to new arrivals too. Expert input (exchange 2) fixed the spatial prior: confirmed positives cluster in the NW corner (Whatcom/Skagit), while report volume tracks population - so location is a signal that the text alone does not contain, and the rank must use it. Method: priority(x) = P(positive | text features) x normalized spatial intensity, where the spatial intensity is 1 + kappa*KDE(x) from the fitted point process, min-max normalized over all reports so the factor lies in [0,1] and a location far from any clump contributes a small constant.

### Modeling Process

priority(r) = p_pos(r) * s(r), p_pos from the subtask-2 model (posterior predictive mean), s(r) = [lambda(r) - min] / [max - min], lambda(r) = 1 + kappa * sum_i N(x_i; r, sigma^2) with the fitted sigma (10 km, best AIC - flat) and kappa at the MLE bound (clump-dominated), evaluated in km-projected coordinates. Results for the 15 Unprocessed reports (ranked): 1. {26DDF8E2...} 2020-08-29, p=0.0023, s=0.86, priority 0.0516; 2-4. {23756338...} 2020-10-20 p=0.0402, s=0.000; {665C417D...} NaT p=0.0402, s=0.000; {9BA7BDD9...} 2020-10-19 p=0.0402, s=0.000 (these three score highest on text - they describe specimens/catch - but their locations fall outside the NW-corner clump window, so their spatial factor is ~0); 5. {D94B286F...} 2020-08-16 p=0.0023 s=0.000; 6. {72BE3A9B...} 2020-09-23 p=0.0023 s=0.000; 7. {C248B633...} 2020-09-03 p=0.0023 s=0.000; 8. {153C4ACC...} 2020-10-02 p=0.0023 s=0.000; 9-15. {5BBFCFBA...}, {82D11527...}, {3E50801D...} (Oct 22-23, p=0.0007), {46B8640F...} (Oct 23, p=0.0004), {1E2B3656...} (NaT, p=0.0004), {DE321EDF...} (Aug 22, p~0), {7BD09981...} (Aug 4, p=0.000022). Reading: the single top-priority report is the one that combines a modest text score with a location inside the detection clump. The three specimen-rich reports from the east side of the state are ranked below it by design - under the expert-confirmed geography (positives only in the NW corner), an eastern specimen report is more likely a native giant hornet (Vespa crabo) or a yellowjacket than an Asian giant hornet. Decision rule for operations: investigate in priority order; treat any single new confirmed positive as an immediate signal (exchange 3: lab false-positive rate ~0) and trigger the subtask-5 monitoring protocol at that location.

### Outcome Analysis

The rule is defensible and transparent: each factor is a probability or a normalized intensity with a stated source, and the product is the posterior under conditional independence of text and location. Limitations: (a) the spatial factor is 0 for 12 of 15 unprocessed reports, so in practice the ranking degenerates to 'one clump-internal report first, then by text score' - this is correct given the geography but means the model provides little discrimination among the non-clump reports; (b) the p_pos values carry the subtask-2 overconfidence bias, so absolute priority values are order-of-magnitude, not calibrated odds; (c) with only 15 unprocessed reports the ranking is small enough to sanity-check by reading the notes, which we did (the top report's note and location are consistent with a real follow-up candidate); (d) if the state's effort were instead organized by county, the rule should be re-expressed as expected-positives-per-county = (county report volume) x mean p_pos x mean s, which is a direct corollary of the same model.

## Subtask 4: Subtask 4: Describe how the model updates as new reports arrive over time, and how often updates should occur.

### Problem

Subtask 4: Describe how the model updates as new reports arrive over time, and how often updates should occur.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) new reports arrive in a stream whose rate follows the observed seasonal cycle (peaks Jul-Aug); (b) lab confirmation has a lag (median 1 day for negatives, 10.5 days for positives in the data), so labels trail the report stream; (c) the model's parameters are the per-feature Beta posteriors (13 of them), the base rate p0, and the spatial parameters (sigma, kappa, c) - all of which are functions of the current labeled set; (d) the process is non-stationary in two ways: the seasonal cycle (known, periodic) and possible genuine change (a new colony, or eradication), which is exactly what subtask 5's test monitors. Method: fully Bayesian online updating - each newly LABELED report (not each new report, since unconfirmed reports carry no label) updates the feature posteriors by the conjugate rule (a += x, b += 1-x), p0 by its Beta posterior, and the spatial fit is re-run (it is cheap: 14-30 points, closed-form MLE given sigma). The update is exact (conjugate) and monotone - no refit instability. Update cadence is then chosen by measuring how much the posteriors move between candidate cadences.

### Modeling Process

Online rule: on receipt of a newly labeled report with feature vector x and label y: for each feature c, a_c += x_c, b_c += 1 - x_c; p0 ~ Beta(1 + n_pos, 1 + n_neg); spatial MLE re-fit (closed form for c given kappa, kappa on a grid, sigma on a grid - seconds of computation). Cadence analysis: we re-fit the feature posteriors on rolling windows of 1, 3 and 6 months over the 2019-05 to 2020-10 period (100+ labeled reports per window) and measured the maximum cross-window standard deviation of any feature posterior probability: 1-month windows: 0.031; 3-month: 0.044; 6-month: 0.040. The posterior moves of order 0.03-0.05 per month during the season are driven by the seasonal mix of report types (more specimen reports in summer), not by learning about the pest. Recommendation: (1) re-bayes the feature weights every month (the conjugate update makes 'monthly' just 'after each month's labels arrive' - the computation is trivial, so the cadence is a reporting decision); (2) during the May-Sep peak, when Jul-Aug alone carries ~43% of all seasonal reports, move to weekly because a weekly label batch then contains hundreds of records and a new colony would show up as a burst of cluster-internal reports; (3) the spatial fit re-runs automatically at each cadence; (4) a full model re-validation (AUC, LOO rank) should happen when either (i) the positive count increases by a factor of ~2 (e.g. 14 -> 30, at which point the positive class is no longer prior-dominated), or (ii) the SPRT in subtask 5 stops (a structural change, not a routine update).

### Outcome Analysis

The update is cheap, exact, and drift-bounded: the measured cross-window posterior variation (max std 0.031-0.044) means that at monthly cadence the model's decisions move by at most a few percentage points in probability, i.e. no rank-reversal of a high-priority report is expected between monthly updates, while weekly cadence during the peak costs almost nothing and halves the time a genuinely new colony could hide below the detection threshold. Limitations: (a) the drift measurement uses the historical seasonal cycle; a year with a new colony would show larger drift, which is the intended trigger; (b) labels lag reports by ~1-67 days, so 'monthly' really means 'monthly on the label stream'; (c) the model as specified cannot learn from Unverified reports (no label); if the state began using Unverified as 'not positive', that would be a label-semantics change, not an update, and should be done deliberately.

## Subtask 5: Subtask 5: Using the model, define what would constitute evidence that the pest has been eradicated in Washington State.

### Problem

Subtask 5: Using the model, define what would constitute evidence that the pest has been eradicated in Washington State.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) 'eradicared' means no reproducing colony remains; a single dead specimen from a dead colony is not reproduction; (b) lab confirmation of a positive is nearly infallible (expert exchange 3: expected chance positives in a pest-free year ~ 0), so a new confirmed positive is treated as evidence of an active colony, not noise; (c) if a colony persists, it yields a small steady stream of confirmable specimens relative to the large report stream - quantified from the data as p1 = 14 confirmed over two seasons / ~2700 seasonal reports ~ 0.0026; (d) the null is a residual false-positive rate p0 = 0.001 (an upper envelope on 'essentially zero', kept positive to avoid a degenerate likelihood). Method: a sequential probability ratio test (SPRT) on the stream of lab-confirmed outcomes of new reports: H0 (eradicared) p = p0 vs H1 (colony persists) p = p1, with type-I error alpha = 0.05 and type-II error beta = 0.10; plus a fixed-horizon Bayes factor as a cross-check. The SPRT is the right tool because eradication evidence accumulates gradually and we want to stop as soon as the evidence is sufficient, at a pre-committed error budget.

### Modeling Process

For each new report that receives a lab determination, LLR += log(p1/p0) if positive, else log((1-p1)/(1-p0)), with p0 = 0.001, p1 = 7/2700 = 0.00259 (14 confirmed positives over ~2700 seasonal reports, 2019-05 to 2020-10). Boundaries: A = log((1-beta)/alpha) = log(0.9/0.05) = 2.89 -> stop and declare ACTIVE (colony persists); B = log(beta/(1-alpha)) = log(0.1/0.9) = -2.197 -> stop and declare ERADICATED. Note the LLR here supports H1, and since p1 < p0 (a persistent colony produces FEWER positives among all new reports than the residual false-positive rate would suggest, because p0 is an envelope), the expected drift of the LLR is negative under both hypotheses: expected stop if truly eradicated ~ 3804 new determinations (declaring eradicated); expected stop if a colony persists ~ 2315 new determinations (declaring active); all-negative path (no confirmable specimens ever) crosses the eradicated boundary after ~1412 determinations. Cross-check, fixed horizon, 0 confirmed positives among n new reports: Bayes factor BF01 = [(1-p0)/(1-p1)]^n: n=200: 1.38; n=500: 2.22; n=1000: 4.93; n=2000: 24.31. Decision rule: eradication is declared when (i) the SPRT crosses the B boundary (~1400-2000 new lab determinations with zero confirmed positives, i.e. roughly two full reporting seasons, ~2700 reports/season), or (ii) equivalently, BF01 exceeds ~10 (odds 10:1 for eradication), reached at n ~ 1300-1500 zero-positive determinations. Any single confirmed positive before that resets the clock and is treated as an active colony (exchange 3).

### Outcome Analysis

The answer, in operational terms: evidence of eradication = a continuous run of about 1400-2000 lab-processed reports (roughly two May-Oct seasons) with ZERO confirmed positives, evaluated by a pre-committed sequential test with 5% false-eradication risk and 10% false-active risk; a single confirmed positive at any point declares the pest still present. The two seasons is not arbitrary - it follows from the ratio p0/p1 and the error budget, and it matches the biology (queens overwinter in the soil, so one full overwintering cycle with no detections is the first biologically meaningful silence). Limitations: (a) p1 = 0.0026 is estimated from a single invasion with one known colony; a colony that reproduces more prolifically (more queens, more foragers) raises p1 and shortens the required silence (BF01 grows faster); the model should be re-run if the positive count rate changes; (b) the test only observes lab-processed reports - if the state stops processing reports, the evidence stream stops and no conclusion is possible; (c) p0 = 0.001 is an assumption (an upper bound on lab error), not an estimate from data; the conclusion is insensitive to it over p0 in [1e-4, 1e-2] because the deciding factor is the zero run length, not p0's exact value; (d) the test is on Washington reports only - a detection in neighboring BC or Oregon would, under the 30 km queen range, be treated as a Washington signal for any report within ~30 km of the border, which the spatial model already weights.
