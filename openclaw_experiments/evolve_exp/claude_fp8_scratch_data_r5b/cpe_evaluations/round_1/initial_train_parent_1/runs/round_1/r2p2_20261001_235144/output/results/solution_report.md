### Problem

Task 1. Build a Human Capital network model of ICM's 370-person organisation from the supplied data (table1.csv), stating the model and all assumptions. The goal is a graph representation of the personnel structure that later tasks (churn dynamics, budget, sustainability) can run on.

### Analysis

ICM's data is aggregate (seven position levels, headcount, salary, recruiting cost/time, training cost) not a named individual graph, so the defensible 'network' is the directed promotion/flow hierarchy implied by the seven levels, augmented with the influence mechanism the problem explicitly describes (issue 2: churn diffuses employee-to-employee). Assumptions: (A1) the organisation is a 7-node directed hierarchy ordered bottom-to-top (Administrative clerk -> Inexperienced employee -> Experienced employee -> Inexperienced supervisor -> Experienced supervisor -> Junior manager -> Senior manager/Executive), capacities equal to the supplied headcounts (30,150,110,25,25,20,10 = 370); (A2) edges are (i) the upward promotion ladder level i -> i+1, (ii) an external-inflow edge into each level, and (iii) a churn-exodus edge from each level to a sink; (A3) each node carries an influence degree (the number of peers whose departure can trigger it, per issue 2) so the structural layer can host the churn-diffusion dynamic of Task 2; (A4) sigma is the company median salary and all monetary quantities are reported in sigma as the problem requires. Data cleaning: the CSV's currency symbol was corrupted (bytes 0xA6 0xD2, a mojibake of the sigma glyph); it was repaired to sigma, and the one missing value (experienced-employee salary) was set to 1.0 sigma, the median, since the company median income is defined as sigma and that level is the modal, mid-salary tier. A 7-node graph with 6 ladder edges, 7 exodus edges and 7 inflow edges is the minimal representation that is both faithful to the data and sufficient to run the dynamics.

### Modeling Process

Let the levels i = 0..6 bottom-to-top with capacity C_i and filled headcount N_i(t), sum C_i = 370. The human-capital network G = (V, E) has V = {level nodes}, E = E_ladder (i -> i+1, promotion), E_in (external source -> i), E_out (i -> sink, churn). Influence degree d_i (peers one churn signal can reach): d = {clerk:6, in-exp emp:8, exp emp:8, in-exp sup:7, exp sup:7, jr mgr:6, sr mgr:4}. Parameters from the dataset (table1.csv), all in sigma unless noted: C = {30,150,110,25,25,20,10}; salary s_i = {0.9,0.9,1.0,1.5,2.0,4.0,8.0}; recruiting cost c_i = {0.3,0.1,0.3,0.6,0.6,0.7,1.2}; recruiting time t_i months = {2,1,3,4,5,6,7}; training cost tr_i = {0.05,0.30,0.10,0.30,0.20,0.60,0.50}. The median (sigma) salary of the whole company is 1.0 by definition of sigma. This graph plus the influence degrees is the substrate on which the churn-flow dynamics of Task 2 are integrated.

### Outcome Analysis

The resulting object is a 7-node, 20-edge directed multigraph (6 promotion, 7 exodus, 7 inflow) with per-node capacity, salary, cost and influence-degree attributes. It is sound because it is the coarsest graph consistent with the supplied aggregate data while still capable of expressing the two dynamics the problem asks for (promotion/backfill and churn-diffusion). Limitations: it is a level-averaged model, so within-level heterogeneity (which specific individuals are connected) is represented only by the scalar influence degree d_i; the missing experienced-employee salary was set to the median (1.0 sigma), which slightly understates that tier if its true salary is above the median. The graph is static in structure; only the node populations N_i(t) evolve in Task 2.

## Subtask 2: Task 2. Identify and model the dynamic processes in the Human Capital network: (1) the dynamics of organisational churn 

### Problem

Task 2. Identify and model the dynamic processes in the Human Capital network: (1) the dynamics of organisational churn (influence, dissatisfaction) and (2) the direct and indirect effects of churn on the organisation's productivity. State the model and all assumptions.

### Analysis

Churn is modelled as a continuous-time compartment flow on the Task-1 graph. The key dynamic is that churn is endogenous: the problem (issue 2) states churn diffuses from employee to employee, and expert exchange 1 confirmed the rate is self-reinforcing but saturates after the first wave or two and is concentrated in the middle-manager/supervisor tiers. Productivity loss is modelled per expert exchange 3 as a vacancy loss (unfilled seats produce nothing) compounded by a ramp-up loss (a just-refilled seat reaches full output only after 6-12 months, front-loaded and partially recovering), with the dip affecting the whole team. The non-linear, saturating form of both the churn cascade and the productivity loss is consistent with the empirical literature on turnover and labour productivity (see parameter table).

### Modeling Process

State: N_i(t) filled headcount at level i. (1) Churn rate. Base churn r_i = r_base for non-mid levels and 2*r_base for the three middle tiers (issue 4: mid-level turnover runs twice the average), with r_base = 0.18 (issue 7: current company churn 18%/yr). An endogenous cascade multiplies it: r_i(t) = r_i * (1 + CASCADE * w_i * S_i(t)), where S_i(t) = D_i/(D_i + k) is a saturating function of D_i = cumulative departure fraction at level i (D_i/C_i), k = 1/CASCADE_SAT, and w_i = 1.0 for mid tiers, 0.5 otherwise (cascade concentrated in the middle). The 1/(1+.) saturation is what makes the cascade strengthen then plateau (exchange 1). Departures this step: dN_i^out = N_i * r_i(t) * dt. (2) Backfill. Each departure is replaced by (a) internal promotion, fraction P_PROMO, drawn from a qualified fraction Q of the level below (issue 6: the multi-year tenure rule makes promotion slow and blocked, so P_PROMO is small), and (b) external hiring, the remainder (1 - P_PROMO), entering a time-lagged pipeline that lands after the level's median recruiting time t_i. External hiring is a FIXED annual throughput HR_SHARE * 370 (exchange 2: it does not scale with the vacancy spike); whatever it cannot absorb that step stays a vacancy and accumulates. (3) Productivity. A seat contributes to output only once ramped. ramp_months(i) = t_i + 6 (9 for mid/senior) implements exchange 3's 6-12-month ramp. Productivity P(t) = (sum_i min(N_i, cap_i) with ramping seats at half credit) / 370. Lost output (indirect cost) is the time-integral of (1 - P(t)) times the salary-weighted seat value. Parameter table (name = value, interval [a,b], source): r_base = 0.18, [0.15,0.20], source: problem issue 7 (CEO reports 18%/yr); mid-tier multiplier = 2.0, [2,2], source: problem issue 4 (mid-level turnover twice the average); CASCADE = 0.30, [0.15,0.6], source: expert exchange 1 (self-reinforcing, saturating, mid-concentrated); HR_SHARE (external throughput) = 0.18, [0.09,0.25], source: expert exchange 2 (near-fixed, non-scaling pace) calibrated to the observed ~85% operating point; P_PROMO = 0.15, [0.10,0.30], source: expert exchange 2 + problem issue 6 (promotion blocked, external-heavy backfill); Q (qualified fraction) = 0.6, [0.5,0.7], source: modelling assumption; ramp onboarding = 6-9 months, [6,12], source: expert exchange 3 (6-12 months to full productivity for experienced/mid-level roles); non-linear turnover-productivity relationship, source: DOI 10.1080/09585192.2018.1449129 (turnover and its volatility have a flexible non-linear effect on labour productivity). Solution procedure: forward Euler integration with dt = 0.05 yr over the 2-yr horizon, departing -> promoting -> external-pipeline -> landing -> training-cost -> productivity order each step.

### Outcome Analysis

At the 18% baseline the model holds the company near its observed 80-85% operating point over 2 years (fill 78.5%, productivity 73.5%), validating the calibration. The dynamics reproduce the three qualitative findings the problem flags: (i) churn is endogenous and self-reinforcing (raising CASCADE raises 2-yr departures 131->145) but saturates rather than exploding, exactly as exchange 1 and the non-linear-turnover literature describe; (ii) the middle tiers churn at 2x and are where the cascade bites hardest, matching issue 4; (iii) productivity loss is an indirect, lagged cost (192 sigma-equivalent over 2 yr at baseline) that exceeds the raw vacancy value because a replacement is not fully productive for 6-12 months and the team absorbs the loss (exchange 3). Limitations and biases: the cascade is a scalar saturation, not an individual-level epidemic, so it cannot capture heterophily or the actual tie structure (only d_i); the ramp discount is a fixed 6-9 months rather than a measured per-role curve; and the model is a mean-field over levels, so it will not show which named individuals churn. These are conservative in the sense that they understate the peak of a true cascade but correctly capture its saturation and its mid-tier concentration.

### Problem

Task 3. Use the model to analyse ICM's budget requirements for talent management, in sigma, for both recruiting and training over the next 2 years.

### Analysis

Talent-management budget has two components. Recruiting cost is incurred each time a seat is refilled externally, at the level-specific median recruiting cost c_i (training cost for a promoted employee is negligible relative to external recruiting and is folded into the training line). Training cost is incurred continuously, at the level-specific annual training rate tr_i for every incumbent. Both are summed over the 2-year horizon as the model evolves N_i(t) and the external-refill count. Because all quantities are already in sigma, the budget is naturally sigma-denominated, matching the decision-makers' stated preference for sigma-based reporting.

### Modeling Process

Recruiting budget over 2 yr: B_rec = sum over external fills (landed recruits x c_i). Training budget over 2 yr: B_train = integral_0^2 sum_i N_i(t) * tr_i * dt. Total talent-management budget B = B_rec + B_train. (These are the model's rec_cost and train_cost outputs.) Baseline 18% churn over 2 years: B_rec = 38.4 sigma (111 external recruits), B_train = 145.0 sigma (averaging ~310 incumbents x weighted training rate), B_total = 183.4 sigma. Annualised: recruiting ~19.2 sigma/yr, training ~72.5 sigma/yr. Training dominates (~80% of the budget) because the training rate is paid on the whole incumbent population continuously, whereas recruiting is paid only on the flow of external fills.

### Outcome Analysis

Over the next 2 years at the current 18% churn ICM needs roughly 183 sigma total for talent management: about 38 sigma of recruiting and about 145 sigma of training. The budget is training-heavy because the 370-person incumbent base is trained every year (0.05-0.60 sigma per person) while recruiting is paid only on the ~55 external fills per year. Sensitivity: if churn rises to 25% the recruiting line rises (46.7 sigma over 2 yr) and, more importantly, the indirect productivity cost rises, while at 35% recruiting cost plateaus (48.4 sigma) because external hiring hits its fixed ceiling - the extra churn becomes vacancies, not extra recruiting spend. A practical implication for the HR manager: the controllable lever is not recruiting spend (it is capacity-capped) but retention, which protects both the training investment already sunk in incumbents and the productivity they generate. Limitation: recruiting cost is charged on external fills only; the true cost of a churn event (lost training investment, the ramp productivity gap) is booked in the productivity line of Task 2, not here, so this 183 sigma figure is the direct cash budget, not the full economic cost of churn.

### Problem

Task 4. Can ICM sustain its 80% full status if the annual churn rate for all positions goes to 25%? To 35%? What are the costs of these higher turnover rates, and what are their indirect effects?

### Analysis

Sustainability of a fill fraction is a steady-state question: at a given churn rate, what is the highest fill fraction the backfill machinery can hold? Because external hiring is a fixed, non-scaling throughput (exchange 2) and internal promotion is source-limited (issue 6), there is a maximum sustainably-holdable fill. I compute this fixed point by finding the fill fraction f* at which the external-hiring demand (the non-promoted share of departures) just saturates the fixed external ceiling; above f* the demand exceeds the ceiling and vacancies accumulate irreversibly. I then read off whether 80% is below or above f* at 25% and 35% churn, and I quantify the costs (direct recruiting/training) and indirect effects (productivity loss, mid-tier collapse, cascade) at each rate using the transient model.

### Modeling Process

Fixed point: f* = max fill such that external_demand(f*) <= HR_SHARE * 370, where external_demand(f) = (1 - P_PROMO) * sum_i (f * C_i) * r_i(f). Results: at 18% churn f* = 98.7% (80% easily sustained); at 25% churn f* = 68.0% (80% NOT sustainable - the ceiling holds at most ~68%); at 35% churn f* = 47.6% (80% far below the ceiling). Transient 2-yr fill: 18% -> 78.5%, 25% -> 72.0%, 35% -> 58.3%. Costs over 2 yr: at 25% churn recruiting 46.7 sigma + training 138.1 sigma = 184.8 sigma direct, plus 217 sigma indirect productivity loss; at 35% churn recruiting 48.4 sigma + training 123.7 sigma = 172.1 sigma direct (recruiting plateaus at the external ceiling), plus 246 sigma indirect productivity loss. Indirect effects of high churn: (i) the endogenous cascade deepens (mid-tier departures rise, saturating); (ii) the middle-manager/supervisor tiers collapse first (ESup and InexSup fall to ~40-50% of capacity by 2 yr at 35%), exactly the tiers issue 4 flags as critical and stuck; (iii) productivity drops to 66% (25%) and 53% (35%) of fully-staffed output because replacements ramp for 6-12 months.

### Outcome Analysis

ICM can sustain 80% at the current 18% churn but NOT at 25% (max holdable ~68%) and decisively NOT at 35% (max holdable ~48%). The paradox that higher churn costs LESS in direct recruiting (48.4 vs 46.7 sigma) is because external hiring is capacity-capped: above the ceiling, extra departures become unfilled vacancies rather than billable recruits, so the true cost shifts from the budget line to the productivity line and to the structural hollowing of the middle tiers. The indirect effects dominate: at 35% churn the company loses ~246 sigma of output over 2 years (more than the entire direct talent budget) and its critical middle-management layer is halved, degrading coordination, trust and the promotion pipeline itself - a self-reinforcing deterioration (the cascade of Task 2). This is the model's central warning: beyond roughly 25% churn, ICM is not merely under-staffed, it is structurally degrading, and no amount of hiring spend (which is capped) reverses it - only retention or a structural fix to the promotion bottleneck (issue 6) can. Limitations: the fixed point assumes the external ceiling is truly inelastic over the 2-yr window; if HR could genuinely expand capacity (a budget shock), f* would rise. The 80% figure is interpreted as the average fill the company wants to hold, not a per-level target.

### Problem

Task 5. Simulate the impact of 30% churn in both junior managers and experienced supervisors (all other levels at 18%) over the next two years under (1) no external recruiting and (2) promoting only qualified employees. Explain the impact on the HR health of the organisation.

### Analysis

This is a targeted shock to exactly the two middle tiers the problem (issue 4) identifies as critical, stuck, and high-turnover, compounded by two restrictive backfill policies: (1) no external recruiting at all (HR_SHARE = 0), so the only inflow is internal promotion; (2) promotion restricted to qualified employees (qualified fraction Q = 0.5, the stricter reading). The question is what happens to HR health when the two linchpin tiers bleed at 30% and the organisation cannot hire externally to replace them. I run the model with per-level churn overrides (junior manager and experienced supervisor at 30%, all others at 18%), HR_SHARE = 0, and the qualified-only promotion rule, over 2 years.

### Modeling Process

Overrides: per_churn = {junior manager (level 5): 0.30, experienced supervisor (level 4): 0.30}, all other levels at base 0.18 (mid-tier 2x applied). No external recruiting: HR_SHARE = 0, so the external pipeline is disabled and the only backfill is internal promotion of a qualified fraction Q = 0.5 of the level below, drawn from the (already thinning) source pools. The endogenous cascade (CASCADE = 0.30) is active, so the 30% shock in the two tiers also raises churn in their connected peers. 2-yr result: overall fill falls to ~52% (productivity ~51%), with the two shocked tiers collapsing - experienced supervisor to ~20% of capacity (4.9/25) and, through the promotion drain, the in-experienced supervisor tier to ~30% (7.6/25), junior manager to ~43% (8.6/20). Because external recruiting is zero, recruiting spend is 0 sigma and the training budget is 120 sigma, but the indirect productivity loss is ~230 sigma and the bottom tiers starve (clerks -> ~0) because they can only be refilled by external hiring, which is forbidden.

### Outcome Analysis

HR health deteriorates severely. The two linchpin middle tiers are hollowed out (experienced supervisor to ~20% of capacity), which has three compounding consequences. First, a managerial vacuum: with experienced supervisors at 20% and junior managers at 43%, the span-of-control collapses and the remaining managers absorb an unsustainable load, which (per the cascade mechanism) raises their own churn risk - the problem's issue 4 'stuck middle managers' becomes a self-fulfilling exit spiral. Second, a pipeline rupture: because only qualified employees are promoted and the source pools are thin, even the qualified-only rule cannot generate enough replacements, so the vacancies persist and compound. Third, a whole-organisation drag: the bottom tiers, which cannot be refilled without external hiring, empty out (clerks toward zero), so the indirect productivity loss (~230 sigma over 2 yr) far exceeds the direct budget (120 sigma training, 0 recruiting). The practical message to the supervisor: a 30% churn shock to the two middle tiers, met with no external recruiting, is not survivable within two years - the organisation loses its management backbone and its entry-level pipeline simultaneously. The single most effective mitigations are (a) restoring external recruiting for the entry and middle tiers, and (b) attacking the promotion bottleneck (issue 6) so qualified middle-tier replacements can flow without depleting the source. Limitations: the 30% is applied as a constant annual rate for 2 years; in reality a sustained 30% would likely accelerate the cascade further (the model's saturation dampens this), and the zero-clerk result is an artifact of forbidding all external hiring at the entry level rather than a prediction that clerks literally vanish.

### Problem

Task 6. Summarise the potential use of team science and multi-layered networks in fulfilling the HR manager's vision of connecting the Human Capital network to other organisational network layers (information flow, trust, influence, friendship), with the HR office taking the lead.

### Analysis

This is a conceptual/integrative task. The supplied model (Tasks 1-5) is a single structural layer of a multi-layer network in the sense of Kivelä et al. (2013): a set of shared nodes (the 370 employees) on which multiple distinct relationship types (edges) are defined simultaneously. 'Team science' (Salas 2008; Stokols 2008) supplies the organisational-psychology content for what those layers should measure and how they jointly determine team performance. The HR office's vision is that the HR/structural layer is the anchor and the other offices' layers (information, trust, influence, friendship) are added as further layers on the same node set, and that HR leads the integration.

### Modeling Process

Multi-layer representation (Kivelä et al. 2013): the organisational network is a set of layers alpha = {structural/HR, information-flow, trust, influence, friendship}, all sharing the same node set V of 370 employees, each layer carrying its own adjacency A^alpha. The HR/structural layer A^struct already exists from Task 1 (promotion ladder + reporting). The other layers are defined on the same V by their respective offices. Integration tools from the multilayer literature that map directly onto the HR vision: (i) layer-wise centrality and betweenness to find employees who are pivotal in the structural layer AND bridge nodes in the information/influence layers - these are the true 'churn-critical' people (Task 2's cascade runs along the influence layer, so a high structural centrality combined with high influence-layer betweenness flags who to retain); (ii) layer cross-coupling / multiplexity metrics (e.g. the correlation of an edge's presence across layers) to measure whether strong ties are also trusted and information-rich - the Kivelä 'edge multiplicity' notion - which identifies the cohesive core teams whose dissolution is most damaging; (iii) cross-layer contagion, modelling churn as a process on the structural layer that propagates through the influence/friendship layers - exactly issue 2, now with a defined substrate. Team science (Salas 2008) contributes the team-level variables (coordination, shared mental models, adaptability, trust) that the trust and friendship layers operationalise, and the result that team performance is a function of these relational properties rather than the sum of individual performance - the empirical basis for why the HR office should treat the network, not the headcount, as the unit of management. The HR office leads because (a) it owns the structural layer and the churn dynamic (the problem it is charged to solve), (b) churn is the cross-layer process that ties all layers together, and (c) it is the office with the incentive and the data to act on the retention signal. A concrete workflow: HR publishes the structural+churn layer; the other offices map their layers onto the same node IDs; a shared node-ID registry (the HR org chart) makes the layers joinable; HR then runs the cross-layer retention-risk score (Task 2's churn probability re-weighted by cross-layer betweenness) and feeds it back to all offices.

### Outcome Analysis

The team-science + multilayer framing turns the HR manager's vision from a slogan into a concrete, buildable architecture: one shared node set, multiple relationship layers, and cross-layer metrics that produce a single retention-risk and team-health score. The highest-value applications are (1) churn-criticality scoring (who to retain, using influence-layer betweenness to extend Task 2's cascade beyond the structural ties); (2) team-vulnerability mapping (which cohesive sub-teams - high edge multiplicity across trust+friendship+influence - would be most damaging if they churned); and (3) early-warning (a rising influence-layer churn contagion is detectable before it shows in headcount). The HR office's leadership role is natural because it owns the anchor layer and the cross-layer process (churn) that motivates the integration. Limitations: multilayer models are data-hungry (each layer needs its own edge data, which the other offices have not yet collected), the cross-layer metrics assume a common, stable node-ID registry (the HR org chart must be maintained as the join key), and the team-science variables (shared mental models, adaptability) are hard to measure at the 370-node scale without surveys - so the vision is a multi-year build, with the HR structural layer (already done in Tasks 1-5) as the foundation on which the other layers are added incrementally.
