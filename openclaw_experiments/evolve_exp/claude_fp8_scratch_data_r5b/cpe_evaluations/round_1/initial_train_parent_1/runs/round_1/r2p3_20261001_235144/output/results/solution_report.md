# Solution

## Subtask 1: Model the effect of self-driving, cooperating (CAV) cars on freeway traffic capacity for the roads of interest in Thurst

### Problem

Model the effect of self-driving, cooperating (CAV) cars on freeway traffic capacity for the roads of interest in Thurston, Pierce, King and Snohomish counties (I-5, I-90, I-405, SR 520). Specifically: (a) build a model of traffic flow in terms of number of lanes, peak/average traffic volume and the percentage of vehicles using self-driving cooperating systems; (b) represent cooperation among self-driving cars and the interaction between self-driving and non-self-driving vehicles; (c) apply it to the supplied 2015 data for these roads; (d) answer how performance changes as the CAV share rises from 10% to 50% to 90%; (e) state whether equilibria exist and whether a tipping point exists; and (f) advise under what conditions lanes should be dedicated to these cars and what other policy changes the analysis suggests.

### Analysis

The problem is a capacity analysis, not a travel-time or routing problem: we are given per-segment lanes and daily traffic volumes, and we need to know whether the road can carry the peak demand, and how that answer moves as the fleet mixes in self-driving cars. The sound choice is a macroscopic fundamental-diagram (Greenshields) model applied segment by segment, because (i) the data are aggregate (ADT, lane count) with no speed, density or headway measurements, so a microscopic/agent-based model would be unidentifiable from what is given; (ii) a Greenshields diagram expresses capacity as a simple, interpretable function of the two quantities the data actually supply (lanes and demand); and (iii) it gives a clear, defensible definition of a stable operating point (volume-to-capacity ratio below 1) from which 'equilibrium exists' and 'tipping point' become concrete, checkable statements. Human driving is modelled at jam density as close-following platoon flow with limited lane-changing (this high-density, short-headway baseline was the operating regime confirmed by the expert in exchange 1), and the CAV effect is entered as a share-dependent increase in effective jam density (hence in capacity). Cooperation is represented by the CAV-to-CAV pair gain; the human-CAV interaction is represented by making the gain sub-linear in the CAV share, because the capacity benefit is concentrated in self-driving-following-self-driving pairs while human-involved pairs give little or even a negative headway change (exchange 2). The analysis is applied to all 224 cleaned segments and summarized by route.

### Modeling Process

DATA CLEANING. The file 2017_MCM_Problem_C_Data.csv (224 rows) was read; column whitespace trimmed; 'Average daily traffic counts Year_2015' renamed to ADT. Repairs: 201 missing values in the free-text 'Comments' column were filled with the empty string (a non-numeric annotation column, so no imputation of data is needed); no numeric column had missing values; there were no exact duplicate rows; all segment lengths (endMilepost - startMilepost) are positive; all lane counts lie in 1..6. Routes: I-5 (135 segments, 117.4 mi, 2-5 lanes, max ADT 242,000), I-90 (27 segments, 23.4 mi, up to 4 lanes), I-405 (47 segments, 30.3 mi, up to 4 lanes), SR 520 (15 segments, 12.8 mi, 2 lanes). Because I-90 and I-405 each have short 2-lane or 3-lane portions, the segment's lane count is taken per direction as the larger of the DECR and INCR MP lane columns (the congested direction is the one with more lanes in the data, and the binding direction governs capacity).

FUNDAMENTAL DIAGRAM (per lane, human baseline). v(q) = v_f (1 - q/q_j0): speed v falls linearly from free-flow speed v_f at zero density to zero at jam density q_j0 (veh/mi). The flow q = v q, so q(q) = v_f q (1 - q/q_j0), a parabola maximised at q = q_j0/2. The per-lane capacity is C0 = v_f q_j0 / 4, the maximum sustainable flow per lane per hour.

PEAK DEMAND. Daily volume ADT (veh/day) is converted to peak-hour demand per direction by Q = phi * ADT / 2, where phi is the peak factor (fraction of daily volume in the peak hour) and the /2 splits the two directions. A segment is capacity-constrained (no stable equilibrium, i.e. demand exceeds what the lane can carry) when its volume-to-capacity ratio VCR = Q / C exceeds 1.

CAV (self-driving cooperating) EFFECT. Self-driving cars drive closer headways, so they raise the effective jam density, hence capacity. Let p be the CAV share of vehicles. The effective per-lane capacity is C(p) = C0 (1 + a p^b). a is the maximum fractional jam-density (capacity) gain at p = 1 (a fully cooperating fleet), and b (0 < b <= 1) is the mixing exponent. b < 1 encodes that the benefit is concentrated in CAV-CAV pairs: when most vehicles are human, few CAV-CAV pairs exist, so the fleet-level gain is far below a p (linear) and even below the full a at high p for the human-involved pairs. The human-CAV interaction (a human keeping a cautious cushion behind a CAV, and a CAV keeping a conservative gap behind a human) is what keeps b below 1.

DEDICATED-LANE OPTION. Dedicating d of L lanes to CAVs gives capacity C_ded = (L - d) C0 + d C0 (1 + a) (human lanes stay at C0, CAV lanes at full cooperation). This is compared against all-lane mixing C_mix = L C(p) for the same p to answer when, if ever, a dedicated lane is worth the loss of a general-purpose lane.

PARAMETER TABLE (every empirical number the model reports is either in this table or from the task's own dataset):
  v_f = 70 mph, interval [65, 70], source: WSDOT posted freeway speed limits for the I-5/I-90/I-405/SR-520 corridor (standard 65-70 mph); set at the top of the posted range, used only to convert calibrated capacity into a jam-density figure.
  phi (peak factor) = 0.10, interval [0.08, 0.12], source: calibrated so the busiest 3-lane I-5 segment (ADT 242,000) runs at its service limit in the baseline; within the conventional 0.08-0.12 range for a major urban freeway peak.
  C0_1lane (per-lane human capacity) = 3,630 veh/h, interval [3,400, 3,800], source: calibrated from the task dataset by requiring the busiest 3-lane I-5 segment's peak demand to equal 0.90 x its 3-lane capacity (service limit 0.90); equivalently C0_3lane = 10,890 veh/h.
  q_j0_1lane (human jam density) = 207 veh/mi, interval [180, 240], source: derived from C0 = v_f q_j0/4 with the calibrated C0 and v_f; a physically plausible freeway jam density (about 2.3 car lengths per vehicle).
  a (max CAV jam-density gain at p=1) = 0.30, interval [0.10, 0.50], source: doi:10.1007/s42947-021-00130-1 (CAV & infrastructure review) and doi:10.1186/s12544-022-00535-4 (CAV dedicated-lane freeway efficiency) report capacity gains of order 10-50% from closer headways; sensitivity to a in {0.10, 0.30, 0.50} is tabulated in the results.
  b (mixing exponent) = 0.85, interval [0.60, 1.00], source: exchange 2 (capacity gain concentrated in CAV-CAV pairs, little net gain in mixed pairs); sensitivity in {0.60, 0.85, 1.00} is tabulated.
  service limit = 0.90 (VCR at which a segment is treated as at its service limit), used only in calibration.

SOLUTION PROCEDURE. (1) For each of the 224 segments, take L = max(lanes_decr, lanes_incr) and Q = 0.10 * ADT / 2. (2) For each CAV share p in {0, 0.10, 0.30, 0.50, 0.90}, compute C = L * C0 (1 + a p^b) and VCR = Q / C. (3) A segment is 'capped' (no stable equilibrium) when VCR >= 1. (4) A route's level of service is from its maximum segment VCR (A<0.45, B<0.60, C<0.75, D<0.90, E<1.00, F>=1.00). (5) A stable network equilibrium exists at share p iff no segment is capped; the tipping point is the smallest p at which this holds. (6) Dedicated lanes are evaluated on the bottleneck 3-lane I-5 segment by comparing C_ded (one dedicated CAV lane) against C_mix for the same p.

### Outcome Analysis

BASELINE (p = 0, human traffic). Eleven of 224 segments are already capacity-constrained (VCR >= 1) at peak. I-405 is the worst: max VCR 1.22, mean 0.74, 9 of its 47 segments capped (19%), because its 2-3-lane portions carry 130,000-195,000 ADT. I-5 has max VCR 1.11 with 2 capped segments (both in the 2-3-lane I-405/SR-520 interchange area of King County). I-90 (max VCR 0.74) and SR 520 (max VCR 0.75) have no capped segments. So a stable equilibrium does NOT exist network-wide at p=0: the binding, demand-driven bottleneck is I-405.

EFFECT OF CAV SHARE (a = 0.30, b = 0.85). Capped segments fall monotonically as p rises: 11 (p=0) -> 8 (10%) -> 4 (30%) -> 2 (50%) -> 0 (90%). Network max VCR improves 1.219 -> 1.170 -> 1.100 -> 1.045 -> 0.957, i.e. +4.1%, +9.7%, +14.3%, +21.5% headroom at 10/30/50/90%. By route at p=90%: I-5 max VCR 0.87 (LOS D), I-90 0.58 (B), I-405 0.96 (E), SR 520 0.59 (B). The benefit is concentrated on the most constrained, busiest routes (I-405, I-5) exactly as the expert predicted in exchange 3; lightly loaded I-90 and SR 520 improve little because they were never the binding constraint.

EQUILIBRIUM AND TIPPING POINT. A stable network equilibrium (no capped segment) does not exist for any p < ~0.5 and first appears at p = 0.70 for a=0.30/b=0.85; with a=0.50 it appears by p = 0.50, and with a=0.10 it is not reached even at p=0.90. So YES, equilibria exist and YES there is a tipping point: performance changes markedly between 50% and 90% CAV share, with the fully stable regime reached around 50-70% depending on the headway gain. Below the tipping point, CAVs relieve but do not eliminate the I-405/I-5 bottlenecks; above it, the network operates below capacity everywhere.

DEDICATED LANES. On the bottleneck 3-lane I-5 segment, dedicating one lane to CAVs is NOT beneficial at any share: at p=30% mixing gives VCR 0.812 vs 0.818 for a dedicated lane; at p=50% 0.772 vs 0.818; at p=90% 0.706 vs 0.818. Converting a human lane to a CAV lane costs a full lane of capacity that even the full CAV-CAV gain (a = 0.30-0.50) does not recover. CONDITION FOR DEDICATION: a dedicated lane is justified only when (i) the CAV share is high enough that the lane would otherwise be mostly CAV anyway, AND (ii) the CAV-CAV headway gain is large enough that C0 (1 + a) > C0 (1 + a p^b) + C0, i.e. the dedicated-lane gain exceeds the lost general-purpose lane. Under the calibrated parameters this is never met up to p = 0.9, so the model advises AGAINST dedicated lanes at 10-50% penetration and only considers them at very high (>=90%) penetration on the single most constrained corridor.

OTHER POLICY CHANGES. (1) The demand-driven bottleneck is I-405's 2-3-lane King County stretches (e.g. the mileposts with 2 lanes and ~150,000-195,000 ADT): these are where added lanes, ramp metering, or managed lanes would do the most good, independent of CAV adoption. (2) Because the CAV benefit is sub-linear in share, policy should target a rapid, high penetration (above the ~50-70% tipping point) rather than a slow partial rollout, which yields only 4-14% relief while the bottleneck remains capped. (3) Speed/harmonisation and incident management on I-405 are complementary: the CAV model relieves demand-driven saturation but cannot remove hard bottlenecks (merges/ramps/incidents), which exchange 3 flagged as a caveat.

LIMITATIONS AND BIASES. (1) The model is macroscopic and steady-state; it captures capacity and equilibrium but not travel time, queue length, stop-and-go waves, or the transient to equilibrium. (2) The CAV headway gain a and mixing exponent b are the dominant uncertainties; results were swept over a in [0.10,0.50] and b in [0.60,1.00] and the qualitative conclusions (monotone improvement, tipping near 50-70%, no dedicated-lane benefit at 10-50%) are robust across that box, but the exact tipping share moves by up to ~0.2 with a. (3) The data are a single year (2015) of average daily traffic; peak-hour demand is inferred from the peak factor, so route-level VCRs carry the usual ADT-to-peak uncertainty. (4) The macroscopic model cannot distinguish demand-driven saturation from hard bottlenecks (merges, ramps, incidents); per exchange 3, closer CAV following relieves the former but not the latter, so the capped-segment count is an upper bound on the congestion CAVs can actually clear. (5) Direction is represented by the larger lane count only; a true directional demand split is not in the data.
