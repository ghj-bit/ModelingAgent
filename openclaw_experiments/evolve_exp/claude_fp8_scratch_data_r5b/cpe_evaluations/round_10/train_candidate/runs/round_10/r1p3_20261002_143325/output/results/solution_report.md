### Problem

Subtask 1. The daily 'number of reported results' varies a lot (from ~2,500 to ~362,000 in the year). Build a model explaining this variation and produce a prediction interval for the number of reported results on March 1, 2023. A related question is whether word attributes affect the share of scores played in Hard Mode, and if so how.

### Analysis

Data cleaning: the sheet (359 rows, 2022-01-07 to 2022-12-31, contest 202-560) had no missing values or duplicates; one column header had a double space ('Number of  reported results') which was collapsed, dates parsed to datetime and sorted ascending, and four words with abnormal length (clen, tash, favor, rprobe) were kept and treated as the words actually played. A key operational assumption, confirmed with the domain expert, is that 'reported results' counts only players who posted their score on Twitter that day, not the total player base; so the quantity modelled is posting/reporting behaviour, and total daily play is an unobserved, much larger population. The expert also identified the dominant driver of posting volume as the game's day-to-day 'buzz' (viral waves, media coverage, holidays/weekends, the early-2022 popularity peak), with word difficulty contributing only a small single-day increment; a word term is therefore omitted from the count model (it has no resolvable signal in 359 days). Model form: because the count is positive and the year is dominated by a large decay from the viral peak, I model the log count additively with a slow time trend plus weekday fixed effects. A full-year log-linear trend fits the whole series (R^2 = 0.883) and captures the decay, but the decay is driven by the early-year peak and would under-predict a stable spring 2023 regime; so the point estimate for March 1, 2023 is instead anchored to the stable late-2022 posting regime (last 100 days, 22 Nov - 31 Dec 2022, R^2 = 0.303 for weekday + within-window trend), which is the regime a March 2023 date would sit in. Uncertainty is the stable-window residual standard deviation (sigma = 0.226 in log units) plus an extrapolation allowance for the 60-day gap between the data end and the target date.

### Modeling Process

Variables: N_d = reported results on day d; y_d = ln N_d; t_d = days since the start of the stable window (Nov 22, 2022); 1[dow=d] weekday dummies (Sunday = baseline). Model: y_d = a + sum_{dow=1..6} b_dow * 1[dow=d] + c * t_d + e_d, e_d ~ N(0, sigma^2), fitted by least squares on the stable window (n = 100). Fitted: a = 10.414, c = -0.0048/day (a very slow within-window drift), sigma = 0.226, weekday effects in log units: Mon +0.013, Tue -0.156, Wed -0.009, Thu +0.001, Fri -0.028, Sat -0.036. For the target day (Wed, 2023-03-01, t = 160): mu = a + b_Wed + c*160, se = sqrt(sigma^2 + (0.10 * 60/365)^2) = 0.226, giving N_hat = exp(mu) = 13280 and a 95% prediction interval [exp(mu - 1.96 se), exp(mu + 1.96 se)] = [8520, 20699]. Context model (full year, n = 359): ln N = 12.426 + weekday dummies - 2.874 * (years since Jan 7, 2022), R^2 = 0.883, i.e. a very large decay from the viral peak. Robustness test (from the expert's decision rule): compare the 95% PI width to the median absolute day-to-day change in the stable window: width = 20699 - 8520 = 12179 vs median|dN| = 1451; since width >> day-to-day variation and the target is 60 days beyond the data, the flag is 'low confidence / noise-sensitive'. Hard-Mode part: let H_d = (Number in hard mode)/(Number of reported results) (share, mean 7.76%, sd 5.05%). Regress H_d on word features (vowel density, distinct letters, starts-with-vowel, double letters, consonant density) plus the day's expected-tries difficulty score. Result: R^2 = 0.004 (essentially none of the word variation is explained); individual t-statistics: vowel density t = 2.73, consonant density t = 3.65, others |t| < 0.9, and expected tries t = 0.08. A time-trend on H_d alone has R^2 = 0.223; corr(H_d, N_d) = -0.435, corr(H_d, expected tries) = 0.008. Parameter table (all empirical inputs): N_stable_window = 100 days [22 Nov - 31 Dec 2022], source: dataset + expert reply E2 (stable regime); sigma = 0.226, source: residual of the fit to the dataset; extrapolation allowance = 0.10/yr over 60 days, source: expert reply E3 (uncertainty grows with distance outside the window); weekday effects, source: fit to the dataset. The only externally-sourced inputs are the three expert constraints (E1 posting-only definition, E2 buzz-dominated driver, E3 robustness threshold); no web-retrieved numeric parameter was needed.

### Outcome Analysis

The count model explains the large within-regime variation (weekday) and, over the full year, the dominant decay (R^2 = 0.883). For March 1, 2023 the model gives a point estimate of about 13,300 reported results with a 95% prediction interval of about [8,500, 20,700]. The expert's robustness rule flags this as low-confidence: the interval is far wider than the typical day-to-day swing and the date lies 60 days past the observed window and past the popularity peak, so the point estimate is only weakly informative and the interval is the operative quantity; the main risks are a further slow decay of the posting base or a new external event (media coverage, a holiday) that the model cannot anticipate. On Hard Mode: word attributes do not meaningfully affect the hard-mode share (R^2 = 0.004; expected tries t = 0.08). The apparent weak effects of vowel/consonant density (t up to 3.65 on 359 days) are not a defensible word-difficulty mechanism because the overall word-attribute signal is near zero; the share is instead driven by the composition of the playing/posting population over time (a time trend, R^2 = 0.223) and by player-base size (corr with the count = -0.435, reflecting selection: on small-attention days a different, perhaps more dedicated, mix of players posts). So the answer is: no, word attributes do not materially affect the hard-mode share; it reflects who is playing, not the word. Limitations: the model is of posting behaviour only (not total play); the March 2023 extrapolation is outside the data; percentages and shares are subject to Twitter sampling and rounding.

### Problem

Subtask 2. For a given future solution word on a future date, build a model predicting the distribution of reported results, i.e. the percentages of (1 try, 2 tries, ..., 6 tries, X). State the model's uncertainties and give a specific prediction for the word EERIE on March 1, 2023, with a confidence statement.

### Analysis

The outcome distribution for a day is the 7-vector of percentages. The natural predictors are word attributes (they are the word-specific signal) plus the day's regime. I use five word features: vowel density, fraction of distinct letters, starts-with-vowel, double-letter fraction, consonant density (all standardised), plus a weekend indicator. For each of the 7 outcomes I fit a linear (regularised) regression on these features; the model explains a modest but real share of the outcome variance (R^2 per outcome: 1-try 0.08, 2-try 0.16, 3-try 0.19, 4-try 0.05, 5-try 0.19, 6-try 0.13, X 0.07). Regularisation does not change the predictions materially (alpha = 1 vs 10 give near-identical profiles), so the weak signal, not the fit, is the binding uncertainty. The mean of the fitted in-sample predictions is the overall base profile; a specific word's profile is its fitted value, clipped at zero and renormalised to 100%. Uncertainty per outcome is the residual standard deviation of that outcome's regression; the 95% interval for each percentage is point +/- 1.96 * residual sd. Because these seven intervals are for marginal percentages that must sum to 100, they are a set of marginal bounds, not a joint distribution.

### Modeling Process

Let p_dj be the percentage for outcome j (j = 1..6, X) on day d (rows renormalised to sum to 100). Word feature vector w_d (5 standardised features) and weekend indicator z_d. For each j: p_dj = alpha_j + beta_j' w_d + gamma_j z_d + eps_dj, fit by ridge regression (alpha = 1). Base profile (mean over days) = [0.47, 5.84, 22.74, 32.94, 23.65, 11.56, 2.80] for (1,2,3,4,5,6,X). For EERIE (vowel density 3/5 = 0.60, distinct letters 3/5 = 0.60, starts-with-vowel 1, double letters 1/4 = 0.25, consonant density 2/5 = 0.40), treated as a midweek day: EERIE profile = [0.02, 1.97, 11.58, 31.58, 33.89, 17.47, 3.49]. Per-outcome residual standard deviations = [0.75, 3.72, 7.01, 5.21, 5.37, 5.76, 3.98] percentage points; 95% marginal intervals: 1-try [0.0, 1.5], 2-try [0.0, 9.3], 3-try [0.0, 25.3], 4-try [21.4, 41.8], 5-try [23.4, 44.4], 6-try [6.2, 28.8], X [0.0, 11.3]. Confidence: the residual RMS is about 4.5 percentage points and the R^2 per outcome is low (mostly <= 0.19), so a single day's observed profile can deviate from this prediction by several percentage points in either direction; the word-level model is a population-average profile, not a precise day-specific forecast. Parameter table: all coefficients and the base profile are computed from the dataset (fit to the 359 daily profiles); the residual standard deviations are the model's own uncertainty estimate; no externally-retrieved numeric parameter is used in this subtask.

### Outcome Analysis

For EERIE on March 1, 2023 the model predicts roughly: 1 try ~0%, 2 tries ~2%, 3 tries ~12%, 4 tries ~32%, 5 tries ~34%, 6 tries ~17%, X ~3.5%. Compared with the all-word base profile (peaked at 4 tries), EERIE is predicted to shift mass toward 5 and 6 tries and away from 3 tries, i.e. a somewhat harder word than average, consistent with EERIE's low distinct-letter count and repeated letters (only 3 unique letters among 5, two pairs of E) which give the solver less information per guess. Uncertainties: (i) word-attribute model is weak (per-outcome R^2 up to ~0.19), so the shape of the profile is only loosely determined by the letters; (ii) day-to-day/regime effects (player population, familiarity) are not fully captured and are the dominant source of the several-percentage-point residual; (iii) the seven 95% intervals are marginal and can jointly exceed 100, so they should be read as per-outcome plausibility bands, not a joint probability statement. Confidence in the EERIE prediction is therefore moderate for the overall shape (peaked at 4-5 tries) and low for exact percentages; I would expect the true realised profile to fall within the marginal bands but not to reproduce the point estimates closely.

### Problem

Subtask 3. Build and summarise a model that classifies solution words by difficulty, identify which word attributes drive each class, state how difficult EERIE is, and discuss the accuracy of the classification model.

### Analysis

I define a continuous difficulty score as the expected number of tries, X counted as 7: E_d = sum_j p_dj * j (j = 1..6, X = 7), computed from each day's renormalised outcome percentages. The dataset's words are then partitioned into three difficulty classes by the terciles of E (E_mean = 4.19; cut points 4.02 and 4.34, giving balanced classes of 118/121/120 words). Two classifiers are trained on the five word features: a multinomial logistic regression (chosen as the primary model for stable, interpretable coefficients) and a multinomial naive Bayes (a simple baseline). Accuracy is assessed by leave-one-out cross-validation to avoid overfitting inflation on the small, feature-sparse design.

### Modeling Process

Difficulty score E_d = sum_{j=1..6} p_dj * j + p_dX * 7, with p_dj the day's renormalised percentages; class labels y_d in {easy, medium, hard} by terciles of E (cut points 4.02, 4.34). Feature vector w_d (vowel density, distinct letters, starts-with-vowel, double-letter fraction, consonant density), standardised. Model 1 (primary): multinomial logistic regression, P(y = k | w) = softmax(w' beta_k + b_k), C = 1.0. Model 2: multinomial naive Bayes, alpha = 0.1. EERIE features: vowel density 0.60, distinct letters 0.60, starts-with-vowel 1, double letters 0.25, consonant density 0.40. Parameter table: all coefficients, cut points and class sizes are computed from the dataset; no externally-retrieved parameter is used. Attribute importances (mean absolute logistic coefficient) rank: distinct letters (1.82) > double letters (0.49) > starts-with-vowel (0.30) > vowel density (0.15) > consonant density (0.06).

### Outcome Analysis

EERIE's class: the logistic model assigns it to the hardest tercile with posterior probabilities [easy 0.12, medium 0.29, hard 0.58] (class 2 of 0/1/2); the naive-Bayes baseline assigns it to the middle class. The two models disagree, which is itself informative about accuracy. Accuracy: leave-one-out cross-validated accuracy is 47.9% for the logistic model and 22.0% for naive Bayes. The logistic model is therefore only modestly better than the 33.3% chance level for a balanced three-way split, and naive Bayes is worse than chance. Interpretation: with only coarse, five-dimensional letter features and 359 examples, word difficulty (as measured by realised expected tries) is only weakly separable; the dominant attribute is the number of distinct letters (more repeated letters -> harder, as in EERIE's two E-pairs), then the presence of double letters, with vowel content a weak secondary signal. The model is honest about being a rough screen rather than an accurate classifier: it recovers the broad direction (repeated-letter words like EERIE tend toward the harder end) but should not be used to rank individual words precisely. Accuracy limitations: real difficulty also depends on the word's presence in players' guess pools (a frequency/entrenchment effect not captured by letter composition), on the player population of the day, and on the fact that expected tries is an average over a large, heterogeneous population; these are why the R^2/accuracy of any letter-only model is low.

### Analysis

With the count and distribution models in place, I examined the data for structural, temporal and cross-sectional patterns: the reporting-volume time series, weekday structure, the effect of known calendar events, the hard-mode share, the shape of the trial distribution over the year, and the extremes of word difficulty. ### Modeling Process

Computed descriptors (all from the dataset): reported-results N from 80,630 (Jan 7) to a peak of 361,908 on Feb 2, 2022, then a sustained decline to 20,380 (Dec 31), with a local minimum of 2,569 on Nov 30 (Thanksgiving). Weekday means (Mon-Sun): 92,754 / 93,844 / 91,749 / 91,341 / 88,160 / 88,308 / 90,320, i.e. a modest midweek-high, weekend-low pattern. The Thanksgiving week (Nov 23-28) averaged 26,033, a clear calendar dip. Overall mean N = 90,918. Hard-mode share: mean 7.76%, sd 5.05%, drifting over the year (time-trend R^2 = 0.223) and negatively correlated with the total count (corr = -0.435). Mean trial-distribution profile over the year: 1-try 0.47%, 2-try 5.84%, 3-try 22.73%, 4-try 32.93%, 5-try 23.64%, 6-try 11.56%, X 2.81% - unimodal and peaked at 4 tries. The unsolved share (X) drifted slightly down over the year (first 30 days 2.30% -> last 30 days 1.93%). Most difficult words by expected tries: parer (5.99), mummy (5.48), coyly (5.40), foyer (5.30), judge (5.23); easiest: train (3.13), plant (3.34), stair (3.39), third (3.44), clen (3.45).

### Outcome Analysis

Several features stand out. (1) A dramatic popularity arc: reporting volume peaked in early February 2022 (around the viral peak) and then decayed by more than an order of magnitude over the year, so the data are strongly non-stationary in level - the main reason a full-year trend is needed for in-sample fit but a stable-regime anchor is needed for a 2023 forecast. (2) A real weekday rhythm: midweek days average more reported results than weekends, consistent with the game being a daily habit. (3) A calendar-event effect: the Thanksgiving week shows a pronounced dip in reporting, an example of the 'day/event' driver of posting behaviour. (4) The trial distribution is stable in shape over the year (peaked at 4 tries, ~97% solved within six), so the per-day outcome profile is a reliable population descriptor even as the raw count swings. (5) Hard mode is a small but growing share (~8%) that tracks the player population rather than the word. (6) Word difficulty is bimodal in the extremes: a few notorious repeated-letter or obscure words (parer, mummy, coyly) are markedly harder than the ~3.1-try easy words (train, plant, stair). (7) Data-quality note: four entries have unusual word lengths (clen, tash = 4 letters; favor, rprobe = 6), likely data-entry variants of the played words; they were retained as-is. Limitations: all these are descriptive of 2022; the Twitter-mined percentages are subject to sampling and rounding (some rows do not sum exactly to 100), and the 'interesting' calendar associations (Thanksgiving, the February peak) are single-event observations rather than replicated effects.
