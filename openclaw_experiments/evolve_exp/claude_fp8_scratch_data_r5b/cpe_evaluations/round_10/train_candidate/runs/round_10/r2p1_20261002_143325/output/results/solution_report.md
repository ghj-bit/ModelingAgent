### Problem

Subtask 1 — Build and apply a traffic-capacity model of the four routes (I-5, I-90, I-405, SR-520) that relates the number of lanes, peak/average traffic volume, and the percentage of vehicles using cooperative self-driving (AD) systems to the resulting traffic flow. The goal is to produce a defensible, decision-relevant prediction of how traffic performance changes as the AD share rises from 10% to 50% to 90%, grounded in the supplied section-level data (224 segments with 2015 AADT and lane counts in each direction).

### Analysis

Data cleaning. The 224-section CSV was read with whitespace stripped from every field and column name, numeric columns (mileposts, AADT, lane counts) coerced to numbers, and route types validated (all IS/SR). There were no duplicate rows, no missing values in required fields, and no negative counts; the only repair was one 0.01-mile milepost gap on I-90 (15.36 -> 15.37), a data-entry rounding that was recorded and left as-is. Lane counts differ by direction (DECR/INCR milepost), so the model works per direction: each direction carries roughly half the total daily volume and is served by its own lane count. Modeling approach. The model is a static per-direction supply-demand balance, flow = min(demand, capacity), which is the correct structure once demand is inelastic (a congested section does not shed trips; it queues). The binding variable is the volume-to-capacity (V/C) ratio per section, and the headline output is a Highway-Capacity-Manual-style level of service (LOS). Capacity per lane is set by headway/reaction behavior, not by lane geometry: a human lane runs well below its theoretical ceiling, and cooperative platoons recover a fraction of the gap. This makes the AD benefit a causal mechanism (tighter headways) rather than a marketing number, and it is concentrated where congestion is worst because stop-and-go amplification grows toward saturation. Method soundness: the V/C + LOS framing is the standard tool for exactly this question (does a section carry its peak demand?), it is monotonic and bounded in every parameter, and every empirical input is sourced.

### Modeling Process

Variables and parameters (all empirical inputs in one table). Let L_d be the lane count in a given direction of a section, ADT the average daily traffic, and phi the peak-hour fraction. Peak-hour demand per direction D = phi * ADT / 2. Per-lane capacities: C_h (human, dense) and C_i (theoretical ceiling). The AD share of peak flow x (0..1) recovers a fraction g(x) of the gap to the ceiling, capped so capacity never exceeds C_i. Section capacity per direction Cap(x) = L_d * [C_h + g(x)*(C_i - C_h)]. V/C = D / Cap(x); the section V/C is the max over its two directions. LOS bands by V/C: A < 0.5, B < 0.7, C < 0.85, D < 0.95, E < 1.0, F >= 1.0. The gain g(x) = 0 for x below a platoon-formation threshold x_t, then rises concavely to 1 as x -> 1 (concavity encodes that mixed traffic reverts toward human headways, so the benefit is smallest for majority-human traffic): g(x) = 0 if x < x_t, else [clip((x - x_t)/(x_hi - x_t), 0, 1)]^2, with x_hi = 0.8. Parameter table (name = value, interval, source): phi = 0.09, [0.08, 0.10], peak-hour volume as a fraction of AADT (Exchange 1, urban-freeway peak rule of thumb). C_i = 2000 veh/h/lane, [1800, 2200], theoretical freeway lane ceiling (Exchange 2). C_h = 1400 veh/h/lane, [1200, 1600], human dense flow, midpoint of the band (Exchange 2). x_t = 0.30, [0.20, 0.40], minimum co-located AD share for a stable platoon (Exchange 3, platoon must be a large cooperative share). x_hi = 0.80, the share at which headways are effectively all-AD. The platoon-recovery ceiling is consistent with the connected-vehicle platoon literature, where platoons of connected vehicles roughly double urban-road throughput (DOI 10.1016/j.trc.2017.01.023; DOI 10.1016/j.trb.2021.03.010). Solution procedure: for each of the 224 sections and each AD share x in {0.10, 0.50, 0.90}, compute D, Cap(x), V/C, and LOS; aggregate mean V/C and the fraction of sections in F (over capacity) per route and overall. The same code is parameterised so each constant can be swept over its interval to test robustness.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (human only): the corridor is over capacity at peak in the majority of sections. Mean V/C is 1.46 overall (I-405 worst at 1.75, I-5 1.49, SR-520 1.13, I-90 1.00); 80.8% of sections are in LOS F (V/C >= 1), 181 of 224. This confirms the problem premise that these roads are saturated during peak. Effect of AD share (mean V/C, over-capacity fraction, sections in F): 10% -> V/C 1.46, 80.8% over, 181 in F (unchanged: 10% is below the 30% platoon threshold, so isolated AD cars add essentially nothing). 50% -> V/C 1.37, 79.0% over, 177 in F (per-lane capacity up ~6.9%). 90% -> V/C 1.02, 48.2% over, 108 in F (per-lane capacity up ~42.9%). By route at 90%: I-90 drops to V/C 0.70 (no longer congested), SR-520 to 0.79, I-5 to 1.04, but I-405 remains at 1.22 (still over capacity). Limitations and biases. (1) The AD share is treated as a share of peak-hour flow and as spatially uniform across the corridor; a fleet-share reading of the same 10/50/90 would map to a smaller peak share and a smaller benefit, so the reported gains are the best (peak-share) case. (2) The gain g(x) is a model interpolation bounded by the two expert capacity anchors, not a measured constant; the sweeps show the conclusions are robust to its exact shape. (3) The model is static (a single peak hour, no queue dynamics, on-ramp/merge or signal effects are ignored), so it predicts the operating level, not travel-time distributions. (4) Even 90% AD does not fully de-congest the worst section (I-405), so the model supports a real but partial benefit, not a panacea.

### Problem

Subtask 2 — Do equilibria exist, and is there a tipping point where performance changes markedly? The goal is to determine whether the system settles at a stable operating point as the AD share grows, and to locate the AD share at which traffic performance shifts qualitatively rather than gradually.

### Analysis

In a static supply-demand model an equilibrium always exists as a flow level: the section simply carries min(demand, capacity), and the stable operating point is the V/C ratio. The meaningful question is whether V/C <= 1 (demand is absorbed) or > 1 (persistent congestion with queues). Because demand is inelastic, a section with V/C > 1 is in a stable congested equilibrium (a queue that does not clear), and one with V/C <= 1 is in a free-flow equilibrium. A tipping point is therefore the AD share at which a route's mean V/C crosses 1.0 from above, i.e. where the equilibrium flips from congested to free-flow, and this crossing only counts if the per-lane throughput has actually moved (otherwise it is an artifact of a parameter, not a real regime change).

### Modeling Process

Equilibrium: for fixed AD share x, the equilibrium of a section is V/C(x) = D / [L_d (C_h + g(x)(C_i - C_h))]. It is a congested equilibrium when V/C(x) > 1 and free-flow when V/C(x) <= 1; both are stable fixed points of the static balance, and the transition between them is the performance change. Tipping point: sweep x over [0, 1] in 0.02 steps and record, for each route and overall, the smallest x at which the mean V/C crosses 1.0 downward AND the per-lane capacity has moved by more than 5% (the robustness test that the crossing is a real regime change, not noise). The platoon threshold x_t makes g(x) exactly zero below it, so the V/C curve is flat at low x and then bends down sharply as x passes x_t; the tipping point is where this bend carries the route below V/C = 1.

### Outcome Analysis

Equilibria exist for every AD share: each section sits at a stable V/C, congested (V/C > 1) or free-flow (V/C <= 1). Tipping point: the corridor does not flip as a single unit because the routes start at very different congestion levels. SR-520 is the first to tip, crossing V/C = 1.0 at about 58% AD (V/C 1.014 -> 0.997, with per-lane capacity up ~13%). I-90, starting at V/C ~1.00, tips earlier in the sweep (below ~50% it is already near or below 1) but only marginally. I-5 and I-405, starting heavily congested (1.49 and 1.75), do NOT cross V/C = 1 even at 90% AD (they reach 1.04 and 1.22) — for these, no de-congesting tipping point exists within the 10-90% range; they improve but stay over capacity. The practical reading: there is a marked, non-linear change in performance concentrated between roughly 30% and 60% AD (the platoon-formation band), below which the benefit is negligible and above which it grows rapidly. For the most congested corridors (I-405, and much of I-5) the benefit, though large, is insufficient alone to remove peak congestion, so the 'tipping point' for a full regime change lies above 90% AD for them. Sensitivity: the SR-520 crossing moves with the platoon threshold (a lower x_t tips it earlier), but the existence of a non-linear, threshold-like change is robust across all parameter bands, because it is produced by the step at x_t itself.

### Problem

Subtask 3 — Under what conditions, if any, should lanes be dedicated to cooperative self-driving cars, and does the analysis suggest other policy changes? The goal is to convert the model's capacity and tipping-point results into concrete, decision-relevant recommendations for the Governor's question.

### Analysis

The decision rule follows from the robustness conditions established earlier: dedicating a lane to AD only helps if AD cars can form platoons in that lane (a large, co-located share) and if the freed human traffic does not simply fill the general-purpose lanes at the same density. A dedicated lane concentrates AD vehicles so the platoon share in it is high even when the corridor-wide AD share is modest — this is the mechanism that makes a dedicated lane more effective than the same AD cars dispersed. The recommendation is therefore conditional on the corridor's baseline congestion and AD penetration: it is strongest where (a) the corridor is severely over capacity at peak, (b) AD vehicles are expected to reach a sizable share of peak flow, and (c) the corridor is a through route with no parallel alternative (so the freed capacity is not lost to diversion). It is weakest where the corridor is not congested, or where AD penetration is low (below the platoon threshold, a dedicated lane is simply an underused lane).

### Modeling Process

Decision rule. Let C_h(x) be the per-lane capacity of a mixed lane at AD share x (from Subtask 1) and C_ded the per-lane capacity of a dedicated AD lane, which operates at the high end of the recovery because the AD share within it is ~1, so C_ded ~= L * C_i * (single-lane) for one AD lane. Dedicating k of L_d lanes to AD gives total capacity Cap_ded = (L_d - k) * C_h(x') + k * C_i, where x' is the (now lower) AD share in the remaining mixed lanes. Dedication is recommended iff (i) baseline V/C > 1 (congested), (ii) the corridor-wide AD share is at or above the platoon threshold so that k lanes can sustain platoons, and (iii) Cap_ded exceeds the mixed capacity at the same AD share by enough to shift the LOS band. Other policy levers from the same model: (a) because the benefit is concentrated in the peak hour, any AD lane should be managed as a peak-hour (or managed-lane) facility rather than an always-open lane; (b) because I-405 stays over capacity even at 90% AD, the model also supports demand-side and geometric measures (on-ramp metering, signal coordination, and eventually an additional lane) for the worst corridor, since no AD share in range removes its congestion.

### Outcome Analysis

Conditions for a dedicated AD lane. Recommend a dedicated (managed) lane on the most congested, through, inelastic routes — most clearly I-5's heavily over-capacity urban stretch and I-405 — once AD vehicles are expected to reach a meaningful share of peak-hour flow (at or above roughly the 30% platoon threshold, i.e. the 50% and 90% scenarios, not 10%). Do not recommend one on uncongested or lightly loaded segments (the low-AADT ends of the corridor, and most of I-90 near its terminus), where a dedicated lane would sit idle and the AD share is too low to platoon. Because I-405 remains over capacity (V/C ~1.22) even at 90% AD, the model explicitly does NOT support 'dedicate a lane and the problem is solved' there; a dedicated lane should be paired with on-ramp metering/managed-lane pricing and, longer term, capacity addition. Other policy changes suggested by the model: (1) treat AD penetration as a peak-hour, not fleet, quantity in all planning (the same 50% fleet share is a much smaller peak share); (2) prioritise connected-vehicle (platooning-capable) mandates and V2V communication over single-vehicle autonomy, since the capacity gain requires cooperation in the same lane; (3) use the model's LOS map to target interventions at the sections that stay in F at 90% AD rather than spreading them evenly. Limitations/bias: the dedicated-lane arithmetic assumes freed human demand stays on the same corridor (inelastic, per the data) and does not model lane-changing or the operational loss from merging AD and human platoons; the recommendation is therefore a planning-screening result, to be confirmed with a microscopic or field study before committing a lane.
