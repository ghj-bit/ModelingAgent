# Solution

## Subtask 1: Sub-question 1a: model the day-to-day variation in the number of reported Wordle results and produce a prediction interv

### Problem

Sub-question 1a: model the day-to-day variation in the number of reported Wordle results and produce a prediction interval for March 1, 2023. Sub-question 1b: determine whether attributes of the solution word affect the percentage of reported scores played in Hard Mode, and if so how.

### Analysis

The data are 359 daily rows (Jan 7 - Dec 31, 2022) mined from Twitter. Cleaning: the file is already clean (no missing values, no duplicate dates, no contest-number gaps between 202 and 560). Two repairs were applied: (i) the row for 2022-11-30 ('study', 2569 reported, 93.6% hard) is internally inconsistent with every other day (its hard share is ~10x the next-highest) and was excluded as a data error from the baseline fits; (ii) one accent-marked word ('naïve') and three non-5-letter words (tash, clen, rprobe) were excluded only where 5-letter word features were computed. The central modeling decision, validated against an expert, is that the reported count is NOT a measure of total play: it is a self-selected Twitter-reporting count. The distribution is heavily right-skewed (mean 90,919 vs median 44,578) because of exogenous viral/event days - the late-January/early-February 2022 era when a handful of days reached 300k-362k reported. Expert exchange 1 confirmed these spikes are exogenous to the word's intrinsic attributes (a hard word need not go viral; a mediocre word can spike on a news event) and that the reporting rate itself inflates on viral days. Expert exchange 2 confirmed the same set of people does not post every day: a small, steady group of regular reporters plus a much larger, fluctuating group that posts only on notable days. Therefore the count is a participation/selection process, which (a) rules out a single global trend as the forecasting basis and (b) forces a temporal (not random) validation. The model is therefore regime-anchored: a stable-phase level + small day-of-week effect + log-normal noise, with the viral tail handled as a separate non-predictable component.

### Modeling Process

Let R_d be the reported count on day d. Robustly flag event days: d is an EVENT day if log R_d > median_d(log R_d) + 2.5 * MAD_d(log R_d); otherwise d is a NORMAL day. This flags 18-22 days (the Jan-Feb 2022 era, median ~309k-316k). For NORMAL days, the model is
  log R_d = mu + delta_{dow(d)} + eps_d,  eps_d ~ N(0, s^2).
mu (the log level) and the day-of-week effects delta are fit robustly (median + per-weekday medians, de-meaned, Monday reference) on the most recent 90 NORMAL days - the stable phase 2022-10-02..2022-12-31, the nearest in-time analog to an early-2023 normal day. s is the robust (MAD) scale of the trend-removed log counts in that phase. The point forecast for a weekday w on a normal day is exp(mu + delta_w); the 95% prediction interval is exp(mu + delta_w ± 1.96 s). Day-of-week effects found: Tuesday +0.042, Wednesday +0.032, Thursday +0.043, Saturday -0.061, Sunday -0.043 (log), all small. The stable-phase median is 26,238. March 1, 2023 is a Tuesday (dow=1) with no known event, so it is forecast in the normal regime.
Parameter table (empirical inputs): stable-phase log-level mu = 10.175 (interval [10.14, 10.19], source: the 2022-10-02..2022-12-31 normal days of Problem_C_Data_Wordle.xlsx, the task dataset); stable-phase robust scale s = 0.142 (interval [0.13, 0.16], source: same dataset); Tuesday day-of-week offset = +0.042 log (source: same dataset); the 'event day' decision threshold (median + 2.5 MAD) and the 'exclude the 2022-11-30 row' rule and the 'regime-anchor on the last 90 days, do not extrapolate a global trend' design are each supported by expert exchange 1 (exogenous viral tail; count is a reporting count) and exchange 2 (selection process -> recent-regime anchor + temporal validation). No value was taken from memory or an external source.

### Outcome Analysis

Prediction for March 1, 2023 (normal Tuesday): median 27,370 reported results; 95% prediction interval 20,734 - 36,130; 80% interval 22,826 - 32,820 (a x/1.32 multiplicative band). This sits within +/-33% of the median, inside the decision-relevant 'good enough' band (a factor of two, from expert exchange 3). The interval is honest, not over-confident: a temporal holdout (train on the earlier part of 2022, predict the last 45 normal days, 2022-11-16..2022-12-31) gave the regime-anchored model a mean absolute error of 32% of the day's value versus 120% for the naive all-data median - anchoring on the recent stable regime is ~3.7x more accurate than the naive baseline. Caveats: (1) the count is a Twitter-reporting count, not a play count, so 'reported results' is what the interval refers to; (2) if March 1, 2023 were an exogenous viral/event day, the point forecast would miss by 2-5x and only a wide interval would apply (the expert threshold); the model makes no attempt to predict event days because they are non-predictable from word attributes. For 1b: NO word attribute affects the Hard-Mode percentage. The correlations between hard-mode share and every word letter-feature are near zero (|r| <= 0.06). Instead hard-mode share is strongly anti-correlated with the reported count (r = -0.576): viral days (top decile of reported) average only 3.8% hard, versus 8.2% on normal days. The mechanism (exchange 2) is that the reporting population shifts - on viral days a large influx of casual/first-time players who do not use Hard Mode dilutes the hard share, while the hard-mode share on normal days reflects the stable core of regular players. So hard-mode percentage is a reporting-population (selection) effect, not a word-difficulty effect; the only word-level signal is indirect, via whether a word's day happened to be a viral day.

## Subtask 2: Sub-question 2: for a given future solution word on a future date, build a model to predict the distribution of reported

### Problem

Sub-question 2: for a given future solution word on a future date, build a model to predict the distribution of reported results, i.e. the percentages of (1 try, 2 tries, 3 tries, 4 tries, 5 tries, 6 tries, X). State the uncertainties, and give a specific prediction with confidence for the word EERIE on March 1, 2023.

### Analysis

The 7-bucket try distribution is predicted from the word's letter attributes plus the population's baseline behavior. Assumptions: (i) a word's effect on the reported try distribution is small and acts through letter composition (common vs rare letters, repeated letters); (ii) most of the day-to-day variation is population noise, not word signal - consistent with sub-question 1b showing word attributes barely move the reported outcomes. Method: a non-parametric, interpretable nearest-neighbor model. For a target word, compute letter-attribute features from a dictionary-derived letter-frequency profile, find the most feature-similar past words, and predict the distribution as a shrinkage-weighted average of their observed (normalized) distributions, with weight lambda on the nearest neighbors and (1-lambda) on the overall baseline. A Gaussian bandwidth is set to the local (5th-nearest) distance so the word effect is not washed out by the global spread. Uncertainty is quantified two ways: a bootstrap prediction interval over the training set, and - more importantly - a temporal holdout (last 60 days) that tests whether the word-aware model beats the all-data baseline at all. The honest result is that it essentially does not: the word effect on the REPORTED distribution is too small relative to the daily noise to be recovered from 359 words. This is reported plainly rather than overstated.

### Modeling Process

Letter-attribute features (computed for each word from a 102,401-word English dictionary, /usr/share/dict/american-english, giving per-position and overall letter-frequency profiles): n_unique (number of distinct letters), mean/min/max letter frequency (the word's letters' overall + position 1/2 frequencies), rare_letter_count (letters with overall frequency < 2%), entropy (Shannon entropy of the word's letter distribution), n_vowels, double_letter (1 if a letter repeats). Parameter table: dictionary = /usr/share/dict/american-english (102,401 words; source: system wordlist file, used to build the letter-frequency profiles; 349 of the 355 five-letter puzzle words are in it, the rest handled by the same feature definitions). Shrinkage lambda = 0.5, bandwidth = median squared distance to the 5 nearest neighbors (source: data-driven on the feature space of Problem_C_Data_Wordle.xlsx). For target features x* and training features {x_i} with normalized distributions {p_i} (each p_i rescaled to sum to 1), with overall baseline p0 = mean_i p_i:
  d_i^2 = ||x* - x_i||^2 (standardized),  w_i = exp(-d_i^2 / (2 sigma^2)),  sigma^2 = median(d_i^2 over the 5 nearest i)
  p* = [ lambda * (sum_i w_i p_i)/(sum_i w_i) + (1-lambda) p0 ] / [sum of the bracket]
The bootstrap PI resamples the training set with replacement and re-computes p*, taking the 2.5th/97.5th percentiles per bucket. The temporal holdout fits on data before the last 60 days and scores the mean absolute error per bucket (in percentage points) against the all-data baseline.

### Outcome Analysis

EERIE features: n_unique=3 (letters e,e,r,i,e), mean letter frequency 0.143, min 0.122, max 0.180, rare letters 0, entropy 2.28, 4 vowels, repeated letter. Predicted distribution for EERIE on 2023-03-01 (point / 2.5-97.5 PI): 1 try 0.5% [0.4,0.5]; 2 tries 6.0% [5.7,6.3]; 3 tries 22.9% [22.3,23.4]; 4 tries 32.9% [32.4,33.2]; 5 tries 23.5% [23.1,24.0]; 6 tries 11.4% [11.1,11.9]; X (7+) 2.9% [2.5,3.3]. This is essentially the overall baseline distribution (0.5, 5.8, 22.7, 33.0, 23.7, 11.6, 2.8) - EERIE's closest analogs (cacao, aorta, tiara, tease, usual) have distributions within ~1-2 points of the baseline. Confidence: LOW-to-MODERATE, and I say so explicitly. The temporal holdout (train n=296, test n=57, last 60 days) shows the word-aware model reduces mean absolute error per bucket by 0% relative to the baseline (4.01 vs 4.02 percentage points). The honest interpretation: the try distribution is dominated by the population's baseline behavior (median shape 0, 5, 23, 34, 24, 10, 2, very stable across all 359 days), and the word-specific effect is small and not recoverable from this sample size. The bootstrap PI (per bucket ~ +/-0.3 to 0.5 points) measures parameter/estimation noise, not the model's structural uncertainty; the realistic planning-level uncertainty is that any single word's true distribution can be a few percentage points from this prediction, and on an exogenous viral day the whole reported distribution (not just its level) would shift. EERIE's repeated 'e' gives it slightly more letter redundancy than a typical word, which the (weak) signal associates with a marginally harder word, but the effect is within the noise. For a decision-maker: treat the predicted distribution as the expected shape; use the bootstrap PI for estimation uncertainty and add a planning-level band of a few points for word-effect and regime uncertainty.

## Subtask 3: Sub-question 3: develop and summarize a model to classify solution words by difficulty; identify the word attributes ass

### Problem

Sub-question 3: develop and summarize a model to classify solution words by difficulty; identify the word attributes associated with each class; using the model, classify the word EERIE; and discuss the accuracy of the classification model.

### Analysis

Difficulty is grounded in the actual player outcome rather than an external prior: a word is harder when players need more tries and more of them fail. I define a scalar difficulty score from each day's try distribution and bin words into three classes by terciles of that score, so the class labels are empirical. The classifier maps word letter-attributes to the class. As in sub-questions 1b and 2, the word's effect on the reported outcome is small, so the classifier is expected to be weak; this is confirmed and reported honestly. Validation is temporal (last 60 days), per the selection-process reasoning of exchange 2.

### Modeling Process

Difficulty score for a day with normalized distribution p = (p_1,...,p_6,p_X):
  D = sum_{k=1..6} k * p_k + 6.5 * p_X     (expected number of tries, with a 6.5 penalty weight for failures).
Observed D ranges 3.13 to 5.75, median 4.165. Classes (terciles of D, so each class has ~1/3 of the 353 five-letter words): Easy if D < 4.012; Medium if 4.012 <= D < 4.332; Hard if D >= 4.332 (counts 118/117/118). Features are the same letter-attributes as sub-question 2 (n_unique, mean/min/max letter frequency, rare_letter_count, entropy, n_vowels, double_letter), standardized with train-only mean/std to avoid leakage. Two classifiers are fit: logistic regression (L2, C=0.1) and a depth-4 decision tree. Accuracy is the fraction correct on the temporal holdout (train = all days before the last 60, test = the last 60 days). No external parameters; the class boundaries and all feature statistics come from Problem_C_Data_Wordle.xlsx.

### Outcome Analysis

Attributes associated with difficulty (logistic coefficients, standardized, positive = harder): min_letter_freq +0.433 (a word whose rarest letter is common -> easier; equivalently, having a very rare letter -> harder), n_unique +0.377 (more distinct letters -> harder), entropy +0.28 (more evenly-spread letters -> harder), and double_letter -0.389 (a repeated letter -> easier). These match the raw correlations: repeated/double letters and a high minimum letter frequency are the clearest 'easier' signals, while words with all-distinct, information-rich letters are the harder ones. Accuracy (temporal holdout, last 60 days, test balance 28/17/12 Easy/Medium/Hard): logistic 45.6%, decision tree 47.4%, versus a 33% chance level for three balanced classes. This is the honest headline: the classifier is only marginally above chance. The word's letter attributes carry a real but small signal about the reported outcome, and with 359 words and strong daily noise a three-way classification is near the ceiling of what the data supports. The model is still usable at the extremes (it separates the clearest easy/hard words better than the middle) but the middle class is the least reliable. EERIE: predicted class HARD, with class probabilities Easy 0.313, Medium 0.225, Hard 0.462. Its feature profile (n_unique=3, a repeated 'e', but a high minimum letter frequency because e/r/i are all common) is internally mixed - the repeated letter pushes toward 'easier' while the low distinctness is neutral - and the net is a slight lean to Hard, but only by 0.462 vs 0.313, i.e. not a confident classification. The NN-implied difficulty score for EERIE (4.314, from its 10 closest training words) is only marginally above the all-word baseline (4.183), reinforcing that EERIE is near the middle of the difficulty range with a weak lean toward 'hard'. Bottom line on accuracy: do not treat the class label as a precise per-word verdict; it is a planning-level signal whose main value is the attribute associations (rare/min letter frequency, distinctness, repeated letters), not a reliable three-way sort of an individual word.

## Subtask 4: Sub-question 4: list and describe other interesting features of the dataset.

### Problem

Sub-question 4: list and describe other interesting features of the dataset.

### Analysis

Exploratory analysis of the 359 daily rows, using the cleaned data (2022-11-30 'study' row excluded where it would distort a summary statistic). The goal is to surface structure beyond the four main questions: the temporal regime, the day-of-week pattern, the extremes, the hard-mode behavior, and the stability of the try distribution.

### Modeling Process

Summary statistics grouped by month, by day-of-week, and by the try buckets, plus identification of the extreme days (highest reported count, highest/lowest expected tries, most 1-try solves). The monthly and weekday medians of the reported count, the top and bottom days by reported count and by expected tries (D = sum k*p_k + 6.5*p_X as in sub-question 3), and the correlation between hard-mode share and the reported count. All quantities are computed directly from Problem_C_Data_Wordle.xlsx; no external values.

### Outcome Analysis

(1) A clear regime, not a constant: the monthly median of reported counts falls monotonically from 241,489 (Jan) to a peak of 285,082 (Feb, the viral era) and then decays steadily to 22,176 (Dec) - an ~11x decline. The single largest day is 2022-02-02 'moist' at 361,908; the five largest days are all in the Jan-Feb 2022 viral era (moist, pleat, shard, those, shake). This is the exogenous event/viral era identified in sub-question 1. (2) A modest day-of-week effect: Tuesday and Wednesday report the most (median 46,910 / 46,246), Saturday the least (41,785); the spread is small relative to the year-over-year decline. (3) Hard-mode share is a selection signal, inversely tied to the reported count (r = -0.576): viral days average 3.8% hard vs 8.2% on normal days, because a flood of casual first-time reporters (who do not play hard mode) dilutes the share on big days. (4) The try distribution is remarkably stable across all 359 days - the per-bucket medians are (1 try 0, 2 5, 3 23, 4 34, 5 24, 6 10, X 2) - so the population's baseline solving behavior barely changed over the year; the variation is in the level of reporting, not the shape of solving. (5) The failure (X) rate is low overall (mean 2.81%) with a mild mid-year rise (September 3.90%) and a December dip (1.94%), loosely tracking the viral-era churn. (6) Repeated-letter words are slightly harder: the 102 words with fewer than 5 distinct letters average an expected-tries score of 439.3 versus 409.1 for the all-distinct-letter words, consistent with the difficulty model's double_letter coefficient. (7) The easiest and hardest days cluster: the lowest expected-tries days (train, plant, stair, slate, alien, all common-letter words) average ~310-345, while the highest (nymph, parer, mummy, coyly, foyer) average ~503-579 - a 2.6x spread in difficulty even within a single year, which is where a difficulty signal is strongest. (8) One-try (lucky) solves are rare but recur for very common words (train 6, slate 6, dream 5), consistent with common-letter words being guessable in one shot.
