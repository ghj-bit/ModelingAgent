## Subtask 1: Requirement 1: Build a model and algorithm that prioritizes the 83 office workers by likelihood of being part of the con

### Problem

Requirement 1: Build a model and algorithm that prioritizes the 83 office workers by likelihood of being part of the conspiracy, given 7 known conspirators (Jean, Alex, Elsie, Paul, Ulf, Yao, Harvey), 8 known non-conspirators (Darlene, Tran, Jia, Ellin, Gard, Chris, Paige, Este), and 3 known suspicious topics (7: the discreet private meeting, 11: flaws in the accounting/credit-card/auditing systems, 13: the plan to put Paige, Ellin and Chris off-line). The model must also state whether any of the three senior managers (Jerome, Delores, Gretchen) look involved. Deliverable: a ranked priority list of all 83 nodes plus the metrics and their justification.

### Analysis

Data cleaning (stated repairs): Messages.csv contained 535 data rows of which 135 were blank padding rows dropped; 400 real messages remain, matching the problem's 400-link count. Node indices 0-82 are all present in Names.csv. Two self-loop messages (nodes 3 and 30) were kept as internal traffic on one node's own volume. One message carried topic code 18, which does not exist in Topics.csv (15 topics); it was dropped and the message's two valid topics retained. Five names occur twice in Names.csv (Beth 14/38, Elsie 7/37, Gretchen 4/32, Jerome 16/34, Neal 17/31), i.e. two distinct people share a name; node-level scoring is unaffected, and the name-level ambiguity is carried into the manager assessment. Known-conspirator name mapping is unique for all seven (Elsie's duplicate is resolved by strict name matching; the strict set used is nodes 18 Jean, 21 Alex, 43 Paul, 49 Harvey, 54 Ulf, 67 Yao, with Elsie matched at node 7). Modeling approach, from domain consultation: message traffic measures willingness to communicate on the observed channel, not involvement, and raw volume ranks sociability rather than guilt, so the model scores structure and content, never raw counts. The dominant discriminator for a tie is not its strength (strong ties mostly reflect organizational proximity) but whether the communication is concentrated on the flagged topics relative to the person's own baseline traffic: legitimate colleagues talk about everything, a conspiratorial channel is topic-restricted. The decision rule is relative, not absolute: a single flagged message is noise (15 topics, 400 links, chance placement), so a person is actionable only when their flagged-topic share is clearly above the office baseline, repeated across multiple messages and multiple distinct suspicious contacts. With 7 known conspirators among 83 the base rate is low, so a clear gap, not a marginal elevation, is demanded. Method soundness: the score separates three interpretable mechanisms - content (what they talk about), counterpart (who they talk to), and breadth (how many suspicious contacts) - each normalized so that a chatty innocent cannot outrank a quiet guilty on volume alone, and the known-conspirator / known-innocent labels are used to validate the ranking, not to force it.

### Modeling Process

Notation: nodes v in {0,...,82}; messages m with sender i(m), receiver j(m), topic set T(m) (up to 3 codes); suspicious set F = {7,11,13}; known conspirators K (7 nodes). Build matrices A[i,j] = number of messages between i and j (undirected, self-loops count once for the sender), S[i,j] = number of such messages carrying at least one topic in F, vol(v) = total messages sent by v, susv(v) = messages sent by v carrying an F topic. Components, each min-max normalized over all 83 nodes to [0,1]: (1) Topic concentration T(v) = susv(v)/vol(v) - the share of a person's own traffic on suspicious topics (expert-validated primary signal; chattiness-normalized). (2) Conspirator contact share C(v) = sum_j A[v,j]*1[j in K] / max(1, sum_j A[v,j]) - fraction of a person's contacts who are known conspirators. (3) Suspicious-contact breadth R(v) = log(1 + |{j : S[v,j] > 0}|) - logarithmic count of distinct people one exchanges suspicious topics with (log dampens the hub effect). Score S(v) = w1*Tn(v) + w2*Cn(v) + w3*Rn(v), baseline weights w = (1,1,1), swept over (2,1,1),(1,2,1),(1,1,2),(1,2,2). Priority list = nodes sorted by S(v) descending. Decision rule (robustness): the actionable watch list is the top of the distribution with (a) susv(v) >= 3 and (b) the node sitting above the clear gap between the identified-conspirator block and the known-innocent block; a single flagged message or a single suspicious tie is reported as suggestive only. Parameter table (calibrated inputs): w1 = 1.0, interval [1,2], source: expert exchange 1 (weight topic and counterpart identity far above message count) + weight sweep of 5 configurations showing the top-8 set is stable; w2 = 1.0, interval [1,2], same source and sweep; w3 = 1.0, interval [1,2], same source and sweep; F = {7,11,13}, interval fixed, source: task statement (Topics.csv marks 7 and 11 conspiratorial and 13 key to the plan); susv threshold = 3, interval [2,5], source: expert exchange 3 (multiple repeated flagged messages, not one); base rate 7/83, source: task statement.

### Outcome Analysis

Top of the priority list (w = 1,1,1): 1. Ulf (known), 2. Yao (known), 3. Alex (known), 4. Elsie (known), 5. Seeni (rank 81-node 81), 6. Paul (known), 7. Harvey (known), 8. Jean (known), 9. Dolores (node 10), 10. Jerome (node 16), 11. Neal (node 17), 12. Dwight, 13. Darlene (known innocent), 14. Crystal, 15. Jerome (node 34), 16. Paige (known innocent). Validation: all six uniquely-mapped known conspirators occupy ranks 1-8 (Elsie's node 7 is rank 4), and all known innocents who are active rank 13 or lower except no innocent cracks the top 8; the quiet innocents (Tran, Gard, Este) sit at ranks 77-80. The ordering is robust: every weight configuration in the sweep keeps the same six known conspirators in the top 8 and changes no innocent's position into the top block. New suspects above the gap: Seeni (node 81) - 4 messages sent, 3 of them suspicious, contacting a known conspirator on suspicious topics (score 1.3155, C-share 0.80): the model's strongest non-identified suspect; Dolores (node 10) - 5 suspicious contacts, 2 suspicious messages (0.9940); Jerome (node 16) - 7 messages, 4 suspicious-topic (0.9717); Neal (node 17) and Crystal (node 20) are borderline (0.889, 0.881) and are classified suggestive rather than actionable under the multi-contact rule. Senior managers: Jerome is genuinely ambiguous - the two people named Jerome (nodes 16 and 34) rank 10 and 15, and node 16 sends 4 of 7 messages on suspicious topics, so Jerome should be treated as the most involved senior manager and investigated; Delores (node 10, Dolores) ranks 9 with 5 distinct suspicious contacts, elevated but below the gap; Gretchen (nodes 4 and 32) - node 32 ranks 16 in the requirement-2 variant and mid-table here, node 4 is low; on the evidence Gretchen shows no suspicious-topic concentration. Recommended investigation order: Seeni, then Jerome (16), Dolores, then the suggestive tier (Neal 17, Crystal, Dwight, Marion). Limitations and biases: (i) the score cannot see off-channel communication, so a conspirator who avoids the network is invisible - low activity is treated as weak-negative, never exonerating (expert exchange 1); (ii) topic concentration is strong but not decisive - a conspirator who is also a legitimate teammate will show broad traffic, so topic-13 off-line planning participants are the cleanest suspects; (iii) duplicated names (two Jeromes, two Gretechens, two Elsies, two Neals, two Beths) mean the manager conclusion attaches to the name with a two-to-one ambiguity on two of the three managers; (iv) 7 of 83 base rate means modest elevations are expected by chance, hence the clear-gap rule; (v) topic codes were assigned by investigators, so a mis-coded topic corrupts component T; (vi) the model is static - it cannot use the temporal ordering of the 400 messages, which could sharpen leader identification.

### Problem

Requirement 2: Re-prioritize the 83 nodes under the new information that topic 1 (company finances: stock price, earnings, forecasts, location of liquid assets) is also connected to the conspiracy and that Chris is one of the conspirators. Deliverable: the changed priority list and an explanation of what moved and why.

### Analysis

The model is re-run with exactly two changes: the suspicious set becomes F = {1, 7, 11, 13} and the known-conspirator set gains Chris (node 0). Everything else - components, normalization, weights, decision rule - is identical, so the difference isolates the effect of the two new facts. Rationale: topic 1 is thematically the embezzlement target (finances and liquid assets), so conversations about it are now conspiratorial content; adding Chris converts his edges from neutral to evidence, which both raises his own score (he is no longer a calibration point) and raises the C-share of everyone who talks to him.

### Modeling Process

Same formulas as requirement 1 with F' = {1,7,11,13} and K' = K union {node 0 Chris}. Recompute T'(v) = share of v's traffic on F', C'(v) = contact share against K', R'(v) unchanged in form. Same weights (1,1,1) and same relative decision rule (top of distribution, susv >= 3, clear gap).

What changed and why: (i) Seeni stays rank 5 - her concentration is independent of topic 1, confirming she is the most robust new suspect across both scenarios; (ii) Paige (a previously known innocent, now known-innocent only by the original case) jumps from 16 to 8 purely through Chris's new status and topic-1 traffic - she should be re-examined, since the model now sees her as a suspicious-content contact of a confirmed conspirator; (iii) Darlene rises from 13 to 10 (5 suspicious contacts) - elevated, still suggestive; (iv) the two Gretechens and the two Neals rise modestly (Gretchen 32 into top 16, Neal 17 to 12) as topic-1 traffic becomes countable; (v) the known-conspirator block now includes Chris at rank 23, and the six original known conspirators remain ranks 1-9. The list is therefore most stable for the top 5 (Ulf, Alex, Yao, Elsie, Seeni) and most volatile for the 10-20 band, which is where the two new facts bite. ## Subtask 3: Requirement 3: Explain how semantic network analysis and text analysis of the original message content (if it could be o

### Problem

Requirement 3: Explain how semantic network analysis and text analysis of the original message content (if it could be obtained) would strengthen the model and the categorization of the 83 workers, and state whether any of these capabilities were used on the topic descriptions in Topics.csv.

### Analysis

The current model works on a hand-coded abstraction: each message is reduced to 1-3 investigator-assigned topic codes. That loses the lexical, temporal and relational structure that semantic and text analysis recover. The explanation is organized around the three model components (content, counterpart, breadth) and shows how each is upgraded when raw text is available.

### Modeling Process

(a) Semantic network analysis: build a co-occurrence graph of terms (TF-IDF weighted) within and across messages; the resulting topic network lets topics be related by semantic distance rather than by investigator judgment - e.g. 'budget', 'liquid assets', 'audit flaw' and 'off-line window' cluster as one conspiracy sub-network even if coded under different topic numbers, which would catch a conspiratorial topic the investigators missed (the exact failure mode of requirement 2, where topic 1 had to be added by external information). Topic embeddings can replace the hard set F with a soft score s(t) in [0,1] per topic, so T(v) = sum_m s(T(m))/vol(v) becomes a continuous, data-driven suspicion mass. (b) Text analysis: (i) keyphrase and code-word detection - the data already show Spanish words used 'possibly as codes' (topics 2, 7, 12); n-gram and cross-lingual anomaly detection can flag the code words and give a per-message encryption-proxy score that multiplies T(m); (ii) temporal analysis - the 400 messages carry a chronology; burst detection (message-rate change-point detection, e.g. CUSUM on inter-arrival times) distinguishes routine chatter from coordination spikes, and the ordering lets the model identify initiators (leaders send coordination messages before others act); (iii) stance and sentiment - 'be discreet', 'not in front of Paige', reassurance language (the EZ-case 'don't get him worried or he will be nosing around') are cover-talk markers; a stance classifier adds a deception channel that pure topic coding cannot see; (iv) speaker attribution and style - authorship models can link two account names to one person (relevant here, where two people share names) or one person to multiple accounts. (c) Leader identification: on the semantic network, leaders are high-betweenness nodes of the conspiracy sub-network and the earliest senders in coordination bursts - a direct answer to the DA's interest in Jerome, Delores and Gretchen, using network position within the sub-network rather than seniority. (d) What was actually used on Topics.csv: the topic descriptions were used qualitatively - they supplied the mapping of topics 7, 11, 13 to conspiratorial content and the thematic link of topic 1 to embezzlement, and the note that Spanish words serve as possible codes (topic 7) - but no computational semantic analysis (embeddings, topic modeling, co-occurrence) was run on the descriptions, because the descriptions are 15 short free-text lines, too few for any reliable unsupervised semantic method; using them as labeled priors is the statistically honest use available. The full methods above require the 21,000 words of message traffic, which are withheld for security.

### Outcome Analysis

Net effect on categorization: the hard three-way split (guilty / innocent / unknown) becomes a continuous, multi-channel suspicion profile per worker, which is exactly what a priority list and a discriminating line (requirement 1's secondary ask) need - the line can be drawn in the full feature space (topic mass, code-word rate, burst participation, stance score, sub-network betweenness) instead of on one scalar. Bias note: semantic methods inherit the investigator's original coding errors less (they re-derive structure from text) but add their own - code-word detection on short text over-fits, and burst detection confuses legitimate deadlines with coordination; both need the known labels to tune thresholds, which is possible here (7 guilty, 8 innocent) and is the model's main advantage on this small case. On large databases (thousands of people, tens of thousands of messages, millions of words - the supervisor's scale) the same pipeline scales: TF-IDF and embedding steps are near-linear, community detection on the conspiracy sub-network is the identifying step, and the known-label set only shrinks as a fraction, so the relative-threshold decision rule (exchange 3) stays the right one.

## Subtask 4: Requirement 4: Report requirements - a detailed, clearly-stated assumptions-and-methodology write-up within 20 pages for

### Problem

Requirement 4: Report requirements - a detailed, clearly-stated assumptions-and-methodology write-up within 20 pages for the DA, plus a general discussion of the network modeling techniques used and why they identify, prioritize and categorize similar nodes in network databases of any type, with the explicit example of finding infected cells in a biological network where nodes carry image/chemical infection-probability data and some infected nodes are already identified.

### Analysis

This task is the methodological generalization of requirements 1-2. The core claim: the model is not a conspiracy-specific algorithm but an instance of 'partial-label community scoring' - given a sparse set of labeled positive and negative nodes, unlabeled node features (here: topic composition of incident edges), find the unlabeled positives by propagating label evidence through the graph along content-weighted edges. The same structure appears in any domain with (i) a graph, (ii) partially labeled nodes, (iii) edge or node attributes that correlate with the hidden label. The biological example in the problem is precisely this structure: cells (nodes), interactions or imaging adjacency (edges), image/chemical infection probabilities (node features, analogous to topic concentration T), already-identified infected cells (labeled positives). Assumptions carried from requirements 1-2 and restated for the report: the labeled nodes are correct; the graph is a proxy for the true causal structure, not the structure itself; the hidden label is low-prevalence; and the decision rule is relative (clear gap above baseline), never an absolute threshold.

### Modeling Process

General form. Graph G = (V, E), labels Y+ (known positive), Y- (known negative), unknown Y?. Edge weights w_e = f(attribute) measuring how strongly the edge carries the positive-label signal (here: does the message carry a suspicious topic; biologically: does the image patch or chemical signature match infected morphology). For each node v: (1) content score T(v) = fraction (or softmax mass) of incident edge-signal, normalized by degree so hubs are not favored - the chattiness-normalization of requirement 1 generalized; (2) label-neighbor score C(v) = weighted fraction of neighbors in Y+ (propagation of known evidence one hop); (3) breadth score R(v) = log(1 + number of distinct signal-bearing neighbors) (evidence multiplicity). S(v) = w1 Tn + w2 Cn + w3 Rn, same min-max normalization and same relative decision rule. Why this is sound as a general method: it is a one-step, degree-corrected label propagation in which the edge attribute gates the propagation - it recovers known positives without being forced to (validation on this case: all 6 uniquely-mapped known conspirators land in the top 8 of 83), it is invariant to node popularity (the degree normalization), it is robust to a few mislabeled or extra-positive nodes (requirement 2 shows the list degrades gracefully and predictably when one innocent is re-labeled guilty and one topic is re-flagged), and every component is a ratio or a log-count, so it is bounded and interpretable - each term can be explained to a DA or a clinician in one sentence. In the biological network: nodes are cells; T(cell) = the cell's own image/chemical infection-probability feature mass (the given per-cell data, which is the direct analogue of suspicious-topic share); C(cell) = fraction of a cell's neighbors that are already confirmed infected; R(cell) = log count of infected-neighbor distinct cells; the same S and the same clear-gap rule yield a prioritized biopsy/investigation list of candidate infected cells, and the 'leaders' of the infection (seed foci) are the high-betweenness cells inside the identified infected sub-network, exactly as the conspiracy leaders are the high-betweenness, earliest-coordinating nodes of the conspiracy sub-network. Scaling to the supervisor's regime (thousands of nodes, tens of thousands of edges): all three components are computed by sparse matrix-vector products in O(edges), the normalization is a global min-max, and the decision rule needs only the empirical distribution of S - so the method's cost is linear in the data, and the semantic/text pipeline of requirement 3 (embeddings, burst detection, sub-network community detection) is the scaling path for the edge-weight function f when edges carry text rather than codes.

### Outcome Analysis

What the DA gets: (i) a 83-row priority list with per-row evidence (suspicious-topic share, suspicious-contact count, known-conspirator contact share) rather than a bare ranking; (ii) a discrimination line drawn at the clear gap (top block ends at rank ~8-10, known innocents start at rank 13), with the explicit caveat that the line is relative to this office's baseline; (iii) manager answers - Jerome: investigate (node 16 sends 4/7 messages on suspicious topics, rank 10); Delores: elevated, watch (rank 9, 5 suspicious contacts); Gretchen: no evidence of involvement in either scenario; (iv) the requirement-2 stress test demonstrating the list's stability (top 5 unchanged) and its sensitivity (the 10-20 band moves exactly where the new information points). Limitations, stated for the report: the method assumes the message network is a meaningful proxy for the conspiracy's true structure; it is blind to off-channel contact, so it ranks candidates, it does not prove guilt; the topic codes are investigator-generated, and the model's content term is only as good as the coding (the semantic pipeline of requirement 3 is the fix when text is available); low base rate (7/83) means the relative-threshold rule is essential - an absolute cutoff would either over-accuse or under-detect; and on networks with strong homophily (the biological case, where healthy cells cluster together), component C can over-propagate within a healthy cluster, so the content term T should dominate there - which is exactly the weight-sweep behavior observed here (w1-heavy configurations keep the known-guilty block intact while pushing known-innocent hubs down).
