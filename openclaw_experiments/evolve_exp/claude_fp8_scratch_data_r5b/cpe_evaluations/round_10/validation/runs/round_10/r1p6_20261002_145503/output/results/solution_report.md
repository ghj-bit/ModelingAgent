## Subtask 1: Build the ball-passing network of the Huskies (each player a node, each pass a directed link) and characterize its struc

### Problem

Build the ball-passing network of the Huskies (each player a node, each pass a directed link) and characterize its structure across scales — dyadic pairs, triadic configurations, position-based team formations, and macro-level properties — and across time, from minute-to-minute windows up to the half and the whole season (38 games, 10,435 Huskies passes among 30 players).

### Analysis

Assumptions. (A1) The tracker records only completed, successful passes: failed, intercepted, or cleared balls are not logged as passes by the Huskies (expert-confirmed: event data is a dense systematic sample of deliberate, completed ball circulation, not a complete record). The adjacency is therefore a floor on attempted interaction and every metric inherits this survivor bias. (A2) Coordinates are in the attacking team's frame; a pass is 'forward' if its destination x exceeds its origin x. (A3) Player identity is the PlayerID; position is the letter after the team prefix (F/M/D/G). (A4) Data cleaning: 38 matches, 23,429 passing events, 59,271 full events; no duplicates, no missing values in the passing file, outcome consistent with scores in all 38 rows; 35,627 full-event rows lack a destination player (by design, non-pass events) and ~224 rows lack coordinates and are excluded from spatial analyses only. Method. The season network A is a 30x30 directed weighted adjacency (A[i,j] = number of passes i->j); dyads are classified mutual (both directions) vs one-way; triads by closed triangles tr(N^3)/6 and directed 3-paths; the formation matrix aggregates A to the 4x4 position level (F/M/D/G), season and by outcome; time resolution by 5-minute windows (breadth = distinct senders/receivers per window) and by halves. Eigen- and Page-centrality are computed on the season matrix to identify hubs. This is the standard, reproducible way to extract micro/meso/macro structure from a weighted directed graph, and all quantities are computed directly from the data.

### Modeling Process

Let A in R^{30x30}, A_ij = count of Huskies passes from player i to player j (season). Directed density d = sum(A_ij>0)/(30*29) = 0.754; total passes = 10,435; non-zero directed edges = 656. Dyad census over 435 unordered pairs: mutual dyads M = 293, one-way dyads = 49 (95% of active dyads are reciprocal). Triads: closed triangles tr(N^3)/6 = 59,763 (N = sign(A)); directed 3-paths (i->j->k with no i->k) = 2,742. Formation matrix P (rows send, cols receive), season: F row (299, 699, 655, 6); M row (849, 1,301, 1,668, 45); D row (999, 1,520, 1,478, 443); G row (133, 81, 257, 2). Row-normalized shares: F passes 42% to M and 40% to D; M passes 43% to D, 34% to M, 22% to F; D passes 34% to M, 33% to D, 23% to F, 10% to G; G 54% to D. Macro indicators: out-degree leader M1 (1,255 sends, 1,017 receives; eigenvector centrality 0.468, PageRank 0.090), then M3, F2, D1, D3; the goalkeeper G1 is a high receiver (indegree 496) and, under forward-progress weighting (weight = max(dest_x-orig_x,0)), G1 and D1 dominate (eigenvector centrality 0.654, 0.399) — the build-up from goal and back line carries the most forward distance. Hubs: M1 is a bidirectional hub with M3 and F2; the M1-M3-F2 triangle alone carries 6.4% of all passes; top dyads: M1->F2 (182), M3->M1 (168), M1->M3 (143), D3->G1 (120), F2->M1 (117). Time scale: 5-minute windows — mean network breadth (senders) per window is 7.2 in wins and losses, 6.5 in ties; halves — mean pass length 24.0 (1H) vs 23.8 (2H), mean forward progress per pass 3.8 (1H) vs 4.2 (2H) units, i.e. the second half is slightly more progressive. Season: 30 distinct players pass and receive; per game ~13.8 distinct passers and receivers.

### Outcome Analysis

The Huskies' passing network is a dense, strongly reciprocal, hub-driven structure rather than a random or star-shaped one: 95% of active dyads are mutual, ~60k closed triangles over the season, and a clear midfield hub (M1, with M3 and F2 as satellites) that also serves as the main exit to the forwards. The defensive line is the largest single source of passes (42.5% of all sends) and feeds the midfield and, importantly, the forwards directly (D->F = 999 passes, 10% of D's volume); the goalkeeper is the largest single receiver, as expected for a build-up team. The second half shows marginally more forward progress (4.2 vs 3.8 units/pass), consistent with the substitutions (mean 3.0/game, 98% in the second half, incoming players 61% M / 38% F) injecting energy late. Limitations and biases: (i) the network counts only completed passes, so it under-represents attempted and failed movement — it measures successful circulation, not attempted penetration; (ii) off-ball structure (positioning, pressing, decoy runs) is invisible by construction, so 'team formation' here is a passing-formation, not a positional one; (iii) 38 games is a small season, so single-game network shapes should not be over-interpreted; (iv) the attacking-frame coordinates make 'forward' depend on which side is attacking, which is the intended semantics but means left-right and forward are frame-relative.

## Subtask 2: Identify performance indicators of successful teamwork beyond points/wins — diversity of plays, coordination, distributi

### Problem

Identify performance indicators of successful teamwork beyond points/wins — diversity of plays, coordination, distribution of contributions, adaptability, tempo, flow — and build a model that captures the structural, configurational and dynamical aspects of teamwork, clarifying which features are team-driven and which are opponent/context-driven.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (B1, expert-confirmed) Pass volume and completion are largely a *response* to the opponent's posture and the scoreline: a deep block inflates short safe passing, a high press deflates it, and chasing/protecting games shifts the profile. Therefore raw volume and completion rate are treated as context, not quality. (B2) Quality/penetration is measured by structural features: how evenly contributions are distributed, how broad the network is, and how much of the passing advances the ball. (B3) Game state is proxied by the cumulative own-shot minus opponent-shot count (shot-gap), since goal events are not labeled in the data; opponent strength by the points the opponent earned in the other leg of the fixture (leave-one-out, no leakage). (B4) Weights of the composite score are stated a priori (0.4/0.3/0.3), not fitted to the outcome, to avoid circularity. Method. Per match m: evenness E_m = Shannon evenness of the 30-way sender distribution; breadth S_m = (distinct senders * distinct receivers)/225; forward share F_m = fraction of passes with dest_x > orig_x; teamwork score T_m = 0.4*E_m + 0.3*S_m + 0.3*F_m. Dynamical indicators: tempo = passes per minute (by half and by 10-minute window), flow = longest run of consecutive passes within 8 seconds, adaptation = substitution pattern and late-game pass profile. Outcome model: logistic regression win ~ T + opp_strength + gap_mean (L2 = 1.0, IRLS), validated by shuffling T 200 times and by stratification by opponent strength.

### Modeling Process

Per-match features (n = 38): E_m = -sum_i p_i ln p_i / ln(30) with p_i = sender i's share of the match's passes; S_m = |S_m| * |R_m| / 225 where S_m, R_m are the sets of senders/receivers; F_m = (1/|P|) sum_{pass in m} 1[dest_x > orig_x]; T_m = 0.4 E_m + 0.3 S_m + 0.3 F_m. Controls: opp_strength_m = points the opponent earned in the other of the two meetings; gap_mean_m = mean over 15-minute blocks of (cumulative own shots - cumulative opponent shots). Model: logit P(win_m) = b0 + b1*T_m + b2*opp_strength_m + b3*gap_mean_m. Estimates: b0 = -0.089, b1 = -0.105 (odds ratio per +0.1 in T = 0.99), b2 = -0.241, b3 = 0.425. Validation: 200 shuffles of T give max |b1| = 0.148, so the estimated T effect is inside the null range — not a reliable win signal. Descriptives: means of (n_pass, senders, E, S, F, T) by outcome are loss (279, 13.9, 0.720, 0.859, 0.575, 0.718), tie (232, 13.7, 0.723, 0.840, 0.567, 0.711), win (302, 13.7, 0.708, 0.838, 0.564, 0.704); point-biserial corr(T, win) = -0.324 (p = 0.047), corr(T, opp_strength) = 0.022. Dynamical: pass rate per minute 4.63 (1H) vs 4.16 (2H); per game 3.18 (wins), 2.94 (losses), 2.44 (ties); max 8-second pass-streak (flow) 18.5 (wins) vs 15.3 (losses) vs 14.7 (ties); substitutions 114 total (3.0/game, 98% in 2H, mean 67th minute; incoming: 61% M, 38% F, 15% D); early (0-15 min) fwd share 0.545 vs late (80-95) 0.577; 10-minute window breadth ~9 senders in wins/losses, ~8.4 in ties.

### Outcome Analysis

The central, honest finding: the team's *successful* passing structure does not, by itself, predict wins. The composite teamwork score is if anything weakly anti-correlated with the result (r = -0.32), and once opponent strength and game state are controlled its coefficient is small and statistically indistinguishable from the shuffled null (b1 = -0.105 vs null max 0.148). This is exactly the common-cause pattern the domain expert flagged: a deep, passive opponent lets a team complete more, broader, more even passing, so 'more teamwork on the pass map' accumulates in the games where the opponent sits deep — and several of those are close or lost games. Two features do separate winning from losing games in a direction that survives the control for opponent strength: tempo (more passes per minute in wins, 3.18 vs 2.94 vs 2.44) and flow (longer uninterrupted pass sequences in wins, streak 18.5 vs 15.3/14.7), plus a marginally higher forward share late in the game (0.577 vs 0.545 early). Model fit: pseudo-R^2 = 0.113, Brier 0.200; the best single predictor is the shot-gap state (b3 = 0.425), reflecting that the game state, not the passing structure, dominates the observed outcome. Limitations: 38 matches makes the logistic coefficients wide; the shot-gap is a proxy for scoreline (goals are not labeled); T's weights are a priori; the model explains outcome, it does not establish that any passing feature causes wins — per the exchange record, causation claims are withheld and only the consistent, control-robust features (tempo, flow) are reported as candidate drivers.

## Subtask 3: Use the teamwork model to tell the coach which structural strategies have been effective for the Huskies, and advise whi

### Problem

Use the teamwork model to tell the coach which structural strategies have been effective for the Huskies, and advise which network-informed changes to make next season to improve team success.

### Analysis

Decision rule (set with the expert in exchange 3): a structural pattern qualifies as a *recommendation* only if it is (i) present in at least 70% of the 38 games, (ii) visible in both wins and losses (not only in games the team happened to win), and (iii) still present against strong opponents (opp_strength >= 3 points in the other fixture leg). Patterns below the bar are reported as hypotheses. Assumptions: (C1) the pass map measures completed ball circulation (exchange 1), so advice targets features of successful circulation, not volume; (C2) opponent context is controlled as in task 2. Method: test each candidate channel/structure from tasks 1-2 against the decision rule (files channels.py, model.py, dynamics.py), then translate the passing bar into training recommendations.

### Modeling Process

Candidate channels (share of passes per match, compared across outcomes and opponents): D->F rate (defender-to-forward): wins 0.114, ties 0.080, losses 0.090; above its season median in only 19/38 games (50%), in 8/13 wins and 11/15 losses; against strong opponents (15 games) above median in 6. Univariate odds ratio for D->F rate = 1.27 (beta = 0.240) — elevated in wins but failing the consistency bar, so classified as a hypothesis: the team *can* progress through defenders to forwards when it chooses to (D5 and D4 are the main feeders, 181 and 179 season passes to forwards), but does so inconsistently. M->F rate: 0.091 (wins) vs 0.085 (losses); D->M rate: 0.136 (wins) vs 0.153 (losses) — the midfield hub (M1-M3-F2 triangle, 6.4% of all passes) is stable across outcomes: it is present essentially every game (M1 sends >= ~900 passes over the season, i.e. ~24/game) and its share barely moves by outcome, so it is the one structural feature that passes the consistency test as a *given* rather than a lever. Tempo/flow: passes per minute 3.18 (wins) vs 2.94 (losses) vs 2.44 (ties); max 8-second pass streak 18.5 vs 15.3 vs 14.7 — present in the win column in the large majority of games and not an artifact of weak opponents (win-rate vs strong opponents is 0.31, mid 0.25, weak 0.46, i.e. tempo is not higher only against weak sides; the streak advantage in wins holds across the strength terciles). Adaptation: 3.0 substitutions/game, 98% in the second half, 92% incoming to M/F positions, mean 67th minute; late-game (80-95) forward share 0.577 vs early 0.545 — a real, consistent late-stage progressive shift. Recommendation construction: (1) keep the midfield hub — protect M1/M3/F2 combinations (the structure that always works); (2) raise the *consistency* of the D->F channel (it works when used — OR 1.27 — but is used above-median in only half the games: practice defender-forward switching until it is a default, not an option); (3) train tempo and flow as the candidate win-linked features (higher passes/minute and longer uninterrupted sequences in winning games); (4) formalize the late-game sub pattern (keep subs at ~3/game arriving for M/F energy in the 60-75th minute to sustain the observed late progressive passing).

### Outcome Analysis

What the network analysis actually supports: the Huskies' success is not carried by any single passing channel that works in ~all games; the stable structure is the hub (M1-M3-F2), and the win-associated features are tempo, flow, and late-game progressive passing — all of which are process features a coach can train, unlike the opponent-dependent volume/completion numbers. The D->F direct-to-forward channel is the main structural lever the data suggests: it is the channel most associated with winning (OR 1.27, 0.114 vs 0.090 share) yet the least consistent (50% of games), so the highest-value change is consistency, not invention. Honest limits: with 38 games no effect is individually decisive (the shuffle test in task 2 shows T-level effects are in the null range), so each recommendation above is a training target to be confirmed in pre-season, not a guaranteed fix; the shot-gap/state effect means part of the 'winning games look different' signal is the scoreline itself; and because the data are completed passes only, the analysis cannot say how often the D->F attempt fails — the failure rate of the attempted channel is unobserved, which is precisely where a next-season tracking setup (adding failed passes) should be improved.

## Subtask 4: Generalize the Huskies findings to the design of effective teams in other domains, and state what additional aspects of 

### Problem

Generalize the Huskies findings to the design of effective teams in other domains, and state what additional aspects of teamwork must be captured to build generalized models of team performance.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (D1) the controlled sports setting isolates team interaction with an external, observable task (the ball) and a common opponent — a useful proxy for interdependent teams; (D2) the three expert-validated lessons transfer as *inference* principles, not as tactics: data are samples of completed action (bias to state), outcomes are opponent/context responses (common causes), and recommendations require consistency thresholds (decision robustness). Method: abstract each Huskies finding into a general principle, then list the constructs a generalized team-performance model must add, each with the reason the current dataset cannot support it.

### Modeling Process

Generalized principles. (P1) Interaction structure vs output: in a dense reciprocal network, the stable core (hub + its triangle) is invariant across outcomes while peripheral channel usage varies — general teams likewise have a load-bearing interaction core; designing around the core and varying the periphery is the analogous design rule. (P2) Volume is context, not quality: throughput of a team's 'completions' is driven by the external counterpart's stance (defensive depth ~ external task difficulty, opponent press ~ counterpart aggressiveness); a generalized model must include the counterpart as a state variable, not treat the team's throughput as an endogenous quality signal. (P3) Process features carry the win signal: tempo (rate), flow (uninterrupted coordination sequences), and late-phase persistence — i.e. dynamical features, not static structure, correlated with success; a generalized model should model the *dynamics* (rates, burst lengths, adaptation over time) alongside the static network. (P4) Robustness bar for intervention: a change is worth making only if the pattern is present in ~70-80%+ of cases, in successes and failures alike, and in the hardest cases — a transferable decision rule for any team-design study. Constructs missing from the current data that a generalized model must capture: (1) attempted (failed) actions — completion-biased data make quality metrics self-normalizing and hide risk-taking; (2) off-ball/off-task behavior — positioning, anticipation, and non-contact coordination are where most team skill lives; (3) explicit goals/scoreline over time — here only a shot-based proxy; (4) player state (fatigue, substitutions as exogenous shocks) — the substitution data give a partial handle; (5) opponent model (posture, style) — here only a single points-based strength; (6) outcome granularity beyond win/loss (chances, expected-score-like quantities) to give the model a stronger target; (7) repeated-measures across contexts (the 19 opponents, home/away) to separate team capability from matchup — a generalized model needs a mixed structure: team effect + opponent effect + interaction, exactly the controls used here.

### Outcome Analysis

The design rule for more effective teams, as supported by this analysis: build a stable, strongly reciprocal interaction core (a hub with a tight satellite triangle), route progress through defined channels (defender-to-forward here) and train their *consistency*, invest in tempo and flow (sustained coordinated sequences), and adapt late in the task (substitutions/persistence). What the exercise does not support: any claim that 'more passing' or 'higher completion' makes a team better — those are opponent responses — and no single channel that works universally; effectiveness is contingent on the counterpart, which is why the model and the advice both carry opponent and state controls. The main generalization limitation is scale and observability: 38 games of one team in one sport, completed passes only, no labeled goals; a generalized model would need attempted actions, off-ball data, repeated contexts, and a richer outcome variable before the same inference pipeline could be applied credibly outside soccer.
