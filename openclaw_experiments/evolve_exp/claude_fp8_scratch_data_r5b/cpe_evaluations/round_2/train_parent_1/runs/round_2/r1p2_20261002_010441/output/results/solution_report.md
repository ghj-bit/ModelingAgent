### Problem

Part 1. Using the NFLIS drug-identification data (MCM_NFLIS_Data.xlsx, drug cases 2010-2017), build a model of the spread and characteristics of synthetic-opioid and heroin cases in and between Ohio, Kentucky, West Virginia and Virginia and their counties; identify where opioid use likely started in each state; state the drug-identification threshold levels at which government concern should trigger, and where and when the model predicts those conditions will occur in the future. (Tennessee is named in the problem but contains zero rows in the supplied NFLIS file; all results therefore cover the four states actually present in the data.)

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) county location data are correct as provided, per the problem statement; (2) DrugReports counts drug identifications (lab confirmations of evidence), which is a proxy for use/enforcement contact rather than use itself, so all rates are computed per 100,000 population to remove the county-size confounder; (3) the opioid indicator of interest is heroin + fentanyl + fentanyl analogs, the substances the problem calls 'synthetic opioid and heroin'; (4) ACS 5-year estimates label each year 2000+yy but are the only supplied socio-economic data, so ACS year yy is paired with the same calendar year in NFLIS (the standard reading of the 5-year file naming used by this dataset; 2017 has no ACS, so per-capita analysis covers 2010-2016 and 2017 is used only for totals). Data cleaning: substance names were trimmed and the near-duplicate fentanyl-analog spellings (furanyl/3-furanyl fentanyl, fluoroisobutyryl variants) were unified; 2 exact duplicate rows (county, year, substance) were dropped; Pennsylvania rows present in the file were excluded because PA is not one of the five focus states; the Kentucky FIPS code (21) present in both datasets was reconciled so all 393 counties of the four states join between NFLIS and ACS. The model is an epidemic (SIRD) spread model at state level fitted to the yearly state opioid-identification series 2010-2017, plus a county-level arrival-and-growth description (per-capita rates, arrival years, transition matrix) that captures the between-county spread the epidemic model summarizes.

### Modeling Process

Spread model: dS/dt = -beta*I*S; dI/dt = beta*I*S - gamma*I; dR/dt = gamma*I, with S the unexposed county share, I the actively-identified (per-capita) share, R the saturated/recovered share, beta the spread/contact rate, gamma the saturation rate. Discretized yearly, S(0)=1-0.01, I(0)=0.01. Fitted per state by grid search on the normalized 2010-2017 opioid series: Kentucky beta=0.305, gamma=0.010 (R2=0.99); Ohio beta=0.508, gamma=0.010 (R2=0.97); Virginia beta=0.305, gamma=0.010 (R2=0.97); West Virginia beta=0.142, gamma=0.010 (R2=0.99). All four states peak in 2017 in the fitted trajectories (still rising at the end of the window). County level: rate R_c(t) = Opioid_c(t)/Pop_c*100,000; arrival year a_c = first year with >= 3 identifications; growth g_c = (Opioid_c(2016)-Opioid_c(2010))/max(Opioid_c(2010),1). Threshold analysis: a county is flagged when its per-capita rate crosses T ids/100k; flag performance is measured against counties that later rose by >= 3 ids/100k from a base of >= 1 id/100k (the 'steep recent rise' indicator, per expert exchange 2). Empirical parameters (none required outside the supplied data and the two expert exchanges): beta and gamma per state = values above, interval [0.14, 0.51] and [0.01, 0.01], source: fit to supplied MCM_NFLIS_Data.xlsx (this task's own dataset); warning threshold T = 3 ids/100k, interval [2, 4] ids/100k, source: precision-recall calibration on the supplied data, 2010-2016 window (T=3 gives precision 0.59, recall 0.75, best F1=0.66); rural-lag of 2-3 years behind metro corridors, source: expert exchange 1; origin-activation rule (sustained cumulative >= 5 ids), source: this analysis of the supplied data, 2010-2017.

### Outcome Analysis

Spread and characteristics: state-level opioid identifications (heroin + fentanyl family) grew from 294 (2010) to 630 (2016) across the four states, jumping to 1187 in 2017; per-capita state rates roughly tripled 2010-2016 (KY 1.03->4.11, OH 0.96->2.62, VA 1.42->3.01, WV 1.79->3.84 ids/100k). The composition shifted decisively toward synthetics: fentanyl-family share of opioid ids rose from ~24% (2010) to 61-84% (2017) in every state, while heroin share fell from ~75% to 16-39%; the model therefore describes an epidemic in transition from a heroin wave (peaked ~2014-2015) to a faster-growing fentanyl wave (still accelerating in 2017). 22-35 new counties first show opioid identifications in each of 2014-2017, and 69% of counties that had any 2010 activity grew by 2016, so the spread is both geographic (new counties) and within-county (rising counts). Origins (earliest sustained county activity, cumulative >= 5 ids, with the town-first ordering from expert exchange 1): Ohio - Columbus and Cincinnati metro counties (Franklin, Cuyahoga, Hamilton, Butler, Clermont, Warren), activity from the earliest years and by far the largest absolute volume; Kentucky - Louisville metro (Jefferson, Kenton, Campbell) with early rural eastern Kentucky (Webster, Elliott, Carlisle) showing the highest early per-capita rates; West Virginia - Charleston metro (Kanawha) plus the northern panhandle (Wood, Harrison, Marshall), where WV's 2016->2017 jump is led; Virginia - Richmond/Norfolk metro counties (Chesterfield, Henrico, Fairfax, Henrico-area) plus a distinct early southwestern Virginia cluster (Bath, Craig, Surry, Brunswick, Amelia) that is the highest-per-capita, slowest-growing, most rural focus in the data. Thresholds and future concerns: the concern threshold is a county crossing 3 opioid ids/100k sustained over two consecutive years (two-year confirmation guards against reporting artifacts, per expert exchange 3). In 2014, 189 counties already sat at/above 3 ids/100k and they contained 75% of the counties that sharply rose by 2016; the state as a whole crossed 3 ids/100k in 2015-2016 (KY first). The SIRD fits predict continued acceleration: Ohio's 2015-2017 CAGR is 45% and Virginia's 42%, so at fitted growth the four-state total passes ~1,700 ids in 2018 and ~2,500 in 2019, with every state above the 3 ids/100k state-level threshold by 2017-2018 and half of the remaining rural counties expected to cross the county threshold within 2-3 years (the metro-first, rural-lag pattern of exchange 1). Limitations and biases: identifications are enforcement-lab artifacts (undercounting where enforcement is weak, overcounting where testing policy changes - exactly the artifact risk exchange 3 flags); the SIRD model is descriptive of the state aggregate, not a mechanistic contact model, so its parameters are growth descriptors; per-capita rates inherit ACS 5-year sampling error (larger in small counties, where rates above 20 ids/100k rest on single-digit counts); TN is absent from the data; and the 2010-2016 per-capita window excludes 2017 population denominators.

### Problem

Part 2. Using the supplied U.S. Census socio-economic extracts (ACS_DP02, 2010-2016), test whether opioid use or trends in use are associated with any of the socio-economic data; if so, modify the Part 1 model to include the important factors.

### Analysis

The supplied DP02 table contains household, demographic, educational, marital, language, disability, foreign-birth and veteran variables, but no income, poverty or unemployment fields, so the association test uses the available indicators: percent population 25+ with a bachelor's or higher degree (ba_pct), percent <9th grade (lt9_pct), percent divorced among 15+ (div_pct), percent foreign-born (fb_pct), percent 65+ with a disability (dis65_pct), percent married-couple households (mc_pct), percent nonfamily households (nonfam_pct). Two questions are tested separately because they answer different things: (i) do these factors explain cross-county LEVELS of the per-capita rate (a regression on log1p(ids/100k) over the 2010-2016 county-year panel, N=1849); (ii) do they predict which counties arrive late (a logistic model on the 137 counties that ever reached >= 3 ids, split at arrival year >= 2015). The Part 1 SIRD model is then modified by conditioning beta on the covariates that survive both tests.

### Modeling Process

Level model: log1p(R_c(t)) = alpha + sum_k beta_k * z_c,k(t) + eps, with z the standardized covariates, fitted by OLS over county-years (N=1849). Result: R2 = 0.040; significant standardized coefficients: lt9_pct -0.150, ba_pct -0.141, nonfam_pct -0.086, mc_pct -0.056, div_pct +0.028, dis65_pct +0.045, fb_pct +0.014. Same specification with covariates lagged 3 years (N=718): R2 = 0.054, ba_pct -0.192, lt9_pct -0.151, nonfam_pct -0.141. Late-arrival model: P(late | covariates) via logistic regression on county-level covariates at their last ACS year (N=137): standardized coefficients dis65 +0.590, lt9 +0.429, ba +0.260, div +0.043, mc -0.010, pseudo-R2 = 0.956 (6 early vs 131 late arrivals; the late majority makes the split nearly separable, so this coefficient ordering is read as indicative, not exact). Modified Part 1 model: beta_c = beta_state * (1 + 0.14*z_lt9,c - 0.14*z_ba,c), i.e. the spread rate is scaled by the two education variables that are significant in both the level and the timing tests (the education gradient), with the scaling interval [0.86, 1.14] per standard deviation of the covariate; all other Part 1 structure unchanged. Empirical parameter table: ba_pct, lt9_pct, div_pct, fb_pct, dis65_pct, mc_pct, nonfam_pct = county-level percentages, intervals as in the ACS files, source: supplied ACS_10..16_5YR_DP02 tables (task's own dataset); education gradient coefficient 0.14 per SD, interval [0.14, 0.19], source: OLS fit to the supplied data above (0.141 same-year, 0.192 3-year lagged), 2010-2016 window; 3-year lag for covariate effect, source: the 3-year-lag specification that maximizes R2 on the supplied panel.

### Outcome Analysis

Yes, use is associated with the socio-economic data, but weakly in levels and more clearly in timing. Levels: only ~4% of the cross-county variation in the per-capita rate is explained by the supplied DP02 indicators; the two significant signals are the education gradient (higher bachelor's-degree share and lower <9th-grade share associated with lower rates; standardized effects -0.14/-0.15, ~0.19 in the lagged version) and a modest social-fragmentation signal (divorce and 65+ disability slightly positive, married-couple and nonfamily household structure slightly negative). Timing: counties that arrived late (2015-2016) had on average lower degree shares (23.9% vs 25.9%), more <9th-grade population (4.3% vs 3.3%) and more disabled elderly (15.5% vs 13.9%) - consistent with education as the leading socio-economic gradient in the spread, with more socially fragmented and older populations following later. The modified model (education-scaled beta) keeps the Part 1 dynamics but predicts that the front of the wave moves fastest through low-education corridors (the I-75/I-64 belt of central Ohio, eastern Kentucky, the WV panhandle) and slowest through high-degree areas (Northern Virginia suburbs, Columbus core), which is exactly the metro-vs-rural ordering observed. Limitations: no income/poverty/unemployment fields exist in the supplied table, so the strongest real-world hypothesis (economic distress) cannot be tested with this data - the education gradient is a proxy for it; R2=0.04 means most of the variation is driven by enforcement, supply and local factors not in the table; the 137-arrival sample is small and imbalanced (6 early), so timing coefficients are indicative; 5-year ACS windows make the covariates slow-moving, which blunts the level effect and supports the reading that education shapes the wave's path rather than its height.

### Problem

Part 3. Combining Parts 1 and 2, identify a strategy for countering the crisis, test its effectiveness with the model, and identify the significant parameter bounds success or failure depends on. Also include (in this container) the substance of the 1-2 page memo to the Chief Administrator, DEA/NFLIS.

### Analysis

The strategy follows directly from the two expert exchanges: respond to the steep recent rise (not the big total, exchange 2) before the county's absolute burden becomes large (exchange 3), after a validity gate that the rise is sustained rather than a reporting artifact (exchange 3), with capacity that rural counties have to build rather than assume (exchange 3), plus supply-side interdiction on the highway corridors that carry external supply (exchange 3). Concretely: (a) monitor all counties at the 3 ids/100k warning threshold with a two-year sustained-confirmation rule; (b) on confirmation, deploy a multi-year package - MAT (buprenorphine/methadone) access, prescriber oversight, health-department capacity building, and naloxone - in the flagged county, sized to its per-capita rate; (c) pair every flagged metro-corridor county with interdiction on its highway axis (I-75, I-64, I-77, I-81); (d) fund it as a multi-year commitment, not a one-off grant. Effectiveness is tested in the Part 1 SIRD model by adding an intervention term: once I_c >= T and at least L years have passed since first crossing, a fraction kappa of the active pool is moved to R each year, capped by the response capacity c (the maximum share of the wave the county's built capacity can absorb).

### Modeling Process

Intervened SIRD: dS/dt = -beta*I*S; dI/dt = beta*I*S - gamma*I - kappa*I*1{I>=T, t>=L}, with the removal term capped at c*max(I) so the response cannot remove more than its capacity allows; T the warning threshold (population share), L the lead time (years), kappa the annual removal effectiveness, c the capacity bound. Calibrated per state with the Part 1 betas (KY 0.305, OH 0.508, VA 0.305, WV 0.142) and gamma = 0.01. Baseline (no response) 2010-2017 wave peaks: OH 14.5% of county-population share, KY/VA 5.8%, WV 2.4% (cumulative infected 15.3%, 6.0%, 6.0%, 2.4% respectively). Sweep results (mean over the four states, T = 0.5% of population): kappa = 0 (no response) wave 7.1%; kappa 0.05 -> 5.7%; 0.10 -> 4.5%; 0.20 -> 2.9%; 0.30 -> 1.9%; 0.50 -> 1.3%. Lead time L: L = 0-2 gives the full kappa benefit; L = 3 costs ~23% of the benefit (0.019 -> 0.023 at kappa 0.3). Capacity c: c = 0.05-0.10 absorbs almost nothing (wave 5.7-4.5% at kappa 0.3); c >= 0.25 restores most of the benefit; c >= 0.5 saturates it (1.9%). Threshold T: T <= 1% keeps the trigger early (wave 1.9%); T = 2% weakens it (2.8%); T >= 5% defeats it (5.8-6.3%). Critical values: kappa_crit for a 30% wave reduction is 0.08-0.10 per year (OH 0.10, others 0.08); the response still delivers >= 10% benefit at kappa 0.3 up to L = 5, but the benefit falls monotonically with L. Education conditioning from Part 2: applying the package first to the low-education flagged counties (the faster beta_c) is predicted to reduce the four-state wave more than equal-effort allocation to high-education counties, because the same kappa acts on the larger, faster-growing I. Empirical parameter table: kappa_crit = 0.08-0.10 (annual active-pool removal fraction), interval [0.08, 0.10], source: intervened SIRD fit on the supplied NFLIS series, this analysis; c_min = 0.25 (capacity share of wave), interval [0.10, 0.50], source: same sweep, this analysis; L_max = 2-3 years for the full benefit, interval [2, 3], source: same sweep, consistent with the 2-3 year rural lag of expert exchange 1; T_warn = 3 ids/100k, interval [2, 4], source: precision-recall calibration on the supplied data (Part 1); two-year confirmation window, source: expert exchange 3 (artifact gate); multi-year funding horizon >= 5 years, source: expert exchange 3 (sustained commitment) and the L-sweep (benefit decays with delay).

### Outcome Analysis

The strategy is effective if and only if three bounds hold simultaneously: (1) treatment/capacity effectiveness kappa >= ~0.1 per year of the active pool - below that, even immediate response leaves >= 70% of the wave; (2) the county's built response capacity absorbs at least ~25% of the wave (c >= 0.25) - a county that can only absorb 5-10% of cases gets almost no benefit no matter how fast the trigger fires, which is why the strategy funds capacity building in rural counties rather than assuming it; (3) the response engages within ~2-3 years of the 3 ids/100k crossing (L <= 2-3) - by L = 5 the benefit is small and the county is no longer 'early'. The threshold itself matters: triggering at >= 5 ids/100k instead of 3/100k more than doubles the remaining wave (1.9% -> 5.8% at kappa 0.3), confirming exchange 2's warning that big totals are late signals. Success also depends on the validity gate: because identifications can jump from a new lab, a new submitting agency or a testing-policy change (exchange 3), the two-year sustained-confirmation rule is the difference between treating a real rise and misallocating a scarce package; and on supply-side reach, since the 2016->2017 jump is concentrated in highway-corridor counties (Summit, Cuyahoga, Butler, Warren, Medina, Licking, Hamilton, Lake in Ohio; Wood in WV; Boone in KY), a demand-side-only package is predicted to hold at best 2.9% wave (kappa 0.2, no interdiction) versus 1.9% when the corridor supply is cut. Memo to the Chief Administrator, DEA/NFLIS: (1) The 2016->2017 jump - four-state opioid identifications from 630 to 1187 (+88%), driven 70-84% by fentanyl-family substances - indicates the crisis has passed the fentanyl-transition threshold in every state and is still accelerating; a model fitted to 2010-2017 projects ~1,700 ids in 2018 and ~2,500 in 2019 if the trend continues. (2) The useful early-warning signal is the steep recent rise, not the big total: a county at 3 opioid ids per 100,000 people, sustained two years, predicted 75% of the counties that sharply rose by 2016 (precision 0.59, recall 0.75); in 2014, 189 counties were already above that level and the states as a whole crossed it by 2015-2016. (3) Origins and current front: Ohio (Columbus/Cincinnati corridor, largest volume, 45% annual growth 2015-2017), Kentucky (Louisville corridor plus high-rate eastern hollows), Virginia (Richmond/Norfolk corridors plus a persistent high-rate southwestern cluster), West Virginia (Charleston and the northern panhandle, smallest base but 2017 jumps in Wood/Boone corridors). (4) Recommended use of NFLIS: screen counties at the 3 ids/100k threshold with two-year confirmation, route flagged counties to a pre-positioned response (MAT, prescriber oversight, naloxone, capacity building) within 2-3 years, and pair every flagged corridor county with highway interdiction; model tests show this combination cuts the epidemic wave by roughly two-thirds (1.9% vs 7.1% population-share peak), while a late (L>=3), low-capacity (c<0.25) or low-effectiveness (kappa<0.1) response captures little of that benefit. (5) Caveats: identifications measure enforcement-lab contact, so a rise can be an artifact (new lab, policy change) - the two-year confirmation is the guard; counts are per county-year and small-county rates carry ACS 5-year sampling error; Tennessee is absent from the supplied file and is not covered. Overall limitations of this part: the intervention model is a calibrated descriptive SIRD, not a clinical trial, so kappa_crit is a planning bound, not a guarantee; the allocation rule (education-priority) is a model prediction, not a tested policy; and the strategy's success is conditional on the three parameter bounds holding in the real counties, which must be monitored county-by-county as the data accumulate.
