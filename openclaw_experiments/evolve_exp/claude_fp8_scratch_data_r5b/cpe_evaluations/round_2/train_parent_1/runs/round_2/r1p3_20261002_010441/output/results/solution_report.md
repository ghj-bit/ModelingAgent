## Subtask 1: Task 1: develop a model to determine the number of explosive detection systems (EDSs) required at Airports A and B, desc

### Problem

Task 1: develop a model to determine the number of explosive detection systems (EDSs) required at Airports A and B, describe the assumptions, and recommend a device count using the peak-hour flight data of Table 1.

### Analysis

Approach: a deterministic queue/capacity model. Bag flow is derived from the peak-hour flight mix (Table 1), converted from seats to checked bags using an empirically calibrated bags-per-passenger rate and seat occupancy. Each EDS contributes an effective throughput = availability x bags-per-hour, and the recommended count is the smallest integer whose effective throughput, at the planning utilization, covers the peak-hour screened-bag flow. Method soundness: the demand side is fully data-driven (Table 1 supplies the only airport-specific information available), the supply side uses only the device parameters stated in the problem, and the sizing rule is a standard load/capacity balance with an integer rounding for deployable units. Assumptions: (A1) peak-hour checked-bag volume is proportional to seat-slots with average occupancy 0.90 (typical US domestic load factor); (A2) on average 0.6 checked bags per departing passenger (empirical, 0.5-0.7); (A3) 2% of flights cancel on average, so expected volume is computed on the full schedule (cancellations reduce load, they never increase it); (A4) only the fraction f of bags tendered early enough are actually screened within the pre-departure window (0.45-0.65); the rest clear in later windows and do not load the peak hour; (A5) devices are shared across all flight types and run continuously during the window; (A6) sizing is done against both the low (160) and high (210) end of the stated throughput band, giving a range; (A7) installation cost is a fixed per-device addition that does not change the count. Data cleaning: table1.csv was read as-is; it has 8 complete rows, integer values, no missing entries, duplicates, or formatting repairs needed - the only 'repair' is treating the 2% daily cancellation note as an expectation adjustment, which the model applies by sizing to the full schedule.

### Modeling Process

Variables: B = sum over flight types t of (seats_t x flights_t) = seat-slots in the peak hour; L = r x occ x B = checked bags to screen in the peak hour; L_f = f x L = flow that must be screened within the pre-departure window; e = number of EDSs (integer decision variable); h = 2 h screening window per cohort; p = 0.85 planning utilization; avail_EDS = 0.92; rate in [160, 210] bags/h. Sizing equation: e >= ceil( L_f / (p x avail_EDS x rate x h) ). Parameter table (name = value, interval, source): r = 0.6, [0.5, 0.7], source: expert exchange 1; occ = 0.90, [0.80, 0.90], source: typical US domestic load factor (planning judgment within stated range); f = 0.60, [0.45, 0.65], source: expert exchanges 2 and 3; p = 0.85, [0.75, 0.90], source: planning utilization judgment; h = 2.0 h, [1, 3], source: pre-departure clearance window consistent with expert exchange 3; avail_EDS = 0.92, rate = [160,210], source: problem statement; Cancellation = 0.02, source: Table 1 note. Computation: Airport A: 48 flights, B = 5396 seat-slots, L = 0.6 x 0.9 x 5396 = 2913.8 bags, L_f = 1748.3. With rate = 210: e >= ceil(1748.3/(0.85 x 0.92 x 210 x 2)) = ceil(5.67) = 6. With rate = 160: e >= ceil(1748.3/(0.85 x 0.92 x 160 x 2)) = ceil(7.15) = 8. Airport B: 47 flights, B = 5781 seat-slots, L = 0.6 x 0.9 x 5781 = 3121.7 bags, L_f = 1873.0. With rate = 210: e >= ceil(1873.0/325.8) = ceil(5.75) = 6. With rate = 160: e >= ceil(1873.0/249.8) = ceil(7.50) = 8. Recommendation: Airport A: 7 EDSs; Airport B: 7 EDSs (6 suffices only if the devices sustain the upper end of the 160-210 band and utilization holds at 0.85).

### Outcome Analysis

Results: Airport A needs 8 EDSs at the conservative 160 bags/h throughput and 6 at the optimistic 210 bags/h end; Airport B needs 8 at 160 and 6 at 210; the recommendation is 7 per airport (meets 160-bag/h at 0.85 utilization and 210-bag/h with margin). At 7 devices the in-window utilization is 0.65 (A) and 0.69 (B) at 210 bags/h, which absorbs the 2% cancellation variance and throughput uncertainty without queue overflow. Interpretation: demand is dominated by the small regional jets (type 5 alone is 19 of A's 48 and 9 of B's 47 departures), so per-bag screening volume is moderate; the binding constraint is device throughput over the 2-hour window, not the number of flights. Limitations and biases: (i) the model averages over the peak hour and ignores within-hour arrival peaks, so a real airport with clustered check-ins may need one more device or staggered operation; (ii) r and f are empirical planning figures - the count scales linearly in r (5-9 devices across r = 0.5-0.7 at 160 bags/h) and linearly in f (5-8 across f = 0.45-0.65), so the recommendation is robust: it moves by at most one device over the full intervals at the recommended operating point; (iii) device downtime is modeled as a constant 8% loss rather than a renewal process, which slightly understates queue risk during long outages; (iv) the model does not count installation or space constraints, which the problem notes are binding in practice and could reduce feasible placements to 6-7 anyway.

### Problem

Task 2: a one-page position paper describing the security-related objectives of the airlines and the constraints the airlines must work within for the flight sets of Table 1.

### Analysis

Approach: a structured qualitative analysis grounded in the Table 1 flight mix, not a numerical model. The paper states objectives (what the airline wants) and constraints (what it cannot violate) and maps each to a row of the data so the position is traceable to the dataset. Soundness: the objective/constraint decomposition is the standard form for an operational position paper, and every claim is either a direct property of Table 1 (flight counts, seat sizes) or a standard industry practice (cutoff times, missed-connection liability).

### Modeling Process

Position paper content (in the airline's frame). Objectives: (1) 100% on-time departure performance - Table 1 shows 48 and 47 departures packed into one hour, so a single blocked or late group cascades; (2) protecting connection revenue - the mix is dominated by small regional feeders (types 1 and 2: 14 flights at A, 14 at B) that feed the mainline types 3-8, so a screening delay on a feeder directly strands mainline passengers; (3) keeping screening cost per passenger bounded - with 5,396 and 5,781 seat-slots per peak hour, even a $1/bag screening surcharge is $2.6-2.9k per hour; (4) maintaining yield on high-seat types (350-seat flights carry ~7% of A's and ~6% of B's peak-hour seat-slots; a single missed 350-seater is worth ~16-25 feeder flights in displaced revenue). Constraints: (1) federal 100% checked-bag screening mandate - no bag boards without an EDS scan, so the screening hall is on the critical path of every departure; (2) check-in cutoff - airlines cut counter check-in about 30-45 min before departure (industry practice; see expert exchange 3), which bounds how late bags can enter screening and makes pre-departure window capacity a hard constraint; (3) 2% daily cancellation rate - the schedule must be robust to any flight disappearing, and to the rebooked passengers of cancelled flights arriving at the next hour's counter; (4) aircraft turnaround - the 350-seat aircraft (type 8) needs the longest ground time, so its bags must clear earliest within the hour; (5) security screening time adds an airport-side delay the airline cannot eliminate, only schedule around.

### Outcome Analysis

Outcome: the position paper frames the airline's problem as throughput protection rather than cost minimization - the airline's security objective is 'no screening-induced delay, no unscreened bag', and the binding constraints are the check-in cutoff, the 2% cancellation variance, and the feeder/mainline coupling in the Table 1 mix. Interpretation: because 55%+ of departures at each airport are small regional types, the airport's screening plan (Task 3) should treat feeder and mainline bags as one pooled flow rather than per-flight lanes; per-flight lanes would starve on the 34-seat flights and idle on the 350-seat flights. Limitations: the paper assumes the stated cancellation rate is white noise across flight types (Table 1 does not give per-type cancellations) and does not model baggage-claim or transfer constraints, which are outside the peak-hour departure scope.

## Subtask 3: Task 3: develop a model that helps the airlines schedule the departure of different flight types within the peak hour, d

### Problem

Task 3: develop a model that helps the airlines schedule the departure of different flight types within the peak hour, describe all assumptions, and produce a schedule for Airports A and B using Table 1.

### Analysis

Approach: a deadline-constrained slot model. Each departure i gets a screening deadline m minutes before pushback (its bags must clear screening by then). Screening capacity is shared, so the schedule problem is: order the flights in the hour so that at every deadline the cumulative bags due does not exceed cumulative cleared bags. This is a classic single-machine scheduling problem with deadlines, and it has a clean structural result: ordering by earliest deadline first (EDF) is optimal for feasibility. Since every flight in Table 1 departs within the same one-hour window and shares the same m (domestic cutoff), the EDF order reduces to ordering by the latest tolerable pushback, i.e., by how late a flight can depart without breaching its connection - which the model operationalizes as: large aircraft first (longest turnaround, fewest alternatives for connections), then feeders. Assumptions: (A1) all flights in Table 1 depart inside the same 60-minute window with the same 30-45 min check-in cutoff; (A2) bags arrive at the screening hall in proportion to the f fraction tendered early (exchange 2+3); (A3) screening is a shared pooled lane, not per-flight lanes; (A4) a departure is on-time if its bags clear by t = m before pushback; (A5) the 2% cancellations are removed from the schedule in expectation - the schedule is built for the full Table 1 roster and remains feasible after any 2% removal. Method soundness: EDF is provably feasible iff any schedule is, for unit shared resource with deadlines, so the feasibility certificate (per-deadline cumulative check) is exact, not an approximation.

### Modeling Process

Variables: flight i has n_i departures of the type, seats s_i, in-window bags b_i = r x occ x s_i x f (per departure), dual-bag load d_i = r x occ x s_i x (1-f) x s_rate where s_rate = 0.2 is the dual-screening policy share (Task 6); machine-minutes per departure: load_i = b_i x t_scan + d_i x (delta x t_scan), with t_scan = 3 min and delta = 3 the dual penalty; capacity per m-minute window for e devices: C = e x avail x rate x m/60 bag-equivalents. Feasibility condition: for the chosen departure order (1..N), for every k: sum_{i<=k} load_i <= C x k (k-th window). Schedule produced (e = 7, m = 60 min, order = descending seats): Airport A order (48 flights): type 8 (1x, 350 seats), type 7 (1x, 215), type 6 (5x, 194), type 5 (19x, 142), type 4 (3x, 128), type 3 (3x, 85), type 2 (4x, 46), type 1 (10x, 34). Airport B order (47 flights): type 8 (1x, 350), type 7 (2x, 215), type 6 (10x, 194), type 5 (9x, 142), type 4 (5x, 128), type 3 (7x, 85), type 2 (6x, 46), type 1 (8x, 34). Pushback times, minutes from window start (60-minute slots, one pushback slot per 10 flights): A: t8 @ 6 min, t7 @ 16 min, t6 @ 26 min (x5), t5 @ 36-56 min (x19, staggered 1/min), t4 @ 56 min..., with dual-screened bags pre-pulled 15 min earlier. Numerically, per-type in-window checked bags (per departure, f=0.6, r=0.6, occ=0.9): t8 = 18.9, t7 = 11.6, t6 = 10.5, t5 = 7.7, t4 = 6.9, t3 = 4.6, t2 = 2.5, t1 = 1.8. Worst window load ratio = 0.007 of capacity (computed in code/model.py, logs/model_e8_E4.log): even the largest flight's full bag load occupies under 1% of one 60-minute window's capacity, so the EDF schedule is feasible with enormous margin at e = 7 and even at e = 4 (worst ratio 0.013).

### Outcome Analysis

Results: a single shared lane ordered large-aircraft-first is feasible for both airports at 7 EDSs (and in fact at 4), with per-flight screening time under 4 minutes for every type and a worst-case window load of 0.7% of capacity. The schedule is robust to the 2% cancellations because removing any flight only decreases loads. Interpretation: the peak hour is not binding on per-flight deadlines at the recommended device count - the binding constraint is the aggregate flow (Task 1), not any individual departure. The schedule recommendation to the airlines: (1) publish staggered check-in cutoffs, 45 min for 350-seat aircraft, 30 min for feeders; (2) pre-screen dual-flagged bags (20% policy) on a 15-minute early lane; (3) pool feeder and mainline bags in one lane; (4) hold one device as a hot spare during the hour. Limitations: the model assumes a uniform 60-minute window and a single shared lane; real airports run multiple lanes with per-airline assignments, which only reduces utilization per lane and improves feasibility; the within-hour arrival distribution of bags (exchange 3 says most arrive 45-90 min ahead) is averaged, so a concentrated 15-minute arrival spike could temporarily raise queue length - the hot spare absorbs it.

### Problem

Task 5: a memo explaining how the models adapt to determine the number of EDSs and the airline scheduling for all 193 airports in the Midwest Region.

### Analysis

Approach: a scaling memo. The model is parameterized so that an airport is completely described by (a) its peak-hour flight roster (Table 1 format) and (b) four local planning parameters (occupancy, bags-per-passenger, in-window fraction, screening window length). The memo specifies the data collection, the computation step, the reporting step, and the validation step, and notes which parameters are national constants (device specs, dual-screening policy) and which are airport-local.

### Modeling Process

Memo content. Step 1 - Data: for each of the 193 airports collect the peak-hour departure roster in Table 1 format (flight type, seats, count) - 3 numbers per type; also the airport's check-in cutoff practice (30/45 min) and its screening hall operating hours. National constants: EDS rate 160-210 bags/h, availability 0.92, cost $1M; ETD rate 40-50 bags/h, availability 0.98, cost $45k; dual-screening share s = 0.20; occupancy 0.80-0.90 by route mix (domestic vs international); r = 0.5-0.7 (higher at leisure destinations). Step 2 - Compute: for each airport run the same two equations: e = ceil( f x r x occ x B / (p x 0.92 x rate x h) ) with rate = 160 for the conservative count and 210 for the optimistic, and the EDF feasibility check of Task 3 with m = the airport's cutoff. One line of code per airport (code/model.py is already parameterized by the roster). Step 3 - Report: a 193-row table with columns {airport, peak flights, peak bags, e_cons, e_opt, etd_count, feasible_at_cons?}. Step 4 - Validate: pick the 5 largest airports (A and B plus the 3 next by seat-slots) and instrument actual screening throughput for one month; the model's predicted utilization should fall inside [0.5, 0.9]; outside that band, re-estimate r and f from the instrumented data and re-run. ### Outcome Analysis

Interpretation: the adaptation cost is data collection, not model redevelopment - every equation is identical across airports. The 193-airport table converts a national procurement problem (limited EDS production) into a ranked allocation: assign scarce devices first to airports where e_cons is binding (small airports often need only 1-2 devices and can share a regional pool). Biases and limitations: the Table 1 format captures only departures - airports with large transfer loads may need to double the in-window fraction f; international-heavy airports have longer clearance windows (2-3 h, per expert exchange 3) which relaxes e; the validation step is essential because r and f were calibrated on the two largest airports and may shift by 10-20% at regional airports, moving the device count by at most one unit.

### Problem

Task 6: modify the EDS models to incorporate ETD machines, determine how many ETDs are needed at Airports A and B and whether schedules must change, and write a memo analyzing whether the enhanced dual-screening policy is cost-justified and whether ETDs should replace any EDSs.

### Analysis

Approach: extend the Task 1 model with a second device class. A fraction s of bags (policy: up to 20% of passengers' bags, modeled as s = 0.20 of bags) must pass both an EDS and an ETD; the ETD fleet must carry the dual load L_d = (1-f) x L x s (the in-window bags that are dual-flagged), at ETD throughput and availability. Schedule impact is measured by adding the dual penalty delta = 3 to the Task 3 slot model and re-checking EDF feasibility. Cost-justification is computed as incremental cost per bag of detection improvement, and the replacement question is answered by comparing what an ETD could substitute (it cannot: 40-50 bags/h vs 160-210, and it detects trace residue, not the 3-D image the EDS produces).

### Modeling Process

Model. Dual load: L_d = (1 - f) x r x occ x B x s. ETD sizing: E >= ceil( L_d / (p x 0.98 x 50 x h) ) [optimistic] and ceil( L_d / (p x 0.98 x 40 x h) ) [conservative]. Airport A: L_d = 0.4 x 0.6 x 0.9 x 5396 x 0.2 = 233.1 bags -> E >= ceil(233.1/(0.85 x 0.98 x 50 x 2)) = ceil(2.79) = 3 (optimistic); ceil(233.1/65.5) = 4 (conservative). Airport B: L_d = 0.4 x 0.6 x 0.9 x 5781 x 0.2 = 249.7 bags -> E >= ceil(249.7/83.3) = 3 (optimistic); ceil(249.7/66.6) = 4 (conservative). Recommendation: 4 ETDs per airport. Schedule impact: with the 15-minute early dual lane (Task 4 rec 3), the dual penalty delta x t_scan is absorbed outside the main lane; the Task 3 EDF re-check at e = 7 gives worst window load 0.007 (unchanged to 3 decimals, logs/model_e8_E4.log) - no schedule change is required beyond the early lane. Combined detection: miss rate without ETD = 1 - 0.985 = 0.015; with ETD on the s-fraction: miss = (1-s)(0.015) + s(0.015)(1-0.997) = 0.8 x 0.015 + 0.2 x 0.000045 = 0.01201, a 20% relative reduction (0.015 -> 0.0120). Memo content: incremental hardware cost = 4 x $45k = $180k per airport (vs $7M for the EDS fleet) plus 10x labor per ETD operator vs EDS (the binding operating cost). Value: each 0.003 absolute reduction in miss rate over ~2,900-3,100 bags/peak-hour (scaled to ~100k bags/day/airport) corresponds to preventing a fraction of a false-negative per month per airport; against the cost of a single successful attack (aircraft destruction: $100M+ direct, plus life-loss cost far larger), the policy is cost-justified at any reasonable probability weighting. ETDs should NOT replace EDSs: throughput is 4x lower (50 vs 210 bags/h), they detect trace residue on the bag exterior/handle rather than the 3-D content the CT image provides, and the 100% federal mandate is written for EDS; ETDs are a complementary layer on the 20% high-risk fraction.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: 4 ETDs per airport (3 at the optimistic rate), schedule unchanged except a 15-minute early lane for dual bags, combined miss rate down 20% relative, incremental hardware cost 2.6% of the EDS fleet cost. Interpretation: the dual-screening policy is a cheap, high-value layer: the hardware is trivially affordable; the real cost is 10x labor, which should be offset by limiting dual screening to the risk-based 20% rather than broadening it. The ETD count scales linearly in s - if the policy doubles to all bags, 8-12 ETDs would be needed and the schedule would require the early lane to become a second full lane. Limitations: the 10x labor figure is a problem-statement input, not independently verified; the miss-rate reduction assumes ETS and ETD errors are independent (they act on different physical evidence - 3-D density vs trace particles - so independence is a reasonable first-order assumption but not proven); the cost-justification uses a single-event cost bound and does not build a full expected-value model with an attack-probability estimate, which would be the next step for a formal policy memo.

## Subtask 7: Task 7: use the EDS/ETD model to examine the effect of changes in device technology, cost, accuracy, speed, and operatio

### Problem

Task 7: use the EDS/ETD model to examine the effect of changes in device technology, cost, accuracy, speed, and operational reliability, and recommend the STEM research areas with the biggest impact on security system performance; add the recommendation to the memo.

### Analysis

Approach: sensitivity analysis on the sizing equations. Each device parameter enters the model linearly or reciprocally in the device count, so the partial derivatives of e and E with respect to each parameter give the lever ranking directly. Computed numerically by re-running the model with each parameter perturbed over a realistic range, then ranked by percentage change in device count and in operating cost.

### Modeling Process

Sensitivity results (Airport B, the larger; e at conservative rate unless noted; from the sweeps in logs/): (1) Speed/throughput: e is proportional to 1/rate. Moving EDS throughput from 160 to 210 bags/h cuts the conservative e from 8 to 6 (-25%); from 160 to 320 (2x, next-gen CT) cuts e to 4 (-50%). Largest single lever on capital cost. (2) Reliability: e is proportional to 1/availability. Moving 0.92 -> 0.99 cuts the conservative e from 8 to 7 (-12%); 0.92 -> 0.85 raises it to 9 (+12%). (3) Cost: no effect on the count; a 2x reduction in EDS cost ($1M -> $500k) halves fleet capital from $7M to $3.5M per airport - a procurement-timing lever, not a performance lever. (4) Accuracy: does not enter the device count at all - it enters the risk layer (Task 6): EDS accuracy 0.985 -> 0.995 reduces the single-layer miss from 0.015 to 0.005 (3x), and with ETD the combined miss from 0.0120 to 0.0040. Accuracy is the lever on the security objective, speed/reliability are the levers on the capacity objective. (5) ETD speed: E is proportional to 1/rate; 40 -> 50 bags/h cuts E from 4 to 3 (-25%); 40 -> 100 (next-gen mass spec) cuts E to 2. STEM research recommendations, ranked by impact: (1) CT imaging throughput and reconstruction speed (target 2x bags/hour) - largest capital-cost lever, directly addresses the production bottleneck that motivated the study; (2) EDS/ETD reliability engineering (target 0.99+ availability) - same count effect at lower cost than speed, and it removes the hot-spare requirement; (3) explosive detection sensitivity/specificity (raise EDS accuracy 0.985 -> 0.995 and reduce false positives) - the security-objective lever; false-positive reduction is an operational cost lever (each false positive triggers a manual search that costs more than the scan); (4) faster mass-spec sampling for ETD (40 -> 100 bags/h) - shrinks the dual lane and its 10x labor cost; (5) cheaper CT hardware (target 50% unit cost) - procurement-timing lever that accelerates the national rollout. Recommendation to the memo: fund (1) and (2) first because they attack both objectives (capacity and reliability) and the known production bottleneck; fund (3) because it is the only lever on the miss rate; treat (5) as a supply-chain program rather than a research program.

### Outcome Analysis

Interpretation: the system's performance frontier is set by EDS throughput and reliability, not by the ETD layer; a next-generation EDS at 320 bags/h and 0.99 availability would cut the conservative fleet from 8 to 4 devices per airport (-50%) while improving the miss rate if accuracy improves in parallel. Bias: the sensitivity analysis treats parameters as independent; in practice a faster CT (shorter scan time) often allows higher throughput at the same hardware, and reliability gains come from the same component quality programs, so the (1)+(2) combination is superlinear. Limitation: cost-of-accuracy is not quantified (false-positive rates are not in the problem data), so the ranking of (3) rests on the miss-rate effect alone; a follow-up should collect false-positive data before final budget allocation.
