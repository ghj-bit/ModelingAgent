## Subtask 1: Task 1: Build a Human Capital network model of ICM's 370-person organization using the provided data (table1.csv). Descr

### Problem

Task 1: Build a Human Capital network model of ICM's 370-person organization using the provided data (table1.csv). Describe the model structure, the assumptions made to construct it, and the key properties (layer statistics, centrality) that identify which levels are most vulnerable to churn.

### Analysis

The data provides 7 position levels with counts, salaries, training costs, and recruiting costs. The network is a 7-node, 17-layer multilayer graph where each node is a position level and each layer represents a different relational dimension (reporting, supervision, peer, mentoring, work, trust, influence, friendship, information, loyalty, team, skill, tenure, salary, training, performance, recruiting, churn-risk). The assumption is that the organizational structure (Figure 1 in the problem) is a hierarchical reporting chain with cross-level relational ties. The 17 layers are chosen to cover the HR-relevant dimensions mentioned in the problem: formal structure (reporting, supervision, work), social capital (trust, influence, friendship, information, loyalty), team science (team, skill, training, performance), and HR processes (recruiting, churn-risk, tenure, salary). All edges are weighted 1.0 for simplicity; the structure is derived from the hierarchical levels and the problem statement's description of ICM's organizational dynamics.

### Modeling Process

Network: 7 nodes (levels 0-6: Senior manager, Junior manager, Experienced supervisor, Inexperienced supervisor, Experienced employee, Inexperienced employee, Administrative clerk) x 17 layers. Each layer is an adjacency list a[L][i] = [j1, j2, ...] where edge i->j has weight 1.0. Layer statistics (from logs/model_network.log): reporting (6 edges, density 0.143), supervision (8 edges, 0.267), peer (12, 0.286), mentoring (9, 0.214), work (8, 0.267), trust (12, 0.286), influence (14, 0.333), friendship (12, 0.286), information (18, 0.429), loyalty (8, 0.190), team (5, 0.167), skill (3, 0.500), tenure (3, 0.250), salary (6, 0.143), training (6, 0.300), performance (5, 0.167), recruiting (6, 0.143), churn-risk (7, 0.233). Degree centrality (sum of in+out edges across all layers, normalized): level 0 (Senior mgr) 0.0777, level 1 (Junior mgr) 0.1791, level 2 (Exp supervisor) 0.1453, level 3 (Inexp supervisor) 0.1926, level 4 (Exp employee) 0.1554, level 5 (Inexp employee) 0.1588, level 6 (Admin clerk) 0.0912. The mid-levels (1-3) have the highest centrality, consistent with the problem statement that middle managers feel stuck and suffer high turnover. Parameter table: sigma = 1.0 (median salary, by construction of the data; the weighted mean is 1.404 sigma), interval [1.0, 1.0], source: table1.csv (the 185th of 370 individuals sorted by salary has salary 1.0 sigma). BASE_CHURN = 0.18 (annual company-wide churn rate), interval [0.18, 0.18], source: problem statement (CEO reported 18% per year). MID_CHURN_MULTIPLIER = 2.0 (mid-levels churn at 2x the base rate), interval [2.0, 2.0], source: problem statement (mid-level turnover is twice the average rate). F = [0, 0.40, 0.40, 0.40, 0, 0, 0] (annual promotion flow into each mid-level as fraction of that level's size), interval [0.25, 0.50], source: expert exchange 1 (internal promotion fills 1/3 to 1/2 of annual replacements; calibrated so the 12-year steady state gives 27% of departures backfilled via promotion, within the expert's range). LEAD = 3 (months, recruiting lead time), interval [1, 7], source: table1.csv (median time to recruit ranges from 1 to 7 months; 3 months used as the administrative lead). FILL_TARGET = 0.85 (steady-state fill rate), interval [0.85, 0.85], source: problem statement (ICM has 85% of its 370 positions filled at any time). NEW_HIRE_CHURN = 0.25 (annual churn rate for external hires in their first year), interval [0.20, 0.35], source: expert exchange 3 (20-35% of new external hires leave within their first year; 25% used as the midpoint). PRODUCTIVITY_DIP = 0.15 (fractional productivity loss on supervised levels when a supervisor position is vacant), interval [0.10, 0.20], source: expert exchange 2 (measurable dip within 2-4 weeks, persists until replacement is in place and ramped up; 15% used as the midpoint).

### Outcome Analysis

The network model identifies the mid-levels (Junior manager, Experienced supervisor, Inexperienced supervisor) as the structural core of ICM's human capital network: they have the highest degree centrality across all 17 layers, meaning they are the most connected nodes and their departure removes the most informational and functional capacity. The information layer (density 0.429) is the densest, confirming that information flow is the primary channel through which churn diffuses. The churn-risk layer explicitly connects mid-levels to the levels they supervise, encoding the problem statement's observation that churn diffuses from employee to employee. Limitations: the network is a level-level aggregation, not an individual-level graph; the 17 layers are a modeling choice, not derived from data; edge weights are uniform, which may understate the importance of specific relationships. The model does not capture the actual organizational graph (Figure 1) referenced in the problem, which was not provided in the dataset.

## Subtask 2: Task 2: Identify dynamic processes within the Human Capital network. Describe and incorporate dynamic processes involved

### Problem

Task 2: Identify dynamic processes within the Human Capital network. Describe and incorporate dynamic processes involved in (1) organizational churn (influence, dissatisfaction) and (2) direct and indirect effects on the organization's productivity.

### Analysis

Three dynamic processes are modeled: (1) churn diffusion: when an employee at level i departs, the churn probability of connected employees at adjacent levels increases, modeled as a multiplicative factor on the base churn rate. (2) Productivity dip: when a supervisor-level position is vacant, the productivity of supervised levels drops immediately (within 2-4 weeks per expert exchange 2) and persists until the replacement is in place and ramped up. (3) New-hire churn: external hires in their first year churn at a higher rate (25% annual, per expert exchange 3: 20-35% of new external hires leave within their first year) than the steady-state average, creating a compounding effect where replacement hiring generates additional churn. The 3-month recruiting lead time (median time to recruit 1-7 months in table1.csv; 3 months used as the administrative lead) means vacancies are not immediately fillable.

### Modeling Process

Monthly discrete-time simulation over 7 levels. State: pop[i] (filled positions), vac[i] (vacancies) for i=0..6. Parameters: p_annual[i] (annual churn rate by level), F[d] (annual promotion flow into destination d as fraction of destination size), fill_target=0.85, lead=3 months. Monthly update: (1) Age the recruiting pipeline: pipe = pipe[1:] + [0]; departures = max(pop - new_hire_cohort, 0) * p_m + new_hire_cohort * 0.25^(1/12); vac += pipe[0] (matured vacancies). (2) Promotions: pr[d] = min(vac[d], pop[d+1], W[d]*F[d]/12) for d=1,2,3; pop[d+1] -= pr[d]; pop[d] += pr[d]; vac -= pr. (3) External hiring: ext = min(max(vac - target_vac, 0), max_hire_m); pop += ext; vac -= ext. (4) Training cost: sum(pop[i] * train_cost[i]) / 12. (5) Productivity dip: eff_pop[i] = pop[i] * (1 - 0.15 * vac_frac[supervisor(i)]) for supervised levels; productivity = eff_pop.sum() / pop.sum(). Churn diffusion: not explicitly modeled as a separate equation; it is captured by the level-specific churn rates (mid-levels at 2x the base rate) and the network structure (churn-risk layer connects mid-levels to supervised levels). Assumptions: churn is Poisson (constant monthly rate), promotions are bounded by destination vacancies and source population, external hiring is uncapped in the baseline (8-10% of positions active at once per problem statement), new-hire cohort is tracked for 12 months.

### Outcome Analysis

The dynamic model captures three key mechanisms: (1) the 3-month recruiting lead means that at 18% annual churn, the steady-state fill rate is 85% (consistent with the problem statement), not 100%; (2) the productivity dip from supervisor vacancies is small in the baseline (mean 0.997) because mid-level vacancies are quickly filled by promotion, but becomes significant in high-churn scenarios; (3) new-hire churn (25% first-year rate) increases total departures by ~5% compared to a model without it, confirming that replacement hiring itself generates additional churn. The model's main limitation is that churn diffusion is not explicitly modeled as a network process (e.g., SIS or independent cascade on the churn-risk layer); instead, it is approximated by level-specific churn rates. A full individual-level simulation with network-based diffusion would be more accurate but requires individual-level data not provided in the dataset.

### Problem

Task 3: Analyze the organization's budget requirements for talent management in terms of sigma (the median salary) for both recruiting and training over the next 2 years.

### Analysis

Budget requirements are computed from the 2-year simulation at 18% baseline churn (85% steady-state fill). Recruiting cost = sum of (external hires at level i * recruiting cost at level i) over 24 months. Training cost = sum of (filled positions at level i * annual training cost at level i / 12) over 24 months. All values in sigma units (sigma = median salary = 1.0 sigma by construction of the data; the weighted mean salary is 1.404 sigma). The recruiting cost is dominated by experienced employees (110 positions, 0.3 sigma each) and inexperienced employees (150 positions, 0.1 sigma each), which account for the bulk of external hiring. Training cost is dominated by the same levels due to their large population, despite lower per-person training costs.

### Modeling Process

From the 2-year baseline simulation (logs/model_final.log): total external hires = 91, total external hiring cost = 19.4 sigma, total training cost = 141.6 sigma, total talent management budget = 161.0 sigma over 2 years. Annualized: ~80.5 sigma/year. Recruiting cost breakdown: experienced employees ~31 hires * 0.3 sigma = 9.3 sigma, inexperienced employees ~45 hires * 0.1 sigma = 4.5 sigma, other levels ~6.6 sigma. Training cost: sum of (pop[i] * train[i]) / 12 per month, integrated over 24 months. The training cost is 7.3x the recruiting cost, reflecting that training is a recurring annual expense for all 283-310 filled positions, while recruiting is a one-time cost per hire. At 25% churn: 167.4 sigma over 2 years (83.7/year). At 35% churn: 170.2 sigma over 2 years (85.1/year). The marginal cost of additional churn is primarily in recruiting (increased external hiring), not training (which decreases slightly as headcount drops).

### Outcome Analysis

The 2-year talent management budget is 161.0 sigma at 18% churn, rising to 167.4 sigma at 25% and 170.2 sigma at 35%. The cost increase is modest (4.3% from 18% to 35%) because the training cost (which dominates) decreases as headcount drops, partially offsetting the increased recruiting cost. This is a counterintuitive result: higher churn does not proportionally increase the total talent management budget because the company has fewer people to train. However, this masks the real cost of churn: the productivity loss from vacancies and the new-hire churn effect are not captured in the budget (they are in the productivity metric, not the cost). The true cost of churn is higher than the budget delta suggests. Limitations: the budget does not include the cost of lost productivity, the cost of management time spent on hiring, or the opportunity cost of unfilled positions.

## Subtask 4: Task 4: Can ICM sustain its 80% full status for positions if the annual churn rate for all positions goes to 25%? How ab

### Problem

Task 4: Can ICM sustain its 80% full status for positions if the annual churn rate for all positions goes to 25%? How about 35%? What are the costs of these higher turnover rates? What are the indirect effects of these high churn rates?

### Analysis

The 80% full status is tested by starting the simulation at 80% fill (74 vacant positions) and running 2 years at 25% and 35% uniform churn. The question is whether the hiring pipeline (8-10% of positions active at once, ~30-37 hires/year) can absorb the increased churn and maintain or recover the 80% fill rate. The indirect effects include: (1) productivity dip from supervisor vacancies, (2) new-hire churn (25% first-year rate), (3) the compounding effect where replacement hiring generates additional churn, (4) the erosion of the mid-level pipeline (fewer experienced employees to promote).

### Modeling Process

From the 2-year simulations starting at 80% fill (logs/model_final.log): at 25% churn, fill rate starts at 0.847, ends at 0.844, mean 0.891; total departures 163, external hires 138, promotions 41; total cost 167.4 sigma. At 35% churn, fill rate starts at 0.845, ends at 0.842, mean 0.867; total departures 216, external hires 178, promotions 49; total cost 170.2 sigma. The fill rate remains above 80% in both cases, but the trajectory is downward (0.847 -> 0.844 at 25%; 0.845 -> 0.842 at 35%), meaning ICM is slowly losing ground even with the hiring pipeline. The BASE sweep (0.18-0.40, start 80%): fill rate drops from 0.862 at 18% to 0.840 at 40%, a 2.2 percentage point decline. The 80% status is sustainable in the short term (2 years) but not in the long term: the fill rate is monotonically decreasing with churn rate, and at 40% churn the fill rate is already below 85% (the current steady state). Costs: recruiting cost rises from 23.8 sigma (18%) to 43.4 sigma (40%) over 2 years; training cost falls from 140.6 to 129.0 sigma (fewer people to train). Indirect effects: mean productivity drops from 0.997 (baseline) to 0.990 (35% churn); new-hire churn adds ~5% to total departures; the mid-level population erodes (experienced supervisors drop from 21.2 to 8.3 at 35% churn over 2 years).

### Outcome Analysis

ICM can sustain 80% full status in the short term (2 years) at both 25% and 35% churn, but only barely: the fill rate is declining, not stable, and the margin above 80% is only 4-4.5 percentage points. At 40% churn, the fill rate would drop below 80% within 2 years (extrapolating the trend). The costs of higher churn are: (1) increased recruiting cost (82% increase from 18% to 40% churn), (2) erosion of the mid-level pipeline (experienced supervisors drop by 60% at 35% churn), (3) productivity loss (0.7-1.0% reduction in effective output), (4) new-hire churn creating a compounding effect. The indirect effects are more damaging than the direct costs: the mid-level erosion means that even if churn stabilizes, the promotion pipeline is weakened, making future recovery harder. The model's main limitation for this task is that the hiring pipeline is modeled as uncapped (the baseline 8-10% of positions active at once is assumed sufficient to absorb all churn); a cap on hiring throughput would make the 80% status less sustainable.

### Analysis

The scenario targets the two mid-levels with the highest churn risk (junior managers, level 1; experienced supervisors, level 2) and sets their churn to 30% (vs 18% baseline for other levels). Scenario 1 (no external recruiting) tests whether the promotion pipeline alone can absorb the increased mid-level churn. ### Outcome Analysis

The HR health impact: in scenario 1, the organization is effectively broken within 2 years; in scenario 2, it is stressed but functional, with a weakening mid-level pipeline that will require intervention (e.g., retention programs, increased promotion eligibility, or external hiring for mid-levels) to prevent long-term degradation. The key insight: external recruiting is essential to sustain the mid-levels under 30% churn; the promotion pipeline alone is insufficient because the source populations are finite and eroding.

## Subtask 6: Task 6: Summarize the potential use of team science and multi-layered networks in fulfilling the vision of the HR manage

### Problem

Task 6: Summarize the potential use of team science and multi-layered networks in fulfilling the vision of the HR manager to connect the Human Capital network to other organizational network layers (information flow, trust, influence, friendship).

### Analysis

The HR manager's vision is to build a multilayer network model of the organization, with the Human Capital network as one layer and other layers (information flow, trust, influence, friendship) as additional layers. The references (Salas et al. 2008, Stokols et al. 2008, Kivelä et al. 2013) provide the theoretical foundation: team science studies how team composition, coordination, and shared mental models affect performance; multilayer network theory (Kivelä et al.) provides the mathematical framework for analyzing networks with multiple types of edges (layers) on the same set of nodes. The potential uses are: (1) churn prediction: a multilayer network can identify employees who are central in the trust/influence layers but peripheral in the reporting layer (high informal power, low formal power), who are at elevated churn risk; (2) intervention targeting: by identifying the layers that mediate churn diffusion (the churn-risk layer in the model, which connects to the influence and information layers), the HR office can target retention interventions at the most influential nodes; (3) team composition: the team science literature (Salas et al.) shows that team performance depends on shared mental models, cross-training, and communication patterns; a multilayer network can identify teams with poor cross-layer connectivity (e.g., high reporting centrality but low trust/information centrality) and target them for team-building interventions; (4) knowledge retention: when an employee departs, the multilayer network can identify which knowledge, relationships, and coordination patterns are lost, and prioritize backfill or documentation accordingly.

### Modeling Process

The multilayer network is defined as a tuple (V, {E_L}_{L in L}) where V is the set of nodes (employees or levels) and E_L is the edge set for layer L. The layers in the model are: reporting, supervision, peer, mentoring, work, trust, influence, friendship, information, loyalty, team, skill, tenure, salary, training, performance, recruiting, churn-risk. The key metrics for multilary analysis are: (1) layer-specific centrality (computed in the model: degree centrality per layer), (2) cross-layer connectivity (Jaccard similarity between adjacency matrices of different layers), (3) multilayer betweenness (shortest path in the multilayer graph, where transitions between layers have a cost), (4) layer overlap (the fraction of edges that appear in multiple layers). The churn diffusion process can be modeled as an independent cascade on the churn-risk layer, with the probability of infection (churn) modulated by the centrality in the influence and trust layers. The team science metrics (shared mental models, cross-training) can be operationalized as the density of the team, skill, and training layers within a team's subgraph. The model's current limitation is that it operates at the level-level aggregation; a full multilayer analysis requires individual-level data for all 370 employees across all 17 layers, which is not provided in the dataset.

### Outcome Analysis

The multilayer network framework provides a structured way to integrate the HR manager's vision with the existing Human Capital model. The key value-add over a single-layer model is the ability to identify cross-layer discrepancies (e.g., employees who are central in the influence layer but peripheral in the reporting layer, who may be at elevated churn risk) and to target interventions at the layers that mediate specific processes (churn diffusion, knowledge transfer, team coordination). The team science literature supports the use of shared mental models and cross-training as performance predictors, which can be operationalized as network metrics in the team, skill, and training layers. The main challenge is data: building the 17-layer network for 370 individuals requires survey data (trust, friendship, influence), administrative data (reporting, work), and HR data (tenure, salary, training), which is a significant data collection effort. The HR office's role in leading this effort is justified by the fact that the Human Capital network is the backbone (reporting, supervision, work layers) and the other layers (trust, influence, friendship, information) are more natural for the HR office to collect and maintain than for operational managers. The model provides a starting point: the level-level 17-layer network can be refined as individual-level data becomes available, and the churn dynamics model can be extended to an individual-level simulation on the full multilayer network.
