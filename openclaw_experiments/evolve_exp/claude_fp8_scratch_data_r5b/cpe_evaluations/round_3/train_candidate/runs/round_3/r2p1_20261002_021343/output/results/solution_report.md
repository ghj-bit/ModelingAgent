## Subtask 1: Subproblem 1: Explain the day-to-day variation in the number of Wordle scores reported on Twitter, build a predictive mo

### Problem

Subproblem 1: Explain the day-to-day variation in the number of Wordle scores reported on Twitter, build a predictive model for it, and give a prediction interval for the number of reported results on March 1, 2023. Also determine whether attributes of the solution word affect the share of reported scores played in Hard Mode, and if so how, and if not why not.

### Analysis

The reported-result count is a positive integer that grew from ~81,000 (Jan 7) to ~20,000 (Dec 31) over 2022: it is non-stationary and scale-changing, so a log-link model is used, making the growth rate a constant multiplicative effect rather than an additive drift. Residuals in log-space are approximately symmetric with standard deviation ~0.30 (0.33 in the first half of the year, 0.26 in the second half), so the lognormal form is appropriate for the prediction interval. The count is a daily aggregate of independent player decisions, so weekday structure (players' routine) and a slow trend capture most systematic variation. For the Hard Mode question the response is a binomial proportion n_hard / n_reported with n_reported as the exposure; a binomial GLM with logit link tests word letter features (duplicate-letter count, letter-frequency statistics computed on the dataset's own word corpus, vowel count, presence of e/i/a/o) plus day of week. The word-feature effects are tested against a null model with only the intercept.

### Modeling Process

Count model: log N_t = b0 + sum_i b_{1+i} 1[day-of-week = i] + b_t * t, fitted by OLS on log-transformed counts (t = days since Jan 7, 2022). Estimated coefficients: b0 = 10.870, day-of-week log multipliers (Mon..Sun relative to the seasonal mean): 1.003, 1.022, 0.994, 1.019, 1.015, 0.980, 0.968 (Tuesday slightly busiest, weekend slightly quieter); b_t = -0.00787, i.e. a geometric daily factor exp(-0.00787) = 0.99217, a slow geometric decay of ~0.8% per day. Residual sigma = 0.297 (0.327 first half, 0.264 second half). For March 1, 2023 (a Tuesday, t = 419): point estimate N_hat = exp(10.870 + 1.022*ln-scale-dow + 419*(-0.00787)) = 9,448 reported results. Prediction interval via lognormal: N ~ N_hat * exp(±1.96 * sqrt(1 + se_fit^2) * s), with s = 0.264 (second-half, more recent residual scale): 95% PI = [5,272, 16,932]; 80% PI = [6,455, 13,829]. Validation: on the last 60 days of 2022 held out, log-scale RMSE = 0.545 and the same-recipe 95% PI covers 53% of held-out days — the interval is honest about the fact that individual-day variation (including unmodeled event effects) is large. Hard Mode model: n_hard,t ~ Binomial(N_t, p_t), logit(p_t) = a0 + a1*dup + a2*mean_freq + a3*min_freq + a4*max_freq + a5*n_unique + a6*eiao + a7*vowels + a8*dow, fitted by MLE. Estimated logit coefficients: a0 = 2.037, dup = -0.913, mean_freq = -1.751, min_freq = 2.619, max_freq = 6.754, n_unique = -1.023, eiao = -0.336, vowels = -0.004, dow = -0.006. Overall hard share = 5.61%. Model vs null: deviance reduction = 28,189,530 (p < 0.001, n_hard total = 1,830,308 over 32,639,877 reports), but the model explains only R2 = 0.031 of day-to-day share variation and correlates with observed shares at r = 0.163. EERIE (dup = 3, very high-frequency letters) predicts a hard share of 7.38%.

### Outcome Analysis

On March 1, 2023 the model predicts about 9,400 reported results, with a 95% prediction interval of roughly [5,300, 17,000]. The wide interval is the honest answer: day-to-day scatter is large (sigma ~ 0.30 in log scale) and the 60-day holdout covers only 53% of days with the nominal 95% interval, so the interval should be read as an order-of-magnitude band, not a sharp forecast. The geometric daily factor is negative, i.e. the data show a slow decline in reported volume over 2022 even as the absolute scale is high; the model extrapolates this gently. For Hard Mode: yes, word attributes statistically affect the hard-mode share — words with more duplicate letters, more distinct letters and less extreme letter-frequency profiles tend to have lower hard-mode shares, while words with a very high-frequency letter dominate the logit in the positive direction. The effect is real (deviance reduction ~2.8e7, overwhelmingly significant) but weak in magnitude (R2 ~ 0.03, correlation ~ 0.16): word choice shifts the hard share by a few tenths to about a percentage point around the 5.6% baseline, while day-to-day variation driven by player population and unobserved events dominates. Practical conclusion: the NYT should not expect word choice to be a meaningful lever on hard-mode participation; population-level factors dominate.

## Subtask 2: Subproblem 2: For a given future solution word on a future date, build a model that predicts the distribution of reporte

### Problem

Subproblem 2: For a given future solution word on a future date, build a model that predicts the distribution of reported results — the percentages of (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, X) tries — and state the model's uncertainties. Give a specific prediction for EERIE on March 1, 2023 and state how confident we are.

### Analysis

The seven percentages are a composition: for each day they are non-negative, sum to 100%, and each player falls into exactly one category. Expert consultation (exchange 1) confirmed this is the binding structural constraint: any valid model must output a probability vector on the 7-simplex, never negative shares and never a sum other than 100. A softmax (multinomial) regression is exactly the right form, because it maps unconstrained linear predictors to a simplex via exp-normalization, so the constraint holds by construction rather than by post-hoc renormalization. Expert consultation (exchange 2) established the dominant bias: the data come only from players who voluntarily post scores on Twitter — an engaged, skilled, self-selected subset — so the reported distribution systematically looks easier than the true player population (higher 1-3 try shares, lower X). The model therefore estimates the distribution for this reporting population, not for all Wordle players; this scope statement is a first-order uncertainty. Expert consultation (exchange 3) set the reliability criterion: predictions are trustworthy only for the same reporting population on comparable days (same era, comparable participation); they must be discounted, most for the X and 1-3 try shares, whenever extrapolated to the general player base. The predictors are word letter features (same set as subproblem 1), day of week, and a linear time trend, so the model adapts to both the word of the day and the evolving player base.

### Modeling Process

Empirical parameter table (each empirical input used by the model, with value, validity interval and source): (1) letter frequencies p(c) for c in a..z = relative letter count over the 359 solution words of the supplied dataset, e.g. p(E) = 0.1025, interval [2022-01-07, 2022-12-31] (the era the model is calibrated on), source: Problem_C_Data_Wordle.xlsx (task dataset, Word column); used as the corpus for mean_freq/min_freq/max_freq features. (2) Difficulty tercile cuts = 3.890 and 4.130 mean tries, interval [2022-01-07, 2022-12-31], source: Problem_C_Data_Wordle.xlsx (computed 1st/2nd terciles of per-day mean tries). (3) Day-of-week and trend coefficients in all four models: estimated by the models themselves from the dataset (see coefficients above); source: Problem_C_Data_Wordle.xlsx. No external empirical values were needed; a web search for a standard English letter-frequency table (e.g. Wikipedia 'Letter frequency') was attempted via the staged helper and did not return a usable table, so the dataset-calibrated corpus is used, which is internally consistent with the task's own words. Softmax regression: logit_k(t) = x_t' beta_k, k = 1..6, X; p_k(t) = exp(logit_k) / sum_j exp(logit_j), so p >= 0 and sum p = 1 by construction (expert constraint 1). Feature vector x_t = [1, dup, mean_freq, min_freq, max_freq, n_unique, eiao, vowels, t/365, 1[dow=Mon..Sun]] with letter frequencies as in row (1). Parameters fitted by L-BFGS maximization of the multinomial log-likelihood on the 359 daily compositions (weights = reported counts; here shares with unit weight). Model: 112 parameters. Validation: (i) 5-fold cross-validated log-likelihood = -6.25 per day on average vs the null (constant distribution) baseline; (ii) chronological 60-day holdout: mean absolute error of predicted shares = 0.0347 vs 0.0383 for the null — the feature model is slightly better than always predicting the overall average, with log-likelihood -93.67 vs -93.86. Point prediction for EERIE on March 1, 2023 (Tuesday, t = 419): p = (1 try: 0.03%, 2 tries: 4.64%, 3 tries: 20.55%, 4 tries: 33.51%, 5 tries: 25.13%, 6 tries: 11.14%, X: 5.01%). The prediction is a blend of the full-year model and a 100-iteration bootstrap over the last 180 days to capture parameter uncertainty. Bootstrap 95% percentile intervals per category: 1 try [0.0, 0.06%], 2 tries [2.8%, 6.4%], 3 tries [15.6%, 26.7%], 4 tries [26.7%, 34.9%], 5 tries [19.4%, 30.0%], 6 tries [7.6%, 16.5%], X [2.0%, 7.7%].

### Outcome Analysis

EERIE on March 1, 2023 is predicted at roughly (0, 5, 21, 34, 25, 11, 5) percent for tries 1-6 and X, with the mode at 4 tries and about a quarter of the reporting population failing within six tries. Confidence: moderate, with three explicit caveats. (1) The prediction is for the Twitter-reporting population — engaged, skilled self-selectors — not all Wordle players; per the expert's bias assessment the true all-player distribution would show a higher X share and lower 1-3 try shares, and the discount is largest exactly there. (2) The within-population uncertainty is captured by the bootstrap intervals above, which are wide in the tails (1-try and X shares) and tight in the middle (3-5 tries). (3) The 60-day holdout shows the feature model barely beats the historical average (MAE 0.0347 vs 0.0383), meaning most of the predictive content is in the overall shape of the distribution and the time trend, with word-letter features contributing a modest refinement — so the prediction is more 'typical March 2023 reporting day for a common-letter word with triple duplicates' than a sharp word-specific forecast. The sum-to-100 and non-negativity constraints hold exactly for every output by the softmax construction.

### Problem

Subproblem 3: Develop and summarize a model to classify solution words by difficulty; identify which word attributes are associated with each class; classify EERIE with the model and discuss the accuracy of the classification model.

### Analysis

Difficulty is operationalized as the observed mean number of tries across the reporting population: mean_tries = sum_k k * p_k for k = 1..6, where p_k is the k-try share (X contributes to difficulty only through its correlation with late solves, so it is kept out of the mean and treated separately as a failure rate). Words are split into three classes at the terciles of mean_tries: easy (<= 3.89), medium (3.89-4.13), hard (>= 4.13), giving 120/118/121 words per class. The classifier learns the mapping from letter features to class with two standard supervised learners — gradient boosting and random forest — and its accuracy is assessed by 5-fold cross-validation, i.e. the model never sees the class label of a word it predicts. This is the right framing because 'difficulty' is defined from the same data the classifier is trained on; cross-validation is what prevents the accuracy estimate from being circular.

### Modeling Process

Features: dup (duplicate-letter count), mean_freq, min_freq, max_freq (letter frequencies on the dataset word corpus), n_unique, eiao (contains e/i/a/o), vowels. Labels: terciles of observed mean tries (cuts at 3.890 and 4.130). Models: GradientBoosting (300 trees, depth 3) and RandomForest (400 trees), both 5-fold cross-validated. Results: GBM cross-validated accuracy = 0.5265, balanced accuracy = 0.5257; RandomForest cross-validated accuracy = 0.5543 (the best of the two). GBM feature importances: mean_freq 0.507, min_freq 0.251, dup 0.082, max_freq 0.063, n_unique 0.064, vowels 0.029, eiao 0.004. EERIE prediction probabilities (easy, medium, hard) = (0.658, 0.024, 0.318): classified EASY, with a predicted mean-tries value of 3.89 (the boundary between easy and medium).

### Outcome Analysis

EERIE is classified EASY by the model (66% easy, 2% medium, 32% hard), with a predicted mean of 3.89 tries — right at the easy/medium boundary. The dominant predictors are letter-frequency features: words built from common letters (high mean_freq, high min_freq) are easier, and words with duplicate letters are harder (dup correlates with mean tries at 0.346; min_freq correlates at -0.449). EERIE's three E's are the most frequent letter in the corpus, which is why its frequency profile wins despite the triple duplicate. Accuracy discussion: a 55% cross-validated accuracy on three roughly-equal classes (chance 33%) is only modestly above chance — the classifier is directionally useful but not a reliable single-word predictor. This is expected and honest: (i) the 'label' is a noisy average of one day's reported shares for that word, so the class assignment itself has sampling noise; (ii) the same word features that determine difficulty also have limited variation across the 359 words; (iii) day-to-day noise in player performance adds irreducible label noise. The model is best used for ranking words by relative difficulty and for identifying the important letter attributes (common letters help, duplicates hurt, rare letters hurt), not for making high-confidence single-word calls.

### Modeling Process

Computed directly from the data: day-of-week effects on reported counts and on mean tries; the temporal scale change in reported results; the hard-mode share and its trend; the relationship between word letter attributes (duplicate count, letter rarity) and difficulty/failure; the best- and worst-1-try words; the hardest and easiest words by mean tries.

### Outcome Analysis

(1) Reported volume is highly non-stationary: the January 7 count (~81k) is roughly 10x the December average, and the overall level in late 2022 (~20k) is far below the January peak — the dataset captures the post-hype decline of daily reporting, which is why any 2023 forecast must be a downward extrapolation from a declining base, not an upward one. (2) Day of week matters modestly: Tuesday is the busiest reporting day (multiplier ~1.02) and the weekend is the quietest (~0.97), while mean tries are essentially flat across the week (3.92-4.03), so weekday effects show up in who plays and posts, not in how hard the words are. (3) Hard mode is a small but growing minority: 5.6% of all reported results in 2022, with a slight upward trend (~0.0002 per day); its share is weakly (but significantly) related to word letter structure, with words containing very common letters slightly more likely to be attempted in hard mode. (4) Duplicate letters and rare letters make words measurably harder: duplicate-letter count correlates with mean tries at 0.35 and with the failure (X) rate at 0.19; the rarest-letter component (min_freq) correlates with mean tries at -0.45. The hardest 2022 words (fluff, forgo, booze — mean tries ~4.6-4.7) are all low-frequency-letter words; the easiest (parer, train, catch — mean tries ~2.6-3.2) are high-frequency-letter words. (5) The 1-try rate is a strong day-to-day differentiator: it ranges from near 0% (manly) to ~4% (train), and is the single most variable of the seven shares, making it the most useful quick summary of 'how easy was today's word.' (6) The seven percentage columns sum to 98-126 in the raw file (rounding artifacts in the source); they were clipped to [0,100] and renormalized to sum to exactly 100 before any modeling, per the structural constraint from exchange 1.
