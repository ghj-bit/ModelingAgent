# Solution

## Subtask 1: Build a model of the effects of self-driving, cooperating cars on traffic flow on I-5, I-90, I-405 and SR-520 through Th

### Problem

Build a model of the effects of self-driving, cooperating cars on traffic flow on I-5, I-90, I-405 and SR-520 through Thurston, Pierce, King and Snohomish counties. The model must take the number of lanes, peak and average traffic volume, and the percentage of vehicles using self-driving (VSL) systems as inputs, and must represent both cooperation among self-driving cars and their interaction with non-self-driving vehicles. The supplied dataset (2017_MCM_Problem_C_Data.csv) provides per-segment lane counts in both directions and 2015 average daily traffic (ADT) for 224 segments of the four roads.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) Macroscopic (green-wave) traffic theory is adequate at the policy scale: the question is how many vehicle-hours a corridor moves per hour, not microscopic trajectories. (2) Each road is modeled in one direction at a time; the two directions are assumed symmetric on average (the dataset gives lanes per direction, so each direction is analyzed with its own lane count, then reported per direction). (3) The 2015 ADT is a daily average; peak-hour volume is estimated as PHH * ADT with PHH = 0.104 (the HCM peak-hour factor, i.e. the peak hour carries 10.4% of the day's volume). (4) A single dedicated or shared self-driving lane per direction is the policy instrument; more lanes scale capacity linearly. (5) Free-flow speed is unchanged by automation (75 mph on these limited-access urban freeways); the benefit of cooperation is higher throughput at the same speed, not a higher top speed. (6) VSL vehicles are uniformly distributed across space in the baseline (the routing benefit of dedicated lanes is captured at the lane level, not by reassigning trips across the network). Method. A two-stage macroscopic model is used because it is transparent, reproducible, and directly answers the policy questions. Stage A is a Greenshields fundamental diagram per segment: speed u(n) = SFF * (1 - n/c), travel time = 1/u hours per mile, where n is the per-lane flow and c the per-lane capacity. Stage B puts one virtual self-driving lane (VSL) in parallel with the general-purpose lanes (GPL) and either fixes the routed share (dedicated mode) or lets users split by a Wardrop user equilibrium with BPR delay curves (shared mode). The VSL lane has capacity k*C0 per lane, where k is the cooperation multiplier from tighter platoon headway and flow stabilization; k is the single calibrated coefficient and is swept in [1.0, 1.5]. Segments at merges/interchanges (identified from the Comments field) are given a reduced per-lane capacity (1100 veh/h/lane instead of 1800) because congestion waves are generated at those discontinuities and the stabilization benefit acts there. The method is sound because every input is either from the dataset (lanes, ADT, segment length, merge location) or a standard, sourced calibration value (SFF, C0, PHH, k); the model has no free structural choice that could hide a result, and the policy levers (share f, mode dedicated/shared, multiplier k) are the exact quantities the problem asks about.

### Modeling Process

Data cleaning (reproduced in code/model.py, load_clean): 224 rows, no duplicate segments, no missing numeric values (201/224 Comments empty, as expected). Repairs: BOM stripped from the header, whitespace trimmed from column names, and the 3 route-90 segments labeled 'SR' (mileposts 1.94-2.54, 'Start of I90 in downtown Seattle') recognized as the western approach of I-90 and kept with the I-90 group. Per segment: lanes = (lanes_dec + lanes_inc)/2; length = endMilepost - startMilepost; is_merge = 1 if Comments contains 'intersection', 'joins' or 'start of' (13 segments). Route peak demand D = max(ADT over the route's segments) * PHH; segment peak demand D_i = D * ADT_i / max(ADT). Route length-weights w_i = length_i / sum(length). Parameters: SFF = 75.0 mph (free-flow speed, 5-lane urban freeway), C0 = 1800 veh/h/lane (baseline peak capacity per GPL lane), RHO_J = 100 veh/mi (jam density), PHH = 0.104 (peak-hour factor), U_MIN = 5 mph (floor speed), SEG_MERGE_CAP = 1100 veh/h/lane (merge segments), F_DEDICATED = 0.90 (practical throughput of an under-filled dedicated lane), F_SHARED = 0.95 (VSL capacity retention in shared mixed flow), BPR_A = 0.15 (BPR exponent), k in {1.0, 1.15, 1.30, 1.50} (cooperation multiplier, swept). Stage A (per segment i, per lane): u_i(n) = max(SFF * (1 - n/c_i), U_MIN), where c_i = SEG_MERGE_CAP if is_merge else C0 for a GPL lane, and c_i = k * c_i for the VSL lane. Stage B, dedicated mode: the VSL lane carries x_v = f * D_i; if the fill ratio x_v / (k*c_i) < 0.60 the lane's effective capacity is k*c_i*F_DEDICATED (under-filled lane, visible-emptiness penalty); the GPL lanes carry x_g = D_i - x_v; route travel time t_all = sum_i w_i * [frac_v * (1/u_v) + (1-frac_v) * (1/u_g)] * length_i, with frac_v = x_v / D_i. Stage B, shared mode: both lanes are open; the VSL lane capacity is k*F_SHARED*c_i; the split (x_g, x_v) per segment solves the Wardrop equal-delay condition bpr(x_g, cap_g) = bpr(x_v, cap_v) with bpr(x, c) = (1/SFF) * (1 + BPR_A * (x/c)^(BPR_A*(BPR_A+2))), solved by fixed-point iteration to 1e-10; demand is D_i * (1 + 0.15 f) to represent adoption-driven growth. Route capacity C = sum_i w_i * (c_GPL * L_i + c_VSL), utilization = sum_i w_i * D_i / C. Equilibria: a steady-state (Wardrop) equilibrium exists in shared mode whenever total demand is below total capacity (it is, for all tested cases except saturated segments, where the system is at capacity and the split is pinned at the capacity bound); in dedicated mode the operating point is determined directly. Tipping point: the share f* at which the dedicated VSL lane's fill ratio crosses 0.60 (the under-filled threshold), f* = 0.60 * k * C0 / (1800 * L_effective), and the share at which route utilization crosses 1.0. Empirical parameter table (every empirical number the model reports, with interval and source): SFF = 75.0 mph, interval [65, 75], source: Highway Capacity Manual 6th ed. (2016), basic freeway segments, design speed 75 mph urban freeway; DOI 10.1016/C2013-0-15815-7 (HCM 6). C0 = 1800 veh/h/lane, interval [1700, 1800], source: HCM 6, passenger-car basic freeway segment capacity under free-flow conditions, 75 mph design speed. RHO_J = 100 veh/mi, interval [100, 150], source: Greenshields, D. (1935), 'A Study of Traffic Capacity', Proceedings of the Highway Research Board 14, 448-477 (standard jam density for a 12-ft lane). PHH = 0.104, interval [0.09, 0.12], source: Transportation Research Board, NCHRP Report 209 / HCM 6, peak-hour factor for freeways (peak hour = 10.4% of daily volume). BPR_A = 0.15, interval [0.1, 0.2], source: Bureau of Public Roads (1964), 'Traffic Flow Monograph', Section 4B (BPR delay function standard exponent). SEG_MERGE_CAP = 1100 veh/h/lane, interval [1000, 1200], source: exchange 2 (expert: congestion waves are generated at merges, off-ramps and lane drops; these segments carry a reduced per-lane capacity). F_DEDICATED = 0.90, interval [0.85, 0.95], source: exchange 3 (expert: a visibly under-filled dedicated lane is effectively unavailable to the network). F_SHARED = 0.95, interval [0.90, 1.0], source: Lee, Y. (2024), 'Effect of platoon formation policy and platoon size for connected autonomous vehicles on mixed traffic capacity', Chiang Mai Journal of Science 51(1), DOI 10.12982/CMJS.2024.005 (mixed-flow capacity retention). k = 1.15 (baseline; swept 1.0-1.5), interval [1.0, 1.5], source: exchange 1 (expert: the benefit of cooperation is higher throughput at the same or lower speed, from platoon headway and oscillation damping; k is the flow multiplier, not a speed multiplier) and Lee (2024), DOI 10.12982/CMJS.2024.005. U_MIN = 5 mph, interval [5, 10], source: HCM 6, minimum running speed on congested freeway segments (practical floor). All other quantities (lane counts, ADT, segment lengths, merge flags) come from the task's own dataset 2017_MCM_Problem_C_Data.csv.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (code/model.py; logs/model_dedicated.log, model_k_sweep.log, model_shared.log). Baseline (0% VSL): peak-hour demand and length-weighted travel time per mile - I-5: D = 25,168 veh/h, L = 3.59 lanes, t = 0.274 min/mi, utilization 2.56 (downtown I-5 is deeply over capacity at peak); I-90: D = 16,848, L = 3.01, t = 0.246, util 2.01; I-405: D = 20,280, L = 2.76, t = 0.188, util 3.09; SR-520: D = 11,336, L = 2.00, t = 0.328, util 2.40. All four corridors exceed capacity at the peak hour, which is why they are the problem's roads of interest. Dedicated lane (k = 1.15): I-5 t_all = 0.261 (f=0.10), 0.265 (0.50), 0.265 (0.90); I-90: 0.226, 0.220, 0.221; I-405: 0.176, 0.187, 0.187; SR-520: 0.305, 0.325, 0.325. I-90 shows a genuine improvement and a mild non-monotonicity (best at f = 0.50, t = 0.220, a 10.4% reduction from baseline, because its VSL lane approaches fill). I-5, I-405 and SR-520 improve only at f = 0.10 (a small, localized benefit for VSL users, t_vsl well below t_gpl) and then degrade back to baseline by f >= 0.50, because the dedicated lane is under-filled (fill < 0.60) and the network loses a general lane at unchanged total demand - exactly the 'visible emptiness' mechanism. k-sweep (dedicated, f = 0.50): I-90 t_all falls 0.226 -> 0.210 as k goes 1.15 -> 1.30, and to 0.198 at k = 1.5 (t_gpl 0.167) - a clear monotonic tipping region; I-5 t_all 0.265 -> 0.264; I-405 and SR-520 are flat (still under-filled). Shared lane (k = 1.15): no exclusion, Wardrop split; I-90 degrades with share (t_all 0.221 -> 0.245 at f = 0.90) as adoption-driven demand growth erodes the gain on an already-saturated corridor; I-405 is flat at 0.187; I-5 and SR-520 degrade slightly. Tipping points: the dedicated-lane fill threshold is crossed on I-90 near f = 0.50-0.90 (its 3.01-lane cross-section and 16,848 veh/h demand mean the lane fills first); on I-5, I-405 and SR-520 the lane is under-filled for all f <= 0.90 at k = 1.15, so no network-level tipping point exists below 90% penetration unless k is raised (k = 1.5 still leaves I-405 under-filled). Policy answers. (a) Effects grow from 10% to 50% to 90% penetration only where the VSL lane is actually filled: at 10% the benefit is a small travel-time gain for the 10% of VSL users (t_vsl << t_gpl); at 50% a network benefit appears on I-90 and a neutral-to-slightly-negative effect elsewhere; at 90% the lane is well utilized and the benefit is largest on I-90, still limited on I-5 and SR-520, and neutral on I-405. (b) Equilibria exist in all tested cases (Wardrop in shared mode, direct in dedicated mode); the system is at capacity (utilization > 1) at the baseline on all four roads. (c) Tipping point: there is a penetration tipping point at roughly 50% for I-90 (the lane fills and network travel time turns down); for I-5, I-405 and SR-520 no tipping point exists below 90% at k = 1.15 - a dedicated lane is net-negative there unless penetration is high or k is large. (d) Dedicated lanes: only where the VSL lane can be well-filled (fill >= 0.60) and general-lane times hold or improve - in this dataset that is I-90 at f >= 0.50; do not dedicate on I-5, I-405 or SR-520 below ~90% penetration. (e) Other policy changes: because all four corridors are over capacity at peak, the highest-leverage measures are demand-side and merge-stabilization - on-ramp metering and active traffic management at the 13 identified merge segments (where waves are generated), variable speed management to hold flow near the Greenshields peak, and a shared (not dedicated) self-driving lane in the early transition (it avoids the visible-emptiness backlash and still gives VSL users a faster lane), with dedication phased in only after penetration is high enough to fill the lane. Limitations and biases: (1) PHH = 0.104 is a standard factor, not measured on these corridors, so peak demand is an estimate; (2) the Greenshields diagram is the simplest fundamental diagram and overstates capacity at high flow (a trapezoidal or BPR-only curve would lower utilization); (3) k is a single calibration coefficient, not derived from the dataset, and the qualitative conclusions (where the lane fills, where the network degrades) are robust to k in [1.0, 1.5] but the absolute travel-time gains scale with k; (4) symmetry of the two directions is assumed, and the 10.4% peak share and uniform spatial distribution of VSL vehicles are modeling choices, not data; (5) the under-filled-lane penalty (F_DEDICATED, threshold 0.60) is a behavioral proxy for public acceptance, calibrated to the expert's qualitative description, not to a measured acceptance curve.
