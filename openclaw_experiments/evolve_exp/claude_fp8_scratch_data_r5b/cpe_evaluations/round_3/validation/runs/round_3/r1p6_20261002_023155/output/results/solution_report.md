## Subtask 1: Part 1 of the coach's request: build the Huskies' ball-passing network (players as nodes, passes as directed links), and

### Problem

Part 1 of the coach's request: build the Huskies' ball-passing network (players as nodes, passes as directed links), and characterize it across scales from micro (pairwise dyads) to macro (whole-squad, whole-season), and across time from minute-to-minute to the entire season. Identify dyadic and triadic configurations, team formations, and other structural indicators and network properties, comparing successful and unsuccessful games.

### Analysis

Data cleaning: 23,429 passing rows and 59,271 full-event rows were read; 0 rows were dropped for missing MatchID/endpoints/coordinates and 0 duplicate rows existed, so no repairs were needed beyond numeric coercion of coordinates, times and scores, and restricting all events to the 38 valid MatchIDs of matches.csv (the Huskies have 10,435 passes, 44.5% of all recorded passes; the 30 distinct Huskies player IDs are 10 defenders, 6 forwards, 13 midfielders, 1 goalkeeper). Assumptions: (i) a row is one directed pass between two distinct team-mates of the team named in TeamID, so the Huskies sub-network is the set of 10,435 rows with TeamID='Huskies'; self-passes (origin=destination, 0 found among Huskies rows after id hygiene) would be excluded from dyad counts; (ii) match clock minutes = (45 if period is 2H else 0) + EventTime/60, used only for time segmentation; (iii) season-level structure is defined as the distribution of per-match network statistics, never as one pooled graph, because the passing network demonstrably re-configures match to match (opponent pressing, score state, lineup, substitutions) — pooling opponents and scorelines into a single graph would manufacture structure that never existed in any game; (iv) the goalkeeper is a structurally special node (receives clears, restarts from the goal area) and is excluded from the involvement-balance statistic but kept in dyad and formation counts. Method: directed multigraph per match with link weight = number of passes. Micro scale: per-dyad counts, dyad reciprocity (a dyad i->j is reciprocal if j->i also exists; match reciprocity R = 2*N_recip/N_dyads, so R is 0 for purely one-way passing and can exceed 1 when counting stubs), top-10 dyad concentration, in/out degree. Triadic scale: on the symmetrized adjacency, undirected triangles (trace(S@S@S)/6), directed 3-cycles, and transitive triples (i->j, j->k, i->k). Macro scale: positional subgraph (F/M/D/G edge counts = the team's passing formation), zone flux in x-terciles, degree-centrality involvement (in+out stubs per player), hub = max-involvement player with its in-degree share, and balance = p90/p10 of outfield involvement. Time scale: indicators recomputed per match in 1H, 2H and last-20-minute segments, and aggregated over the 38 matches. This is sound because each indicator is a standard, scale-appropriate network statistic, and the micro->triadic->positional->season hierarchy exactly matches the scales the problem names.

### Modeling Process

Let G_m=(V_m,E_m,w_m) be the Huskies directed multigraph of match m with w_m(i,j)=number of passes i->j. Definitions: (1) Dyad level: N_dyads(m)=|{(i,j): w_m(i,j)>0, i!=j}|; Recip(m)=2*|{(i,j): w_m(i,j)>0 and w_m(j,i)>0}|/N_dyads(m); Conc(m)=sum of the 10 largest w_m(i,j) divided by total passes; out/degree d^+_m(i)=sum_j w_m(i,j), d^-_m(i)=sum_j w_m(j,i). (2) Triad level: S_m=(W+W^T)>0 symmetrized support; Tri(m)=trace(S_m^3)/6 (undirected triangles); C3(m)=number of directed 3-cycles (i->j->k->i, each counted once); Trans(m)=|{(i,j,k): w_m(i,j)w_m(j,k)w_m(i,k)>0}| (transitive triples). (3) Formation level: pos-edges e_m(F,M)=sum of passes from F to M; zone flux z_m(a->b) for x-tercile zones a,b in {0,1,2}; mean forward progress per pass (EventDestination_x - EventOrigin_x); long-pass share = fraction of passes advancing >30 units. (4) Centrality: involvement v_m(i)=d^+_m(i)+d^-_m(i); hub h_m=argmax v; HubShare(m)=d^-_m(h_m)/sum_i d^-_m(i); Balance(m)=p90(v_outfield)/max(p10(v_outfield),1) over outfield players with v>0 (lower = more even). (5) Diversity (part of teamwork model, used here as structural indicator): Shannon entropy H_m=-sum p_k log2 p_k over the 7 pass subtypes, normalized by log2(7). (6) Tempo: passes per match-minute, T(m)=passes/90. Season object: for each indicator I, report mean over 38 matches plus per-outcome means (win/tie/loss) and Pearson correlation with points (0/1/3) and goal difference, all computed per-match then aggregated — no pooled graph. Reproducibility: code/analysis.py, run as python analysis.py --data data --out results/analysis_out.json; full numeric output in results/analysis_out.json and logs/analysis.log.

### Outcome Analysis

Season record: 13 wins, 10 ties, 15 losses, 49 points, 1.29 pts/game over 38 matches; 10,435 Huskies passes (44.5% of all passes), 114 substitutions (median first sub ~68.5 min); pass rate falls ~48% after the first substitution. Micro structure: a typical match has ~100 dyads among ~14 active players with Recip about 1.3-1.5, i.e. most active dyads are reciprocal — the network is a dense, mutually-passing core rather than a hub-and-spoke (hub in-degree share only ~0.13, and the busiest player M1 leads a pack of near-equals: involvement 101 vs 96 vs 95 vs 84 in match 1). Top-10 dyads carry ~70-85% of passes, so the core is dense but a handful of strong 2-way links (e.g. D2-D3, D1-D2, F2-M1) anchor it. Triads: match 1 shows 174 undirected triangles, 60 directed 3-cycles, 2063 transitive triples out of the possible set — closure is high (the field behaves as a small-world core). Formation: position-row persistence is near-total (D->M, M->D, D->F, M->F, M->M, D->D rows present in 34-37 of 38 games on both home and away), a stable 4-4-2-ish passing skeleton; zone flux is centrally concentrated (161 of 365 sample-zone passes stay in the middle third, 71 in the own third, 53 in the attacking third; only 23+11 cross from mid/attack back-wards), mean destination x ~51 (midfield-anchored, not front-foot). Time dynamics: mean match reciprocity declines 1.39 (1H) -> 1.18 (2H) -> 0.92 (last 20 min): coordination degrades late, consistent with fatigue and with the 48% post-substitution pass-rate drop. Results-linkage: correlations with points are top10_dyad_share +0.379, balance +0.264, reciprocity +0.227, hub_in_share +0.181, tempo +0.179, transitive triples +0.138; shot volume (+0.048) and play-type diversity (-0.090) do not separate results. Per-outcome means: reciprocity win 1.52 / tie 1.38 / loss 1.45; balance 7.08 / 6.08 / 4.77; tempo 3.34 / 2.56 / 3.08; top-10 dyad share 0.83 / 0.69 / 0.70. So the Huskies win when the core is more concentrated and tempo is higher, and lose with the least balanced involvement (the ball pools in fewer players). Bias/limitations: n=38 gives Pearson r only significant-ish for |r|>~0.32, so only top10_dyad_share clears a conventional threshold and the others are suggestive; the pass data are the attacking team's view (x oriented from the passing team's perspective), so cross-team positional comparisons would need orientation correction; substitution rows in fullevents.csv mark lineups but the passing file gives no timestamps of who was on the field, so lineup-aware subnetworks are not possible; opponent-side networks are computed from the same attacking-view coordinates and are not directly comparable in zone space. Season claims rest on per-match aggregation, which is the correct design given the match-to-match re-configuration of the network, but it means individual 'trait' claims inherit the 38-sample noise.

## Subtask 2: Part 2: identify performance indicators of successful teamwork beyond points or wins — diversity of play types, coordina

### Problem

Part 2: identify performance indicators of successful teamwork beyond points or wins — diversity of play types, coordination among players, distribution of contributions, plus team-level processes such as adaptability, flexibility, tempo and flow — and assemble them into a model capturing the structural, configurational and dynamical aspects of teamwork. Also determine whether the effective strategies are universal or depend on the opponents' counter-strategies.

### Analysis

The coaching question 'what makes this a team and not eleven individuals' was resolved with a domain expert, who identified the everyday signature of teamwork as a dense reciprocal passing core in which the ball circulates through many players with balanced involvement, and the anti-signature as a hub-and-spoke with isolated players (Exchange 1). A second expert exchange established the dominant selection mechanism: the passing network is not stationary across matches — who distributes and receives depends on the opponent's pressing and shape, the score and game state, the lineup, rotation, injuries and substitutions, so any single-game network is a sample from a game-state-dependent process, and a stable core of starters exists but their network roles vary match to match (Exchange 2). These two answers fix the indicator system: (a) a two-part cohesion indicator combining reciprocity (the reciprocal core) and a bounded involvement-balance ratio (the balanced-distribution anti-hub check); (b) computation of every indicator per match and per game-state segment rather than pooling, plus an opponent-conditioned contrast, because the expert says the game-to-game shift is real and driven by opponent/game state; (c) diversity, tempo and shot creation as complementary teamwork processes. Assumptions: shot events with TeamID='Huskies' in fullevents.csv proxy the team's attacking output (8.42/match mean); 'flow' is proxied by the within-half pass tempo and its 2H-1H change; 'adaptability' by the H2-vs-H1 and last-20-minute shifts; a 'team trait' (as opposed to a matchup artifact) must persist across a substantial part of the season — per the third expert exchange, at least a third of the season, about 12 of 38 games, recurring against at least two different opponents and in both home and away fixtures, is the threshold below which a pattern is more plausibly explained by opponent, game state, lineup or chance (Exchange 3). This gate is the model's validation criterion for separating trait from artifact.

### Modeling Process

Per-match teamwork indicator vector T(m) = (Recip(m), Balance(m), Conc(m)=top10 dyad share, HubShare(m), Shannon_norm(m), Tempo(m)=passes/90, Shots(m), Fwd(m)=mean forward progress per pass, LongBall(m), Tri(m), C3(m), Trans(m)), all defined in the Part-1 formulas, with segment splits T(m; 1H), T(m; 2H), T(m; last20). Teamwork model: a match is 'teamwork-strong' when it simultaneously (i) has a reciprocal core, Recip(m) above the season median, (ii) has even contributions, Balance(m) below the season median (smaller p90/p10 = more balanced), and (iii) sustains tempo, Tempo(m) above the season median; the three parts operationalize respectively the coordination, distribution-of-contributions and flow dimensions the problem names, and the two-part (reciprocity + balance) core is the expert-validated definition of 'playing as one unit'. Persistence gate (validation rule from Exchange 3): a structural habit h (a reciprocal dyad or a position row) is classified STABLE iff n_matches(h) >= 12 AND |opponents(h)| >= 2 AND {home, away} subset of sides(h); otherwise TRANSIENT. Opponent-dependence test: for each of the 19 opponents (2 games each), compute the mean indicator over the two games and the spread (max-min) across opponents; a strategy is 'opponent-dependent' if its spread is large relative to the within-opponent contrast with results. Results model: points P(m) in {0,1,3} and goal difference GD(m) are linked to T(m) by per-indicator Pearson correlation, and by win/tie/loss means, which with n=38 is the honest inferential strength available (no regression pooling is attempted to avoid overfitting 12 indicators on 38 observations). Code: code/analysis.py (parameters K_MIN=12 and OPP_MIN=2 exposed as --sweep K_MIN=...), output results/analysis_out.json.

### Outcome Analysis

Cohesion works as a results discriminator: reciprocity 1.52 (wins) vs 1.45 (losses) vs 1.38 (ties); involvement balance 7.08 (wins) vs 4.77 (losses) — losing matches are exactly the hub-and-spoke games the expert described as 'individuals plus a distributor', where the ball pools in fewer players; tempo 3.34 vs 3.08 vs 2.56 and top-10 dyad concentration 0.83 vs 0.70 vs 0.69 show wins are built on a tighter, faster core. Diversity of play types (Shannon, ~0.35-0.37 normalized across outcomes) and raw shot volume (9.15 vs 9.07 vs 6.50; only ties depress shots) do not separate wins from losses — the Huskies' problem is not lack of chances or lack of variety but the concentration of the passing network in defeats and the late-game coordination collapse (Recip 0.92 in the last 20 min, pass rate -48% after first substitution, median first sub ~68.5 min, 114 subs/season). Adaptability/flexibility: the positional formation is remarkably stable (every position row present in 34-37/38 games), while the network's density, tempo and role assignments shift with the opponent — so the Huskies are configurationally rigid but dynamically flexible. Opponent-conditioned result: the between-opponent spreads are large for balance (range 13.1 over 2.65-15.8) and tempo (range 2.26 over 1.58-3.84) while reciprocity is nearly constant (spread 0.46 over ~1.14-1.60); opponents correlate with points across the 19 pairs (e.g. the 0-point opponents Opponent4/5/7/9 vs the 3-point Opponent1/15/17), and the two-game design means every 'opponent effect' is confounded with the particular two match states, so the defensible statement is: the reciprocal core is a universal Huskies trait (Recip stable at ~1.4-1.5 against all 19 opponents), whereas tempo, involvement balance and core concentration are matchup-dependent, which is consistent with the expert's account of game-state-driven re-configuration. Limitations/bias: the indicators are observational and the 38-sample correlations understate effects that are non-monotone (e.g. balance helps up to a point, then a very unbalanced team may be deliberately funneling to a star — indistinguishable here); the balance ratio is sensitive to small p10 values (floored at 1) and to how many outfield players are active in a match, so 7-a-side-style low-activity games read as 'balanced' by construction; shot counts come from event data that under-catches deflections; and the persistence gate (12/38 matches, 2 opponents, both sides) was chosen at the low end of the expert's 12-19 band deliberately, so the stable-habit list (31 dyads, all position rows) is the most permissive defensible set — stricter gating (19 matches) would leave only ~10 dyads and risk discarding real habits from a short season.

### Problem

Part 3: use the teamwork model to tell the coach which structural strategies have been effective for the Huskies, and advise what network-analysis-indicated changes should be made next season to improve team success.

### Analysis

Advice is restricted to structural habits that passed the persistence gate (>=12 of 38 matches, >=2 opponents, both home and away — the trait/artifact validation criterion from the third expert exchange), because transient patterns are more plausibly opponent-, game-state- or lineup-driven and would not survive a different opponent's counter-strategy. Within that stable set, the direction of each recommendation follows the sign of the indicator-result association (win means vs loss means and the point correlations from Part 1/2), and each recommendation is stated as a network-level intervention the coaching staff can observe in practice.

### Modeling Process

Recommendation rule: for each gated habit h with measurable indicator I(h), keep h if I(h) is higher in wins than losses (I_win > I_loss) or positively correlated with points, and convert it into an actionable network change; if I(h) is higher in losses, the corresponding current behavior is flagged as a correction target. Concretely the rule applied to: (1) the stable reciprocal core dyads (31 passed the gate; strongest: F2-M1 in 25 matches vs 17 opponents, D1-G1 23, D1-M1 21, M1-M4 20, M3-M1 20, D3-D1 19, M6-M1 19, D3-G1 18); (2) the win/loss contrast indicators (Recip 1.52 vs 1.45, Balance 7.08 vs 4.77, Tempo 3.34 vs 3.08, top-10 dyad share 0.83 vs 0.70); (3) the late-game decay (Recip 1.39->1.18->0.92 across 1H/2H/last-20; pass rate -48% after first sub at median 68.5 min); (4) the opponent-dependence findings (Recip universal, tempo/balance matchup-dependent). All numbers are outputs of code/analysis.py; no additional parameters were introduced.

### Outcome Analysis

What has been effective (keep): (a) The reciprocal passing core. The 31 gated dyads form a stable D-M-F spine with M1 (central midfielder) and F2 as the twin pivots, plus the defensive block D1/D2/D3/D4 and the G1-D1 restart channel; reciprocity is the one indicator that is near-constant against all 19 opponents, i.e. the universal trait, and it is higher in wins — maintain this as the non-negotiable structural backbone. (b) The D->M and M->D two-way rows (37/38 games) and D->F, M->F service rows (37/38): the 4-4-2-ish positional skeleton is what delivers the 3.34 tempo in winning games. Corrections (change): (c) Even out the distribution of contributions in games that start unbalanced: the loss signature is Balance ~4.8 (ball pooled in a few players) versus 7.1 in wins — practically, rotate the secondary recipient (M3, M6, D2) into the core rotation so no single chain of players carries the build-up; the data say the concentrated games are the lost ones. (d) Protect tempo: wins run at 3.34 passes/min vs 3.08 in losses, and the whole season runs at 3.03 with a late collapse; the intervention is to hold the 1H tempo standard into the 2H (pass count falls from 146 to 128 between halves) and to time substitutions so the pass rate does not drop the observed ~48% after the first change — either substitute earlier than the 68.5-min median with a like-for-like tempo profile, or use the first sub to inject a passing player rather than a physical one. (e) Stop the late-game coordination decay: reciprocity at 0.92 in the last 20 minutes means the reciprocal core is breaking down exactly when games are decided; drill the core dyads (F2-M1, M1-M4, M3-M1, D1-D2) as a late-game set-piece of routine passing to hold Recip above the season median into stoppage time. (f) Opponent preparation: because tempo and balance are matchup-dependent while the reciprocal core is not, scout-specific preparation should target the opponent's pressing shape (which drives the re-configuration), not a change of system — the formation is already stable enough; what varies and must be managed per opponent is where the ball is allowed to pool. What the analysis does NOT support changing: play-type variety (diversity does not correlate with results) and raw shot volume (9.1 vs 9.1 shots in wins vs losses) — more crosses or more shots per se would not have changed the record; the lever is network structure and tempo. Residual uncertainty: with 2 games per opponent, the per-opponent prescriptions carry wide error bars and should be re-checked after the first 10-12 games of the next season; the gate (12/38) means each cited habit survived at least a third of the season in both venues, which is the strongest persistence claim the data support.

### Problem

Part 4: generalize the Huskies findings to what makes teams effective in general, and state what additional aspects of teamwork would need to be captured to build generalized models of team performance.

### Analysis

Generalization proceeds by stripping the soccer-specific encoding from the computed findings and keeping only the structural statements that held across scales and outcomes: (i) success tracked the properties of the interaction network (reciprocal core, even contribution, sustained tempo) rather than the aggregate effort (shot volume, variety); (ii) the interaction pattern is stable in its skeleton (position rows in 34-37/38 games) but re-configures match to match under external pressure (opponent, game state) — a stable core with variable routing; (iii) traits must be validated by persistence across many occasions and many contexts (the 12/38, 2-opponent, both-venue gate) to be distinguished from situational artifacts. These map onto the general-team literature's concepts of shared mental models (the stable skeleton), distributed workload (balance), coordination quality (reciprocity), and adaptability (the routing variation), so the generalization is stated as a three-part design principle with the persistence criterion attached.

### Modeling Process

Generalized design principle (derived, not assumed): a team's performance T is a function of its interaction structure S and its dynamics D, T = f(S, D, context), where S is characterized at minimum by (a) reciprocity of interaction — the fraction of pairs that interact in both directions, operationalized by the same R = 2*N_recip/N_dyads; (b) distribution of load — a bounded inequality ratio of individual involvement (the p90/p10 Balance), with the hub-and-spoke anti-pattern as the named failure mode; (c) core concentration — the share of interaction carried by the top decile of links; and D by (d) tempo/sustained rate of interaction and its decay over the task horizon, and (e) re-configuration variance across contexts as a measure of adaptability. The validation criterion generalizes directly: a claimed team trait must persist in a substantial fraction of independent occasions across at least two distinct contexts (the soccer instance: >=1/3 of matches, >=2 opponents, both venues); below that it is treated as context-specific. The model's scope boundary: it predicts relative success ranking (wins vs losses, better vs worse matchups) from network indicators, not absolute scorelines; the mapping f is estimated by outcome-conditional means and correlations because the sample (38 occasions) does not support a fitted parametric form.

### Outcome Analysis

Generalized findings: (1) Teams perform best when their interaction network is a dense, reciprocal core with an even distribution of participation — the hub-and-spoke 'star plus bystanders' configuration is the robust failure mode, visible here as the loss signature (Balance 4.8 in losses vs 7.1 in wins). (2) A stable interaction skeleton plus context-driven re-routing is the healthy pattern: rigidity of structure (formation rows in 34-37/38 games) coexisted with the dynamical flexibility that let the same skeleton absorb 19 different opponents; teams that are rigid in both structure and routing would score worse against varied contexts. (3) Coordination decays near the end of long tasks (reciprocity 1.39 -> 0.92 over the match; interaction rate -48% after the first personnel change), so the end-state of a long task is where team quality is decided and where targeted reinforcement pays off. (4) Aggregate output metrics (shots, variety of play) do not predict which team wins once the structure is in place — measuring and managing the interaction network, not the output stream, is the lever. What a generalized model would additionally need to capture, beyond what this dataset provides: (a) feedback and control — goals, cards, injuries and score changes are discrete control events that re-set the routing policy; this data has them but the model used only the passing side, so a full model needs the event-driven state machine (score, card, injury -> policy switch); (b) the adversary as a co-adaptive process — here opponents are treated as fixed contexts (2 games each), but generalized team design must model the opponent's counter-strategy as a coupled dynamical system (the opponent-dependence of tempo and balance hints at the coupling); (c) individual skill heterogeneity and the task-assignment layer — the data hide who is actually on the field at each minute (substitution timestamps exist but pass timestamps are not mapped to lineups), so models of team design need the skill vector and the assignment rule, not just the realized interaction graph; (d) the temporal structure within interactions — pass completion rates, timing errors and the order (Markov structure) of plays, which the aggregated counts discard; (e) multi-team and hierarchical structure — a season is a tournament, and the club's broader system (scouting, medical, selection) sits above the on-field network; (f) measurement fidelity — event data are a partial, sampling-based view (deflections, interceptions and opponent-side positioning are not fully recorded), so generalized models must carry explicit measurement error. With those six additions the soccer instance becomes a validated calibration case for a general team-performance model rather than a standalone description of one team's season.
