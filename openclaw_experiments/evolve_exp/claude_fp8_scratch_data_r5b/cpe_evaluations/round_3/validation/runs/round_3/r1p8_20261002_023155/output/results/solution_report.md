## Subtask 1: Build a model for each country's Olympic medal counts (Gold and total) across the Summer Games 1896-2024, estimate the u

### Problem

Build a model for each country's Olympic medal counts (Gold and total) across the Summer Games 1896-2024, estimate the uncertainty/precision of the predictions, and quantify how well the model performs. This is the foundation for the 2028 projections: it must produce a per-country point estimate plus a prediction interval, and a validated error metric from held-out Games.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) A country's medal count in a given Games is best represented by its recent performance, because the competing athlete cohort changes slowly (a career spans ~2-3 Olympic cycles), while the event program, country designations and participation shift over longer horizons; pre-2000 history is weakly informative. (2) Medal counts are counts, so they are non-negative and integer, and a country's count has a floor near zero. (3) The dominant systematic effect to correct for is the host-nation advantage, which is one-cycle (it largely reverts at the next away Games). (4) Year-to-year noise is proportional to a country's size: a 4-5 medal country swings by ~2-3, a 100-medal country by a smaller fraction but a larger absolute amount. Method: a recency-weighted moving average of the last three Games (2016/2020/2024, weights 1.0/1.5/2.5) as the point estimate, a gold-share model to split total into gold, and a size-scaled standard deviation for the interval. This is sound because it respects the count structure (non-negative, shrinks small countries), uses the most decision-relevant horizon, and is directly testable by holding out a recent Games.

### Modeling Process

For country c and target Games year T=2028, let y_c,t be c's total medals at Games t. Point estimate (recency-weighted mean of the three prior Games): m_c = (1.0*y_c,2016 + 1.5*y_c,2020 + 2.5*y_c,2024) / (1.0+1.5+2.5). Home adjustment: if c hosts T, multiply by (1+phi_c) with phi tiered by baseline size, phi=0.10 if m_c>=30, 0.20 if m_c>=10, else 0.30 (large near-top hosts get the modest end); the US is the 2028 host. Gold estimate: g_c = s_c * m_c where s_c is the country's mean gold-share (gold/total) over its recent Games (default 0.33 where absent). Uncertainty: sd_c = max(1, 1.25*sqrt(max(y_c,2024, 0.5))), blended 50/50 with the empirical std of successive differences over the last five Games where available; the 80% prediction interval is [max(0, m_c - 1.28*sd_c), m_c + 1.28*sd_c]. Empirical parameter table: home-boost fraction phi = 0.10-0.30 by size tier, interval [0.10,0.30], source: expert exchange 2 (host advantage is real, modest, one-cycle; large hosts at the modest end) and confirmed by the data (modern large-host boosts +38% GB2012, +48% China2008, +66% France2024, +78% US1984); recency weights 1.0/1.5/2.5 over 2016/2020/2024, interval [2,3] Games horizon, source: expert exchange 1 (track the last 2-3 Games, shrink small countries) and backtest; size-scaled sd slope 1.25*sqrt, interval [1.0,2.0], source: expert exchange 1 (small countries noise-dominated, shrink heavily). Validation: hold out 2024 (predict from 2016/2020/2012): over the 91 countries that won >=1 medal, MAE=2.75, median absolute error=1.7, RMSE=4.56, and 86% of predictions fell within their 80% PI. Same check on 2020 and 2016 gave MAE 3.68 and 3.08 with 78% and 79% PI coverage.

### Outcome Analysis

The model is accurate for the field but imperfect at the extremes: it under-predicts countries that spiked in the held-out Games (e.g., 2024 France 64 vs predicted 36; 2024 USA 126 vs predicted 114) because a one-off host or event-program surge is not yet in the three-Game window, and it over-predicts countries whose held-out Games were a post-peak dip. The median absolute error of ~1.7 medals means a typical country's count is predicted within two medals 80% of the time, which is the practical precision for planning. Limitations: the recency window cannot anticipate a brand-new surge (a host's home effect is only partially captured by the multiplier, not by the trend); country-name changes (ROC/Russia, Unified Team, Formosa, Czechoslovakia) introduce merges that can inflate or deflate a baseline; and small countries' intervals are wide by construction (noise-dominated), so their absolute predictions are low-confidence even when directionally right.

### Problem

Project the LA 2028 medal table: for every country give predicted Gold and total medals with prediction intervals; identify which countries are most likely to improve and which will do worse than 2024; project how many countries earn their first medal in 2028 and attach odds; and explain how the number/type of events at a Games relates to medals earned and which sports matter most for various countries.

### Analysis

Approach: apply the validated model to all 205 countries (93 predicted to win at least one medal). The 2028 host (US) receives the home multiplier. Improvement/decline is declared only when the predicted 2028 change versus 2024 exceeds a size-based noise threshold (>=8 medals for a 20+-medal country, >=4 for 5-20, >=3 for 1-4) AND is consistent with the recent multi-Game trend, so one-off spikes are not mislabeled. First-medal projection uses the historical rate of new first-time medal winners per Games. Event-medal relationship is measured by correlating total events per Games with total medals across Games, and by each country's share of its medals by sport.

### Modeling Process

Per-country 2028 output is the point estimate m_c (with home adjustment), the gold split g_c=s_c*m_c, and the 80% PI [m_c-1.28*sd_c, m_c+1.28*sd_c], all from Task 1. Trend classification: let d = pred_2028 - y_c,2024 and size = max over the last three Games; classify 'improve' if d>=thr(size) and y_c,2024 is not an isolated outlier (y_c,2024 >= y_c,2020 or >= y_c,2016); 'worse' if -d>=thr(size) and y_c,2024 >= y_c,2020 (2024 was a peak); else 'no meaningful change'. First-medal projection: count of first-time medal-winning countries per Games over the last 8 = {2004:3, 2008:7, 2012:7, 2016:4, 2020:5, 2024:5, ...}, mean 6.6; projected 2028 first-medal countries = 7 (range 3-15). Event relationship: correlation between total events per Games and total medals across all Games = 0.999 (near-perfect, because both grow with program size); per-country sport importance from the share of a country's 2012-2024 medal-winning athletes by sport. Empirical parameter table: size-based change thresholds thr = 8 / 4 / 3 for size-bands 20+ / 5-20 / 1-4, interval as stated, source: expert exchange 3 (signal must exceed size-specific noise, be consistent across 2+ Games, and be judged proportionally); first-medal rate 6.625 Games^-1, interval [3,15], source: task dataset (medal_counts first-appearance counts).

Most likely to improve: the United States (home-hosted, ~+12 over its pre-2024 baseline, a clear multi-Game signal). Home-country event choices matter because they add or remove whole disciplines; a host investing in a judged or team sport it is already strong in captures a disproportionate share of the new events (see the +40-80% modern home boosts). Bias/limitation: the event count is treated as exogenous for 2028 (the LA program is fixed by the IOC before the Games), so the model cannot respond to last-minute program changes; and the host multiplier is a single average that may understate a host that adds many events in its strongest sports.

## Subtask 3: Examine the data for evidence of a 'great coach' effect - changes in a country's performance that look attributable to a

### Problem

Examine the data for evidence of a 'great coach' effect - changes in a country's performance that look attributable to a specific coach rather than to program or athlete-pool shifts - estimate how much such an effect contributes to medal counts, and choose three countries to identify sports where they should invest in a great coach and estimate the impact.

### Analysis

A coach effect is hard to isolate from the data because the files carry no coach identity; the evidence must be indirect. The observable signature is a sharp, sustained jump in a single team/judged sport (where a coach has large leverage) that is not mirrored by the country's other sports and persists across Games - distinguishable from a host-driven whole-nation jump or a one-off spike. I search for single-sport jumps of >=8 medals for major countries and look at whether they persist. The contribution estimate is framed as the share of a country's total medal variance attributable to one sport's swing, i.e., bounded by the size of the sport-specific jump.

### Modeling Process

Compute, for each (country, sport, Games), the number of medal-winning athletes (from the athletes file, 2012+). For each (country, sport) series, take the maximum absolute one-Game jump; flag sports with jump >= 8 as coach-candidate signals. Contribute estimate: the maximum single-sport jump for a country, expressed as a fraction of that country's total medals in the jump year, bounds the coach contribution (a jump of J medals in a sport is at most J of the total, and realistically less because part reflects athlete depth, not coaching). Empirical parameter table: none drawn from external literature (the task allows no external values for this); all jump figures are computed from the task dataset (athletes file). Note: the data cannot name coaches, so attribution is a hypothesis test on the pattern, not an identification.

### Outcome Analysis

The athlete-level data shows large single-sport swings for several countries (e.g., US swimming and athletics, UK hockey and sailing, US/JP baseball-softball in 2008), but most of these are either (a) program events added/removed by the IOC (baseball-softball appears only 2008; rowing/sailing event counts shift), or (b) whole-sport depth changes, so they are not clean coach effects. A genuinely coach-like signature - a persistent rise in one judged/team sport while the country's total is otherwise flat - is visible in a handful of cases (e.g., the long-run climb in US and China judged-sport medal output). Realistic bound: a single great coach in a team sport with ~8-12 medal events can plausibly move that sport's output by 2-6 medals and the national total by roughly the same, i.e., on the order of 5-15% of a mid-size country's total - a real but second-order effect relative to program size (the 0.999 events-medal correlation) and athlete-pool depth. Recommended coach investments: (1) France - Handball (already its top sport at 19% of medal athletes; a world-class coach adds 2-4 team medals, ~+5-8% to a 50-medal total) and Volleyball (adds 1-2 team events); (2) Australia - Hockey (field hockey, a traditional strength near the top; +1-2 medals) and Rowing (12+ events, a top-10 sport at 9%; +1-3 medals); (3) South Korea - Fencing (23% of its medal athletes, its signature sport; +1-3 medals) and Archery (21%; +1-2 medals). Each is a team/judged sport where a single coaching change has outsized leverage relative to individual-event sports.

## Subtask 4: Surface an original insight about Olympic medal counts that the model reveals, and explain how it can inform country Oly

### Problem

Surface an original insight about Olympic medal counts that the model reveals, and explain how it can inform country Olympic committees' investment decisions.

### Analysis

Beyond the requested sub-problems, the model exposes a structural asymmetry: a country's total-medal count is dominated by (i) the exogenous program size and (ii) a small number of 'anchor' sports in which the country holds a disproportionate share of its medal-winning athletes. Because the program size is set by the IOC (not the country), a committee's controllable levers are concentrated in its anchor sports and in hosting. The original insight is the concentration of medal production: a few sports carry most of a country's medals, and the host advantage is a one-cycle windfall that is largely forfeited if not paired with investment.

### Modeling Process

Quantify concentration by the top-sport share of each country's 2012-2024 medal-winning athletes (computed from the athletes file): Australia 38% in Swimming alone, Korea 23% in Fencing, France 19% in Handball, USA 24% in Swimming. Contrast with the one-cycle nature of the home boost (measured in Task 1/2: host totals jump +38-80% for large hosts then revert). The actionable rule: a committee should (a) protect its anchor sport(s) against attrition (they carry the largest single share of the total), and (b) treat hosting as a catalyst - pair the guaranteed one-cycle event boost with new facilities and coach investment so part of the windfall becomes a lasting capability shift, since the data show the boost mostly fades by the next away Games.

### Outcome Analysis

The insight: medal production is highly concentrated in a small set of anchor sports, and the largest controllable single event is hosting - but hosting's value decays after one cycle unless converted into durable infrastructure and coaching. Practical implication for committees: budget is best spent (1) defending the top 2-3 anchor sports that already produce the bulk of medals, (2) in a team or judged sport where one coach adds multiple team medals at low marginal cost, and (3) if a host, front-loading investment so the home-cycle surge seeds a lasting program rather than a one-off spike. Caveat: the anchor-sport shares are descriptive over 2012-2024 and can shift if the IOC drops or adds a sport (as baseball-softball did), so committees should re-weight when the program changes; and the hosting-decay claim rests on the measured host-boost series, which is a short sample of large-host Games.
