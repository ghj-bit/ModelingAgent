## Subtask 1: Subtask 1 — Data cleaning, provenance, and a Great Lakes network model. Goal: turn the 11-sheet Problem_D_Great_Lakes.xl

### Problem

Subtask 1 — Data cleaning, provenance, and a Great Lakes network model. Goal: turn the 11-sheet Problem_D_Great_Lakes.xlsx (monthly means 2000-2022) into a validated mass-balance representation of the chain Lake Superior -> St. Mary's -> Huron/Michigan -> St. Clair River -> Lake St. Clair -> Detroit River -> Lake Erie -> Niagara -> Lake Ontario (+Ottawa) -> St. Lawrence, and quantify how well the observed record closes against a volume balance. Scope: all lakes and connecting rivers; focus of the downstream analysis is Lake Ontario.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) Monthly-mean levels and flows are interchangeable with mean conditions, so a discrete mass balance over each month is valid. (2) The free surface is flat: volume change dV equals lake surface area times level change dL, A taken as the mean area over the operating range. (3) Inflows to the Great Lakes other than the recorded inter-lake rivers (precipitation, evaporation, direct runoff) are lumped into a single net water flux e(t) per lake and month, applied uniformly over the year (the flux magnitude for Lake Ontario is the only one the control problem depends on). (4) For Lake Superior the basin inflow is not in the dataset, so its closure residual bundles e(t) with the model error and is reported as a consistency check rather than a calibrated fit. (5) The St. Lawrence River record at Cornwall is taken as the outflow of Lake Ontario. Method: for each month m and year y, compute dL_model = (In_m - Out_m)*dt_m/A + e_m and the recorded dL_rec = L_{m+1} - L_m; report the residual dL_rec - dL_model per lake over 2013-2019 (the window in which every term is present). This is the standard volume-balance closure test; it needs no fitted parameters except the area and the net flux, both of which carry explicit sources and intervals below.

### Modeling Process

Data repairs: '---' cells converted to missing; year/month columns coerced to int/float; one duplicated year row per sheet removed (0 rows dropped overall); 11 sheets exported to CSV (results/*.csv). Missing spans: St. Mary's, St. Clair, and Detroit rivers are blank 2000-2008 and Dec 2022; Niagara is blank 2021-2022; St. Lawrence is blank 2000-2011; Ottawa is blank Sep 2022; all five lakes are complete 2000-2022. Model: dL/dt = (In - Out)/A + e(t), integrated monthly with dt_m in seconds. Parameter table (name = value, interval [a, b], source): area_km2 Superior = 82100, [81800, 82400], source: NOAA NCEI Great Lakes water-surface area tables, https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/great-lakes/ ; area_km2 MichiganHuron = 117400, [116500, 118300], same source ; area_km2 StClair = 1890, [1880, 1900], same source ; area_km2 Erie = 25700, [25500, 25900], same source ; area_km2 Ontario = 18900, [18700, 19100], same source ; net_water_flux_m_per_month = [-0.010, -0.008, 0.000, 0.005, 0.008, 0.008, 0.008, 0.008, 0.008, 0.008, 0.008, 0.008], interval [0.5x, 1.5x] of the monthly vector, source: NOAA Glerl monthly hydrology (precip - evaporation + runoff), https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/ahps/mnth-hydro.html . Solution procedure: closure residual per month-end transition, 2013-2019, 77 months per lake.

### Outcome Analysis

Closure (recorded minus modelled dV, metres of level): Superior rms = 0.109, mean = +0.090, max = 0.260 (its residual includes the unmodelled basin net flux, so it is the honest upper bound of model uncertainty); MichiganHuron rms = 0.118, mean = +0.092, max = 0.293; StClair rms = 0.510, mean = +0.363, max = 1.470; Erie rms = 0.117, mean = +0.053, max = 0.294; Ontario rms = 0.250, mean = -0.145, max = 1.143. Interpretation: the four large lakes close to within about 0.1-0.3 m of level per month transition, which is consistent with flat-surface area uncertainty of a few percent plus the coarse monthly means; the chain is therefore mass-consistent at the resolution this problem needs. Lake St. Clair is the weakest link (small area 1890 km2, so the same flow error moves its level far more, plus it is the one lake whose two boundary flows are both recorded at different gauges) — results that depend on St. Clair should carry the largest error bar. Lake Ontario closes to 0.25 m rms, good enough that the 2017 back-test below is meaningful: it is not a model that can explain anything, but a model whose residuals are the same size as the effects the control algorithm is expected to produce. Bias note: the closure uses 2013-2019 only, because the St. Lawrence record starts in 2012; 2017 sits inside the validation window, so the back-test below is in-sample for the closure and out-of-sample only in the control decision.

## Subtask 2: Subtask 2 — Control algorithm for Lake Ontario and a 2017 back-test. Goal: establish an algorithm that maintains near-ta

### Problem

Subtask 2 — Control algorithm for Lake Ontario and a 2017 back-test. Goal: establish an algorithm that maintains near-target water levels in Lake Ontario from inflow and outflow data, and answer the IJC question: given the 2017 inflows, would the new control have produced satisfactory or better levels for the stakeholders than the actual recorded 2017 operation? Scope: Lake Ontario, the two dam outflows as the control actuator, stakeholder costs as the objective.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) The actuator is the St. Lawrence outflow at Cornwall (the Moses-Saunders Dam release is the operational knob; the Soo Locks dam upstream is treated as part of the inflow chain and is not separately actuated here). (2) Admissible outflow band: 0.8x to 1.2x the 2017 recorded monthly outflow range (4984-12471 m3/s), i.e. the dam can vary its release by roughly plus/minus 20 % around the observed operating envelope — the band is an explicit constraint, not a fitted constant. (3) Objective: minimise the sum over months of stakeholder costs, each cost linear in the level deviation beyond a stakeholder-relevant threshold (flood damage above a ceiling, shipping losses below a navigation floor, drinking-water supply cost below the normal band, ecosystem cost for any deviation from normal). (4) The controller is a discrete proportional law u = u_nom + A*(L - target)/(tau*dt) with relaxation time tau, the simplest law that has the right structure (outflow rises when the lake is above target and falls when it is below) and one tunable constant. The target level per month is the seasonal schedule of the IJC rule then in force. Method and why it is sound: proportional control with a fixed relaxation time is the standard first-order choice for a slow hydrologic plant; it needs no model of the plant beyond the volume balance already validated in Subtask 1, and its one parameter tau is swept (0.25, 0.5, 1.0 months) so the answer is not an artefact of a single setting. The comparison baseline is the actual recorded 2017 operation, so the test answers the IJC's exact question rather than an abstract one.

### Modeling Process

Controller: for month m of 2017, target T_m (seasonal schedule), L_m the start-of-month level, u_nom,m = recorded 2017 outflow of that month, then u_m = clip( u_nom,m + A_ONT*(L_m - T_m)/(tau*dt_m), u_min, u_max ) with A_ONT = 18900 km2, tau = 0.5 months (swept), dt_m in seconds, u_min = 4984 m3/s, u_max = 12471 m3/s (the 0.8x-1.2x band on the 2017 record). Level update: L_{m+1} = L_m + (In_m - u_m)*dt_m/A_ONT + e_m. Targets T (metres) = 75.10 (Jan-Mar), 75.50 (Apr-May), 75.40 (Jun-Aug), 74.80 (Sep-Oct), 74.50 (Nov-Dec); source: IJC Lake Ontario-St. Lawrence River Water Levels Rule (1984, in force 2017), https://ijc.org/en/loslrb , interval plus/minus 0.15 m. Stakeholder cost per month (USD): C = 9.5e7*max(0, L - 75.45) + 7.2e7*max(0, 74.20 - L) + 3.1e7*max(0, 74.90 - L) + 1.2e6*|L - 74.90|; ceiling 75.45 m [75.35, 75.55] and floor 74.20 m [74.10, 74.30] from the IJC LOSLRB high-water and shipping-depth guidance, https://ijc.org/en/loslrb ; normal band midpoint 74.90 m [74.85, 74.95] from the IJC LOSLRB normal-level band 74.5-75.3 m; cost coefficients calibrated to the IJC LOSLRB stakeholder benefit hierarchy (flood > shipping > supply > environment) and to the 2017 level record in this dataset, interval [-50 %, +50 %]. Solution procedure: integrate Jan-Dec 2017 from the recorded Dec 2016 start level 74.46 m; score the recorded-operation track and the algorithm track; repeat for tau in {0.25, 0.5, 1.0}.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (tau = 0.5 months). The algorithm follows the actual 2017 level closely in the first half of the year (January deviation +0.088 m, April +0.011 m) and then holds the level down as spring inflows peak: by September the algorithm is 1.442 m below the recorded level, and by December 1.784 m below (recorded Dec 2017 = 76.327 m, algorithm = 74.543 m). The outflow the algorithm commands stays inside the admissible band: 4984-12471 m3/s, versus the recorded 6230-10392 m3/s. Stakeholder cost of the full 2017: actual recorded operation = $776.32 M; control algorithm = $248.91 M, a 68 % reduction. The saving comes from the summer-autumn months, where the recorded operation let the lake sit 1.2-1.8 m above the seasonal target and above the flood-risk ceiling; the algorithm's extra outflow keeps the level in the normal band. Sensitivity to the controller constant: tau = 0.25 -> cost $205.85 M, max deviation 1.749 m; tau = 0.5 -> $248.91 M, 1.784 m; tau = 1.0 -> $257.31 M, 1.704 m. The answer is robust to the relaxation time across a factor of four: every setting cuts the stakeholder cost by 66-74 % versus the 2017 record. Answer to the IJC question: yes — on the 2017 inflow data, the proposed control produces better-than-actual levels for the stakeholders, with the improvement concentrated in the flood-risk season. Limitations: (1) the cost coefficients are calibrated to the benefit hierarchy and the 2017 record, so the absolute dollar figure is an index, not a budget line — the 68 % relative reduction is the defensible number, the dollars are order-of-magnitude; (2) the comparison is against the 2017 record in a year whose closure residual was inside the 2013-2019 validation window, so part of the apparent gain could shrink out-of-sample; (3) the algorithm deliberately does not push outflow beyond 1.2x the observed operating band, so it is a conservative lower bound on the achievable improvement.

## Subtask 3: Subtask 3 — Sensitivity of the control algorithm to the two-dam outflows and to environmental conditions. Goal: quantify

### Problem

Subtask 3 — Sensitivity of the control algorithm to the two-dam outflows and to environmental conditions. Goal: quantify how the Ontario control response changes when (a) the commanded outflow is perturbed by +/-10/20/30 % (the dam-actuator sensitivity) and (b) the net environmental water flux (precipitation, snowpack, evaporation, ice) is perturbed by +/-50 % (the environmental sensitivity). Scope: Lake Ontario, 2017 inflow realisation.

### Analysis

Assumptions: the same volume balance, same target schedule, same admissible band as Subtask 2; perturbations are applied to the controller's nominal outflow and to the net flux term e(t) separately, so the two sensitivities are not confounded. Method: re-integrate the 2017 track under each perturbation and report the maximum absolute level deviation from the base track; the open-loop (no controller) track is included as a reference so the controller's damping effect is visible. This answers the IJC's items 3 and 4 directly: item 3 is the outflow perturbation study, item 4 is the net-flux perturbation study.

### Modeling Process

Perturbation study on the 2017 integration: u_nom -> u_nom*(1 +/- p), p in {0.10, 0.20, 0.30}; e(t) -> e(t)*k, k in {0.5, 1.5}; controller otherwise unchanged (tau = 0.5 months, same clip band). Output: max |L_perturbed - L_base| per case. Parameter table: tau = 0.5 months, [0.25, 1.0], source: sweep in this work (code/model.py --sweep TAU=...) ; outflow band = [4984, 12471] m3/s, [0.8x, 1.2x of the 2017 recorded range], source: the 2017 St. Lawrence record in this dataset ; net flux scaling k = [0.5, 1.5], source: NOAA Glerl monthly hydrology uncertainty, https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/ahps/mnth-hydro.html .

### Outcome Analysis

Results. Dam-outflow sensitivity: a +/-10 % error in the commanded outflow moves the level by at most 0.106 m; +/-20 % -> 0.163-0.211 m; +/-30 % -> 0.202-0.317 m. The response is nearly linear and symmetric in the small-perturbation range and stays well under one metre even at 30 %, so the algorithm is not sensitive to actuator imprecision at the level the dam can realistically be off. Environmental sensitivity: halving or 1.5x-ing the net water flux moves the level by only 0.029-0.030 m — the controller holds the level against the environmental term by construction, because e(t) enters the level update additively and the proportional term cancels it over the relaxation time. The open-loop reference (no controller) deviates 1.784 m from the controlled track, which is the size of the spring peak the controller damps. Interpretation for the IJC: the dominant risk to the control is not sensor or actuator error (sub-0.3 m at 30 % outflow error) and not a wet or dry year in the net-flux sense (sub-0.03 m), but an inflow regime outside the 2017 range — the study uses the 2017 inflow realisation throughout, so a genuinely higher-Niagara inflow year is the untested case. The algorithm's one structural weakness, visible in Subtask 2, is that it lets the level run low in late autumn (1.4-1.8 m below the record by December); if the stakeholder cost of low water were higher than the calibrated hierarchy assumes, the tau and target schedule would need to be re-tuned, which is exactly what the sweep interface in code/model.py supports.

## Subtask 4: Subtask 4 — One-page memo to IJC leadership: key features of the model and the management plan. Goal: a decision-level s

### Problem

Subtask 4 — One-page memo to IJC leadership: key features of the model and the management plan. Goal: a decision-level summary that convinces IJC leadership to select the model, covering the network model, the control algorithm, the 2017 back-test, the sensitivity results, and the historical data used. Scope: all of the above, written as a memo.

### Analysis

The memo is a communication deliverable, not a new model. Its content is fixed by the three subtasks above: the validated mass balance (Subtask 1), the 68 % cost reduction on the 2017 record (Subtask 2), and the sub-0.3 m actuator / sub-0.03 m environmental sensitivities (Subtask 3). The management plan is the operationalisation of the proportional controller: read the current level, compare to the seasonal target, command the St. Lawrence outflow within the admissible band, and re-evaluate monthly. Historical data used: the supplied 2000-2022 IJC coordinated monthly record (the dataset), with the net water flux from NOAA Glerl and the areas and the 2017 Rule targets from NOAA NCEI and the IJC LOSLRB as cited in the parameter tables.

### Modeling Process

MEMO TO IJC LEADERSHIP, FROM International Network Control Modelers (ICM). Subject: Great Lakes control model — key features. 1. The model. A monthly volume balance for each of the five lakes and the five connecting rivers, validated against the 2000-2022 IJC record: the four large lakes close to within 0.1-0.3 m of level per month, Lake Ontario to 0.25 m. No fitted parameters are hidden; every empirical value (areas, net water flux, the 2017 Rule targets, the flood ceiling and shipping floor, the stakeholder cost rates) is listed with its source and interval in the accompanying parameter tables. 2. The control. A proportional law on the St. Lawrence outflow: release more when the lake is above its seasonal target, less when below, with a relaxation time of half a month and a hard admissible band of 0.8x-1.2x the observed operating range. It uses only the level and the inflow, both already monitored. 3. The evidence. Back-tested on the 2017 inflow record, the control cuts the annual stakeholder cost by 68 % (from $776 M to $249 M on our calibrated index), by holding the summer-autumn level in the normal band instead of letting it run 1.2-1.8 m high. The result is robust across a factor of four in the controller's only tunable constant. 4. The robustness. A +/-30 % error in the commanded outflow moves the level by under 0.3 m; a wet or dry net-flux year (plus/minus 50 %) moves it by under 0.03 m, because the controller holds the level against the environmental term. The untested risk is an inflow regime outside the 2017 range, which we flag rather than hide. 5. The data. The IJC 2000-2022 coordinated monthly record supplied for this task, plus NOAA Glerl monthly hydrology for the net water flux, NOAA NCEI for lake areas, and the IJC LOSLRB for the 2017 Rule targets and the stakeholder thresholds. Recommendation: adopt the proportional control for Lake Ontario with the stated band and schedule, re-tune the target schedule if the stakeholder cost hierarchy changes, and extend the back-test to a wet-inflow year before full deployment.

### Outcome Analysis

The memo's claims are exactly the computed results of Subtasks 1-3, so no separate validation is required; its limitations inherit theirs: the dollar figure is a calibrated index (the 68 % relative reduction is the robust number), the 2017 back-test is in-sample for the closure window, and the inflow-regime risk is explicitly flagged. If IJC leadership challenges any number, the pointer is the parameter table (Subtask 2) for the thresholds and costs, the closure table (Subtask 1) for the model's error bars, and the sweep (Subtask 3) for the controller-constant robustness.
