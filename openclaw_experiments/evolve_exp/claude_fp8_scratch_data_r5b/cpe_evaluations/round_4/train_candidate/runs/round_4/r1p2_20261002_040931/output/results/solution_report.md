## Subtask 1: Subtask 0 — Data preparation, cleaning and provenance audit for the three Amazon review files (hair_dryer.tsv, microwave

### Problem

Subtask 0 — Data preparation, cleaning and provenance audit for the three Amazon review files (hair_dryer.tsv, microwave.tsv, pacifier.tsv). Goal: establish a clean, documented base table on which every later model runs, and quantify the data gaps (missing values, irregular timestamps, duplicate reviews, ambiguous labels) that could skew the analysis. Scope: all 32,025 rows across the three files, 2002-03-02 to 2015-08-31.

### Analysis

The files are a public marketplace dump, not a controlled experiment. Audits found: no duplicate review_id, no invalid star ratings, no unparseable dates, no helpful_votes > total_votes in any file. The real gaps are structural: (i) helpful_votes/total_votes are zero for the majority of reviews (only 4,329 / 1,081 / 5,308 of the 11,470 / 1,615 / 18,939 rows in hair_dryer / microwave / pacifier have total_votes > 0), so any helpfulness measure is defined on a self-selected subset of voted reviews; (ii) the verified_purchase flag is not uniformly 'Y' — it is 0.855 Y for hair_dryer, 0.674 for microwave, 0.517 for pacifier, and lowercase variants exist; (iii) 2 pacifier rows and 1 hair_dryer row have empty headlines (bodies intact). Assumption: rows with total_votes = 0 are treated as 'no vote information yet' rather than 'review was not helpful' — votes accumulate over time after a review is posted, so the dump date, not the review date, truncates the vote count; helpfulness rate is therefore computed only over reviews with total_votes > 0. The verified-purchase gap is handled by an explicit comparison of rating levels between verified and unverified reviews (reported in subtask 4), which bounds the bias instead of assuming it away.

### Modeling Process

Cleaning pipeline (code/clean.py): parse TSV as strings -> drop exact duplicate review_id (0 removed) -> coerce star_rating to int and keep {1,...,5} (0 removed) -> coerce votes to non-negative ints and clip helpful_votes to total_votes (0 corrected) -> parse review_date as M/D/YYYY, drop unparseable (0 removed) -> flag empty headline/body. Output: data/cleaned/{name}.csv with columns date (datetime), star_rating (int), helpful_votes, total_votes, helpfulness_rate = helpful_votes/total_votes (NaN when total_votes = 0), verified_purchase, vine, review_headline, review_body, product_parent. Row counts after cleaning: hair_dryer 11,470; microwave 1,615; pacifier 18,939. Provenance audit numbers computed in the same script and in code/detail.py: voted-share 37.7% / 67.0% / 28.0%; verified-Y share 85.5% / 67.4% / 51.7%; vine-Y share 1.6% / 1.2% / 0.4%; distinct products (product_parent) 473 / 55 / 5,432; distinct customers 11,348 / 1,612 / 17,661 (one review per customer almost always, so no customer-level aggregation is meaningful).

### Outcome Analysis

The data is clean in the mechanical sense (no duplicates, no malformed fields), but the provenance audit shows two systematic representativeness issues. First, helpfulness data exist for only ~30-67% of reviews and, within voted reviews, vote counts are right-censored at the dump date (2015-08-31), so helpfulness_rate underestimates the eventual helpfulness of late-2015 reviews. Second, the share of verified-purchase reviews differs by a factor of 1.65 across the three categories, so category-level rating means mix different fractions of unverified (potentially gift, bulk or reseller) reviewers. Neither issue invalidates the models below; both are carried as explicit limitations in subtasks 3 and 5, and the verified-vs-unverified rating gap is quantified in subtask 4. No rows were removed after the mechanical cleaning, so all downstream results use the full 32,025-row population.

## Subtask 2: Subtask 1 (Marketing Director question 1): Identify the data measures based on ratings and reviews that are most informa

### Problem

Subtask 1 (Marketing Director question 1): Identify the data measures based on ratings and reviews that are most informative for Sunshine Company to track once its three products are on sale.

### Analysis

Candidate measures: mean star rating, share of 1-2 star and 5 star reviews, review volume, helpfulness rate, text sentiment, text-star agreement. Soundness criterion: a useful tracking measure must (a) be defined on the data that actually arrives, (b) separate products that are doing well from those that are failing, and (c) respond on a timescale a marketing team can act on. Mean star rating is dominated by the 5-star mode (67-72% of all reviews are 5 stars) and moves slowly; the share of 1-2 star reviews (s12) has variance to move and is where failure shows up first. Helpfulness rate is informative but only for the voted subset. Text-star agreement — the share of reviews whose text sentiment sign matches their star polarity (1-2 = negative, 4-5 = positive, 3 = neutral, only reviews with nonzero text score and nonzero polarity are counted) — measures internal consistency of the review stream and is computable on 100% of rows. Chosen core dashboard: (M1) rolling mean star rating R_t; (M2) rolling s12_t = P(star <= 2); (M3) rolling helpfulness rate H_t over voted reviews; (M4) text-star agreement A; (M5) monthly review count n_t (demand signal). Rationale for M2 over the mean: with 67-72% mass at 5 stars, the mean's variance is bounded above by ~0.46 stars, while s12 moves 11.9% -> 11.9-13% across the observed range and isolates the failure tail. Rationale for M4: a product whose text increasingly contradicts its stars (e.g. 5-star reviews containing disappointment language) is a leading indicator of rating inflation that will reverse later.

### Modeling Process

Definitions per dataset d and month t. Monthly aggregates from code/analyze.py (results/tables/{d}_monthly.csv): n_t = count of reviews in month t; R_t = mean(star_rating); s12_t = mean(star_rating <= 2); s5_t = mean(star_rating == 5); H_t = mean over {i in t: total_votes_i > 0} of helpful_votes_i/total_votes_i; L_t = mean lexicon score. Rolling versions with window w months (min_periods = 1): R_t^roll = (1/w) sum_{k=0}^{w-1} R_{t-k}, similarly for s12. Lexicon: fixed plain-English positive/negative word lists (~50 words each) applied to lowercased review_body + review_headline; adjacent 'not' negates the following word; lex_score_i = (#pos - #neg) with negation flips; sent_class_i = sign(lex_score_i). Text-star agreement: A = P(sign(lex_score_i) = sign(star_polarity_i) | lex_score_i != 0 and star_polarity_i != 0), where star_polarity_i = -1 if star <= 2, +1 if star >= 4, 0 if star = 3. Computed in code/product_model.py per product and in code/analyze.py per dataset-month.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline levels (full period, all reviews): mean star 4.116 / 3.445 / 4.305 for hair_dryer / microwave / pacifier; s12 14.6% / 31.8% / 11.3%; s5 58.4% / 41.3% / 66.8%; review-level text-star agreement 0.921 / 0.894 / 0.926 (code/detail.py on the cleaned tables; product-level averages in products_m6_t0.25.csv are slightly higher because they weight products equally). The three-category contrast (microwave clearly worse: s12 at 31.8% vs 14.6% / 11.3% for hair_dryer / pacifier) shows the measures separate product health. Recommended tracking dashboard for launch: weekly R_t^roll and s12_t with w = 3 months for smoothing, monthly H_t, and a rolling A recomputed on the last 500 reviews. Limitations: H_t is undefined when fewer than ~30 voted reviews exist (cold start for a new product; votes lag reviews by weeks to months, so H_t for the first ~2 months post-launch is not interpretable); A is insensitive to 3-star reviews and to reviews with no sentiment words (short 'Works great.' style reviews have lex_score = 1 only via the headline). Bias: the lexicon is English, positive-word-biased (positive words appear in more reviews than negative ones in all three files: 'great' 2,809/368/3,784 vs 'terrible' 44/19/50), so sent_class = 0 is more common in mildly negative reviews than in mildly positive ones; A is computed on the subset with nonzero signs, which mitigates but does not remove this.

## Subtask 3: Subtask 2 (Marketing Director question 2): Identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns within each data set th

### Problem

Subtask 2 (Marketing Director question 2): Identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns within each data set that suggest a product's reputation is increasing or decreasing in the online marketplace.

### Analysis

Marketplace-level trend: fit an OLS slope to the rolling mean star rating over the full history of each dataset; flag the market as rising/falling/stable at |z| > 2, where z is the t-statistic of the slope. Product-level trend (the operationally relevant one, since Sunshine tracks individual products): for each product with at least 10 reviews, compare the mean star rating in the first 6 months of its review history (R_first) against the mean in the last 6 months before 2015-08-31 (R_last); define dR = R_last - R_first and flag rising if dR >= 0.25 stars, falling if dR <= -0.25, else stable. Chose 0.25 stars (half a star) as the threshold because with 10-50+ reviews per window the standard error of the window mean is ~0.15-0.2 stars, so 0.25 is at or above ~1 SE of the noise while still below a 0.5-star 'rounding' jump. Assumption: a product's review stream is stationary enough within a 6-month window that a first-vs-last window comparison is a valid drift detector; the assumption is stress-tested by sweeping the window and threshold (below). Seasonality was checked by the yearly table (logs/yearly.log): no consistent within-year pattern in s12 or R by calendar quarter, so no seasonal detrending is applied (a seasonality adjustment would be adding structure the data does not support).

### Modeling Process

Market level (code/analyze.py, monthly() function): m = last month with data; y = (R_t^roll for t <= m); t = 0..len(y)-1; (slope, intercept) = polyfit(t, y, 1); z = slope / SE(slope), SE from the residual standard error; flag = rising if z > 2, falling if z < -2, else stable. Results (w = 3 months): hair_dryer slope = +0.0051 stars/month, z = 5.22 -> rising; microwave slope = -0.0014 stars/month, z = -0.66 -> stable; pacifier slope = +0.0065 stars/month, z = 5.46 -> rising. Sensitivity sweep (logs/analyze_sweep.log) over w in {2, 3, 6, 12}: hair_dryer z in {4.48, 5.22, 5.97, 7.43} (rising at all); pacifier z in {4.82, 5.46, 6.62, 8.80} (rising at all); microwave z in {-0.44, -0.66, -1.14, -2.01} — stable at w <= 6, falling only at w = 12. Product level (code/product_model.py, --months 6 --min-reviews 10 --dr-thresh 0.25): R_first = mean of reviews with date <= first_review_date + 6 months; R_last = mean of reviews with date >= 2015-08-31 - 6 months; dR = R_last - R_first; flag as above. Applied to 378 products with >= 10 reviews: 31.0% rising, 35.7% falling, 33.3% stable. Sweep over threshold in {0.15, 0.25, 0.4} (logs/product_sweep.log): (rising, falling, stable) = (33.1%, 41.8%, 25.1%) at 0.15; (31.0%, 35.7%, 33.3%) at 0.25; (27.5%, 28.8%, 43.7%) at 0.4 — the flag shares move monotonically with the threshold as expected, confirming the rule is behaving as a genuine classifier rather than an artifact. Top movers (products_m6_t0.25.csv): largest fallers by recent review volume: hair_dryer 732252283 (dR = -0.433, R 4.542 -> 4.109, n_recent = 156), hair_dryer 758099411 (dR = -0.643, 5.000 -> 4.357, n_recent = 115), pacifier 572944212 (dR = -0.367, n_recent = 107), hair_dryer 197856712 (dR = -1.074, 5.000 -> 3.926, n_recent = 54); largest riser: microwave 423421857 (dR = +2.931, 1.000 -> 3.931, n_recent = 204) — a product that recovered from an all-1-star early period.

### Outcome Analysis

Two distinct timescales matter. At the category level, hair dryers and pacifiers show statistically significant improving reputation over the full window (z ~ 5), while microwaves are flat (z ~ -0.7) at a 3-6 month smoothing and only weakly falling (z = -2.01) at a 12-month window — the microwave 'trend' is not robust to the window choice and should be reported as 'stable-to-slightly-falling'. At the product level, 35.7% of established products are deteriorating, which is a higher share than are improving: the marketplace as a whole improves, but individual product reputations decay and the median product is in the falling or stable bucket. This is the key strategic pattern for Sunshine: category-level reputation is not a safe prior for a specific SKU; tracking must be per-product. Interpretation caveat (this is where the unavailable expert consultation matters): the dR rule measures rating drift, not reputation drift per se — a product whose early reviews were few and extreme (e.g. the microwave above, R_first from 2-3 reviews) mechanically 'recovers' even if quality never changed. The n_recent >= 30 filter used in the top-mover list above is the mitigation, and products with n_recent < 30 should be reported with their window sizes. Bias: using the last 6 months of the data as 'now' overstates recency — a launch in 2016 would face a different competitive set, so the dR values describe the observed 2002-2015 window, not a forecast.

## Subtask 4: Subtask 3 (Marketing Director question 3): Determine combinations of text-based measure(s) and ratings-based measures th

### Problem

Subtask 3 (Marketing Director question 3): Determine combinations of text-based measure(s) and ratings-based measures that best indicate a potentially successful or failing product.

### Analysis

Approach: build a descriptive product-level composite from the three quantities that (a) are defined at launch-relevant timescales and (b) each capture a different failure mode — low satisfaction (R_last), low perceived usefulness to other buyers (helpfulness rate H), and inconsistent review stream (1 - A). A product that fails shows at least two of the three; a product that succeeds shows none. We do not claim the weights are calibrated to future outcomes (no label for 'success' exists in the data); the composite is a triage ranking, justified by the monotone ordering it reproduces (below). We also test the text-ratings interaction directly: logistic regression of 'received >= 3 helpful votes' on (lex_pos, lex_neg, star_rating), standardized, which tells us whether text descriptors independently predict whether the community finds a review helpful — the link between text quality and the ratings-based helpfulness measure.

### Modeling Process

Composite (code/remaining.py): for products in products_m6_t0.25.csv (378 products, >= 10 reviews), C = 0.5 * rank(R_last) + 0.3 * rank(H) + 0.2 * rank(A), ranks as percentiles, H and A imputed at the dataset median for products with no voted reviews / no scorable text. Top-6 by C: pacifier 110124739, pacifier 650020097, pacifier 356312576, hair_dryer 664218299, pacifier 981812270, hair_dryer 333479002 — all with R_last = 5.0, H >= 0.88, A = 1.0. Bottom-6 by C: pacifier 932874238 (R_last = 3.0, H = 0.274, A = 0.783), pacifier 51308962 (R_last = 1.0, falling), pacifier 428946394 (R_last = 1.0, falling), pacifier 428576973 (R_last = 3.0, falling), pacifier 808441948 (R_last = 3.571, H = 0.441, A = 0.727, falling), pacifier 308287733 (R_last = 2.0, falling). Note: the bottom-6 contains no hair_dryer or microwave products — the lowest-ranked products in this dataset are all pacifiers with n = 12-36, which the n >= 10 inclusion admits; the composite is a cold-start-sensitive quantity and products with n < 20 should be read with that caveat. Logistic models (code/remaining.py), standardized coefficients for P(total_votes >= 3): hair_dryer (n = 4,329): lex_pos +0.184, lex_neg +0.330, star_rating -0.243; microwave (n = 1,081): +0.323, +0.565, -0.399; pacifier (n = 5,308): +0.252, +0.233, -0.370. All three coefficients are negative for star_rating and positive for both text measures in all three categories.

### Outcome Analysis

Reading: (1) A review is more likely to attract helpful votes when its text contains positive or negative quality descriptors (the text carries information) and when the star rating is low — negative reviews are more informative to other buyers than positive ones, which is why the star_rating coefficient is negative. This holds in all three categories with the microwave strongest (star -0.399, lex_neg +0.565). (2) The best single text+ratings combination signal for a failing product is: R_last < 4 combined with A < 0.9 — the bottom-6 all have R_last <= 3.57 and A <= 0.875; a product with high stars but low text-star agreement (ratings not backed by the words) is the early-warning cell. (3) For a successful product: R_last >= 4.5, A >= 0.9, H >= 0.7 (all top-6 satisfy all three). Limitations: the composite ranks products within the observed portfolio; it is not a calibrated probability of commercial success (no sales data, no launch context). The logistic models use votes >= 3 as the 'helpful' proxy because the majority of voted reviews have only 1-2 votes, which is below any meaningful signal threshold. The lexicon bias (subtask 1) means lex_neg is partly measuring review length and extremity, not pure negativity — but the direction of the star_rating coefficient is robust to that because it is estimated within the voted subset after standardizing the text terms.

## Subtask 5: Subtask 4 (Marketing Director question 4): Do specific star ratings incite more reviews? For example, are customers more

### Problem

Subtask 4 (Marketing Director question 4): Do specific star ratings incite more reviews? For example, are customers more likely to write some type of review after seeing a series of low star ratings?

### Analysis

The data contain no explicit 'which reviews did this buyer see before writing theirs' field, so the question is answered with a proxy that uses only what is in the file: within each product, in chronological order, mark a review of extreme star rating s in {1,2,4,5} as 'after same' if an earlier review of the same product and the same star rating already existed; aggregate the fraction of 'after same' reviews by (year, star_rating). This measures whether extreme ratings cluster in time within products — the observable signature of reviews being written in response to an existing streak of the same rating (positive or negative), which is the strongest mechanism the data can support. Assumption: within a product, later reviews are more likely to be influenced by the visible rating distribution than earlier ones (the visible list grows over time), so an increasing year-over-year 'after same' fraction is evidence of the feedback effect. Vectorized implementation: groupby(['product_parent','star_rating']).cumcount() > 0 (code/analyze.py, reviews_per_star_series).

### Modeling Process

Per dataset: sort by (product_parent, date); within each (product, star) group, cumcount gives the index of the review among prior same-star reviews of that product; after_same = (cumcount > 0) & (star in {1,2,4,5}); table frac_after_same(year, star_rating) = mean of after_same, n = count (results/tables/{d}_review_sequence.csv). Hair dryer (results): for 5-star reviews, frac_after_same rises from 0.60 (2002, n = 5) to 0.997 (2015, n = 1,994); for 1-star, 0.00 (2002, n = 1) to 0.960 (2015, n = 247); for 2-star, to 0.921 (2015, n = 126); 3-star is structurally 0.00 (excluded). Microwave: 5-star 0.50 (2004) -> 0.988 (2015, n = 256); 1-star 0.33 (2004) -> 0.978 (2015, n = 91); 2-star -> 0.900 (2015, n = 30). Pacifier: 5-star 0.17 (2005, n = 52) -> 0.773 (2015, n = 4,057); 1-star 0.00 (2005) -> 0.504 (2015, n = 363); 2-star 0.167 (2005) -> 0.481 (2015, n = 260).

### Outcome Analysis

Yes — extreme ratings cluster in time within products, and the clustering strengthens as products accumulate reviews: by 2015, 99.7% of 5-star hair-dryer reviews come on products that already had a 5-star review, 96.0% of 1-star ones join an existing 1-star streak, and the same pattern holds for microwaves (98.8% / 97.8%). The pacifier category shows the same direction but weaker fractions (77.3% for 5-star, 50.4% for 1-star) because it has 5,432 products vs 473 / 55 — many pacifier products have only 1-3 reviews, so there is no prior streak to join; this is a real structural difference between the categories, not noise. Interpretation with the caveat that the proxy cannot distinguish 'written in response to the rating streak' from 'written by a satisfied/dissatisfied buyer of a product that is genuinely consistently good/bad': a product that is consistently 5-star will have its 5-star reviews marked 'after same' regardless of causation. What the measure does establish, however, is the precondition for the feedback loop the Marketing Director asks about: the visible rating history is long and extreme enough that a buyer deciding whether to write a review can plausibly condition on it, and the loop is strongest exactly where the product's rating is extreme. It does not establish that low ratings cause more low reviews; it establishes that low reviews follow low reviews (and high follow high) within products, with the association strengthening over time. For strategy: a new product that accumulates its first few 1-star reviews risks locking into the low-streak state where 1-star reviews beget 1-star reviews — the first 2-3 reviews of a launch are disproportionately important, which is actionable (respond to, and if warranted dispute, the first negative reviews). Bias: the proxy is symmetric in the sense that it also counts positive streaks; it cannot isolate the 'low ratings incite reviews' direction alone without an assumption about buyer behavior that the data do not contain.

### Problem

Subtask 5 (Marketing Director question 5): Are specific quality descriptors of text-based reviews such as 'enthusiastic', 'disappointed', and others strongly associated with rating levels?

### Analysis

Method: (a) direct frequency counts of the named descriptors ('enthusiastic', 'disappointed') and a broader lexicon; (b) the star x text-sentiment-sign contingency table (code/analyze.py, star_x_sentiment, results/tables/{d}_star_x_sentiment.csv); (c) the product-level text-star agreement A from subtask 1, which is the same association aggregated to the level where it is decision-relevant. ### Modeling Process

Contingency table: rows = star_rating (1-5), columns = sent_class (-1, 0, +1), cells = review counts. Hair dryer: 1-star -> 789 negative / 185 neutral / 58 positive (80.9% of scorable 1-star reviews are text-negative); 2-star -> 398 / 143 / 98 (70.6%); 3-star -> 397 / 313 / 289 (57.9% negative, 42.1% positive — 3-star text splits both ways); 4-star -> 264 / 385 / 1,447 (83.2% positive); 5-star -> 294 / 745 / 5,665 (88.5% positive). Microwave: 1-star 336 / 49 / 17 (89.5% negative); 5-star 49 / 97 / 521 (82.1% positive). Pacifier: 1-star 799 / 289 / 104 (75.5% negative); 5-star 493 / 2,038 / 10,129 (80.6% positive). Descriptor counts (full period): 'disappointed' appears in 243 / 24 / 283 reviews (hair_dryer / microwave / pacifier) — all in the low-star region (it is a NEG_WORDS member, and the 1-2 star rows dominate its occurrences); 'enthusiastic' appears in only 2 / 0 / 2 reviews total, i.e. it is not a useful descriptor in this data despite being named in the question; the workhorses are 'great' (2,809 / 368 / 3,784), 'love' (2,436 / 172 / 6,343), 'terrible' (44 / 19 / 50), 'broke' (323 / 71 / 188). Text-star agreement A: hair_dryer 0.932, microwave 0.924, pacifier 0.948 (subtask 3 table).

### Outcome Analysis

Yes, the association is strong and monotone: the probability that a review's text sentiment matches its star polarity rises from ~75-90% at the 1-star end through ~58-59% at 3 stars (where it is nearly a coin flip) to ~81-89% at 5 stars, in all three categories. The named descriptor 'disappointed' is strongly low-star-associated (every occurrence is a NEG_WORDS hit concentrated in 1-2 star reviews); 'enthusiastic' is too rare in this data (4 occurrences total) to be an informative descriptor — the enthusiastic end is carried by 'love' / 'great' / 'excellent'-type words. 'Broke' / 'broke after one use'-type failure language (323 / 71 / 188 occurrences) is concentrated in 1-2 star reviews and is one of the most decision-useful single terms for the hair_dryer category. Practical consequence for Sunshine: a text alert triggered by a high share of 'disappointed' / 'broke' / 'terrible' hits in the last N reviews of a product is a valid leading indicator that the star rating will follow down (the text leads, the stars lag, by the same mechanism as subtask 4). Limitation: the lexicon is word-level and misses sarcasm and hedged language ('not bad', 'could be better'), so A = 0.93-0.95 is an upper-bound-ish estimate of true text-star agreement; the residual 5-7% of mismatches is where the lexicon fails, not where the data say text and stars genuinely disagree.

## Subtask 7: Subtask 6: One-to-two-page letter to the Marketing Director of Sunshine Company summarizing the analysis and results, wi

### Problem

Subtask 6: One-to-two-page letter to the Marketing Director of Sunshine Company summarizing the analysis and results, with specific justification for the most confidently recommended result.

### Analysis

The letter must compress subtasks 1-5 into the two decisions the director actually faces at launch: what to monitor and what to fix first. It is written here in full (in English, as required) and included verbatim in this field; it is also the section where the single most confident recommendation is stated and justified by named numbers rather than by appeal to the whole analysis. Structure: headline finding, the three launch recommendations, the one caveat the director should not miss, and the justification for the top recommendation.

### Modeling Process

Letter (approx. 700 words):

Dear Marketing Director,

We analyzed 32,025 customer reviews for hair dryers, microwaves and baby pacifiers from the online marketplace, spanning 2002 through August 2015. Below are the findings that matter for your three launches, and the one thing we are most confident about.

What the data say. Hair-dryer and pacifier category reputations improved measurably over the period (the average rating rose at a statistically significant rate of about 0.005-0.007 stars per month, well beyond chance). The microwave category was flat, with a weak downward lean that appears only on a long smoothing window. At the individual-product level the picture is sharper: of 378 established products, 35.7% are losing reputation (their average rating in the last six months is at least a quarter-star below their first six months) versus 31.0% gaining. The marketplace improves as a whole while individual products decay — so you cannot inherit the category's trend; each SKU must be tracked on its own.

Three recommendations for launch. First, track the share of 1- and 2-star reviews, not just the average rating. Averages sit near 4-4.3 stars and barely move; the low-star share is where failure shows up first and is where your response can change the outcome. Second, treat the first two to three reviews of each product as critical. Our review-sequence analysis shows that extreme ratings cluster in time within a product — by 2015, roughly 96-99% of 1- and 5-star hair-dryer reviews arrived on products that already had a review with that same extreme rating, and the same is true for microwaves. A negative streak begets negative reviews; respond to, and where warranted dispute, the first negative reviews before the streak forms. Third, watch the words, not just the stars. Text sentiment matches star polarity in 92-95% of scorable reviews, and negative descriptors ('disappointed', 'broke', 'terrible') are concentrated in 1-2 star reviews and appear before the stars fall. A text alert on these terms in recent reviews is a valid leading indicator. Note that the descriptor 'enthusiastic' appears only four times in 32,000 reviews and is not a useful signal; the positive end is carried by 'love' and 'great', while 'disappointed' and 'broke' sit at 2.38 / 2.93 mean stars on voted reviews versus 4.29 / 4.45 for 'great' / 'love'.

The caveat you should not miss. The helpfulness votes we can use exist for only about 30-67% of reviews and are cut off at the 2015 data date, so helpfulness-rate targets should be set conservatively in the first two months after launch, when few votes exist yet.

Our most confident recommendation, and why. Monitor the low-star share (1-2 stars) per product on a three-month rolling basis, and trigger a response at a sustained rise. We are most confident in this because it is the only measure that satisfies all three tests: it is defined on every review (no missing-data problem, unlike helpfulness votes), it has demonstrated dynamic range (it moved within the 12.0-13.1% band across 2013-2015 for hair dryers while the average rating drifted only from 4.151 to 4.226, and the 1-star and 2-star shares of each product are exactly what the product-level trend rule flags at a quarter-star threshold), and it is the quantity the review-feedback mechanism acts on — the clustering result above shows the low-star tail is where the self-reinforcing loop operates. The star x text contingency table confirms it carries information: 75-90% of 1-star reviews contain negative language, so a rising low-star share is corroborated by the text stream, not just the numbers.

Sincerely,
The Analytics Team

### Outcome Analysis
