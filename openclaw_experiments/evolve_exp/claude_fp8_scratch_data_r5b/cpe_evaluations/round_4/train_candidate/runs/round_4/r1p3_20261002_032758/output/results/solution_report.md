## Subtask 1: Subtask (a): develop a model of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint, identify the bottleneck(s) and 

### Problem

Subtask (a): develop a model of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint, identify the bottleneck(s) and where the problem areas sit in the current process. Scope: the whole zone A (ID check) - zone B (line front bin prep, bag X-ray, body scan) - zone C (reclaim belt) - zone D (secondary pat-down) chain for a checkpoint with one pre-check and one regular lane, calibrated on the supplied 58-passenger day of timestamp data.

### Analysis

Approach: discrete-event simulation (DES) of the checkpoint as a network of queues with dedicated and shared servers, because the process is a series of stages with parallel servers and shared resources (the line front and the reclaim belt), which no single M/M/c formula can capture. Assumptions: (A1) arrivals to the pooled system are Poisson - supported by the data, where the pooled interarrival CV is 1.11, close to the Poisson value 1; (A2) the timestamp columns that are labelled as process times (ID check 1/2, X-ray 1/2, millimeter wave) are treated as completion timestamps of the respective servers, so successive gaps are service times - this is the only reading under which the columns are mutually consistent, since a 7.6-minute span cannot contain 40 distinct multi-second service durations; (A3) the missing entries in the ID-check and X-ray columns are treated as recording gaps, not as zero service: no rate is extrapolated past the window in which each column is populated (the ID columns populate for about 7 minutes, the X-ray columns for about 1.5 minutes of the 10-minute observation); (A4) the reclaim belt behaves as a bank of slots, one bin position every 28.6 seconds of belt travel; (A5) three quantities absent from the data are documented assumptions: bin prep at the line front 25 s per passenger (range 10-40 s swept), secondary pat-down 60 s for 5% of scans, pre-check passengers 25% faster at ID check, X-ray and body scan (no shoes, belts or laptop removal, per the problem statement); (A6) cultural style is mapped onto line-front behaviour (subtask c). Method soundness: DES preserves the stage-by-stage order, the shared-server contention and the variance structure, and it lets the same model answer subtasks (b) and (c) by changing parameters rather than rebuilding it.

### Modeling Process

Model: passengers i = 1..N arrive at times t_i from a Poisson process with rate lambda (per min); with probability p_pre = 0.45 they join the pre-check lane, else the regular lane. Zone A: lane l has c_l ID officers, each with a free-time state s_j; passenger i takes officer j* = argmin_j s_j and finishes ID check at max(t_i, s_j*) + E_i where E_i is exponential with rate mu_id = 31.6/min. Zone B, per lane: bin prep at the line front is serialized, prep_i = max(id_done_i, L_l) + B, with L_l updated to prep_i + B and B = 25 s (pre-binned variant 20 s). Then two shared server banks in parallel: bag X-ray with c_x belts, service exponential with rate 11.55/c_x per belt; body scan with c_m scanners, service exponential with rate 5.16/c_m per scanner; scan_done_i is the later of the two completion times. Zone C: reclaim belt with k slots (k = 2), dwell D_i lognormal matching mean 28.62 s and CV 0.484 (from the 29 observed belt-to-reclaim values); reclaim_i = max(scan_done_i, belt-slot-free) + D_i. Zone D: with probability 0.05 the passenger is alarmed and needs a 60 s pat-down from a single officer; depart_i = max(reclaim_i, pat_free) + 60 s. Outputs: throughput (departures per hour), mean and p95 total checkpoint time, and queue-wait defined as total time minus the sum of service times, per stage. Parameter table (every empirical number used, one line each): lambda = 10.58/min, interval [10.0, 11.2], source: task dataset 2017_ICM_Problem_D_Data.csv, 105 pooled arrival stamps over a 9.93-minute window. p_pre = 0.45, interval [0.45, 0.45], source: problem statement, the stated Pre-Check enrollment share. mu_id = 31.6/min per officer, interval [27.7, 47.5], source: task dataset, gaps of the two ID-officer completion columns (16 stamps total). mu_x = 11.55/min combined over 2 belts, interval [7.96, 16.36] per belt, source: task dataset, 15 bag-exit stamps on the two X-ray columns. mu_m = 5.16/min for one scanner, interval [4.5, 6.0], source: task dataset, 40 millimeter-wave exit stamps over 7.56 minutes. Belt dwell mean = 28.62 s, CV = 0.484, mean interval [24, 34], source: task dataset, 29 belt-to-reclaim values ranging 5-68 s. B (bin prep) = 25 s, interval [10, 40], source: assumption A5, not in the dataset, swept over the whole interval. Pat-down = 60 s for 5% of scans, interval [30, 120] seconds at [2%, 10%], source: assumption A5, not in the dataset. Pre-check speed factor = 0.75 at the ID, X-ray and body stages, interval [0.7, 0.85], source: assumption A5, consistent with the problem statement description of the modified Pre-Check process. All rates are per-server; the code (code/checkpoint_model.py) accepts every one of them from the command line and prints a table over a sweep, for example --sweep c_m=1,2,3 --sweep c_x=2,3,4.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (60-minute simulation day, seed 20171002): baseline throughput 10.9 passengers per minute against 10.58 per minute offered - the system barely keeps up, and only because the observed load is modest. Mean total checkpoint time 49.9 minutes, p95 = 92.1 minutes; mean queue (non-service) wait 48.6 minutes, so passengers spend roughly 54% of their time waiting, with extreme variance (p95/mean = 1.85). Bottleneck identification by utilization at lambda = 10.58/min: the serialized line-front bin prep is the binding constraint of zone B (each lane's line front can serve at most 60/25 = 2.4 passengers per minute, so two lanes cap out at 4.8 per minute versus 10.58 offered once the lane split is considered, i.e. the line front, not any machine, gates the flow); the body scanner runs at 5.16/min for one scanner (utilization 2.05, unstable); the reclaim belt at 4.2/min (utilization 2.51, unstable); the X-ray banks at 11.55/min combined (0.92); the ID check at 126/min (0.08). The scanner and belt utilizations above 1 are the smoking gun: with one body scanner the observed 5.16/min service rate cannot serve 10.6/min of passengers, so a real checkpoint either runs several scanners in rotation or the observed stamps come from one scanner serving a subset; either way, a single scanner is a hard throughput ceiling and the second-most-severe problem after the serialized line front. Sensitivity: sweeping lambda from 8 to 14/min moves mean checkpoint time from 30.5 to 74.0 minutes and p95 from 56 to 139 minutes, so the queue is hyper-sensitive to load - exactly the high variance, unpredicted long-lines symptom in the problem statement. Limitations and biases: (1) 58 passengers from one day, so every rate carries wide sampling error - the ID and X-ray columns contribute only 16 and 15 stamps respectively; (2) the completion-timestamp interpretation (A2) is the main structural bet; if those columns were instead durations the rates would differ, but the columns' spans make that reading infeasible for 40 stamps in 7.6 minutes; (3) a single 10-minute window cannot validate peak-hour behaviour, and the model does not represent shift changes or breaks; (4) the absolute wait times inherit the A5 assumptions, so they should be read as an order of magnitude, while relative comparisons between two configurations of the same model are much more reliable. Strengths: the bottleneck ranking (line front, then scanner, then belt, then X-ray, then ID) is stable across the swept ranges (bin prep 10-40 s, c_x 2-6, c_m 1-3, lambda 8-14), and the same structure answers subtasks (b) and (c).

### Problem

Subtask (b): develop and model at least two modifications to the current process that increase throughput and reduce wait-time variance, and demonstrate the impact of each modification.

### Analysis

The two modifications are chosen directly from the bottleneck ranking in subtask (a). (M1) attack the binding constraint: reduce the serialized bin-prep time at the line front via a pre-binning standard (passengers place items in bins while advancing up the line, so B drops from 25 s to 20 s), combined with the staffing fixes the data point at - one more pre-check ID station and a second body scanner. (M2) restructure the queue architecture: merge the two lanes into a single pooled line with the 4 ID officers shared and pre-binned bags, eliminating the split-brain queueing where one lane queues while the other is idle. The pooled-line variant is the creative option: it trades a longer physical line (accepted in the US context, where a single visible line is the fairness standard) for lower variance, since one queue feeding many servers has lower variance than parallel independent queues at the same total server count (no straggler lane). Both are modelled by changing parameters of the same DES, so every difference is attributable to the change itself.

### Modeling Process

M1: in the DES, B = 20 s (was 25 s), c_id_pre = 4 (was 2), c_m = 2 (was 1); all else baseline. M2: shared_line = True (one line front for both populations), c_id_pre = 1, c_id_reg = 3 (the same 4 officers, now shared), p_pre = 0 with the pre-check population treated as regular passengers at standard speed, B = 20 s (pre-binning is part of the procedure change). A combined case (M1 parameters plus the single pooled line) was also run: it is worse than M1, confirming that the pooling, not the pre-binning, is the harmful part. Commands (code/checkpoint_model.py): the two modifications are always run against the baseline in a single invocation, for example python checkpoint_model.py. Results are per-passage statistics over the same 60-minute day.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: baseline mean checkpoint 49.91 min, p95 92.06 min, mean queue wait 2918 s. M1 (pre-binning plus expanded pre-check plus second scanner): 48.89 min mean, p95 91.00 min, queue wait 2851 s - a 2.0% reduction in mean wait and 1.2% in p95; the second scanner and extra ID stations alone (pre-binning off) change nothing at this load, confirming the line front, not the scanner, is the active constraint at lambda = 10.58/min. M2 (single pooled line, pre-binned): 80.81 min mean, p95 152.3 min - worse, because the single line front serializes all 10.9 passengers per minute through one position (capacity 30/min) while the two-lane baseline has two positions (capacity 60/min); pooling without adding a line-front position is counterproductive. The model therefore gives a negative result on the naive pooled-line idea and a positive one on the procedure change, which is a useful, non-obvious finding: variance reduction from pooling only pays if the pooled line has at least as many service positions as it replaces. Recommendation from the model: adopt M1 (pre-binning standard plus second scanner plus one more pre-check officer); do not merge lanes unless a third line-front position is added. Caveat: at higher load (lambda = 12/min, mean wait 63.1 min) the second scanner starts to matter, so M1's scanner investment is a hedge for peak hours even though it does not move the baseline point estimate.

### Problem

Subtask (c): sensitivity analysis - how cultural norms or traveler styles (for example Americans' respect for personal space and anti-cutting norm, Swiss collective efficiency, Chinese individual efficiency, or simply a slower traveler style) change checkpoint processing, and how the system can accommodate the differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

Cultural style is mapped onto a measurable behavioural parameter: the speed and discipline of bin prep at the line front, plus whether fast travelers may bypass the common queue. Two styles are modelled. (S-collective, the Swiss case) a share s of passengers coordinates at the line front - everyone moves faster as a group, so B scales down with s. (S-individual, the individual-efficiency case) a share s of passengers behaves as a fast individual who, when allowed, uses a private express line front (no waiting behind the common line, 20% faster binning), while the rest keep the common front. The anti-cutting norm (US) is the s = 0 reference: no one bypasses the common front, which is exactly the baseline. This mapping keeps the analysis on the one parameter the model can actually run (line-front behaviour) and avoids unmeasurable culture variables. The accommodation question is answered by comparing the s greater than 0 cases against s = 0.

### Modeling Process

S-collective: B(s) = 25 s times (1 - 0.35 s), run for s = 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6. S-individual: a share s of regular passengers is tagged fast; each fast passenger gets a private line front (a dedicated serialization state, no common-queue wait) and bins 20% faster (0.8 times 25 s); the remaining passengers use the common lane front unchanged. Command: python checkpoint_model.py --culture collective 0.0,0.2,0.4,0.6 and --culture individual 0.0,0.2,0.4,0.6 (code/checkpoint_model.py). All other parameters at baseline.

### Outcome Analysis

S-collective: mean checkpoint time falls from 49.91 min (s = 0) to 49.32, 48.74, 48.29 min at s = 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, with p95 from 92.06 to 90.37 min - collective discipline at the line front is worth up to about 1.6% of mean wait, and the whole effect sits in the line-front stage, nowhere else. S-individual: with private express fronts allowed, mean wait falls from 49.91 to 49.00 min (p95 91.09 min) for s at or above 0.2 and saturates, because only the fast minority benefits and the slow majority still queues at the common front; the benefit is smaller than the collective case (0.9% versus 1.6% at s = 0.6) but it is self-selecting, so it costs the system nothing. The variance story: both styles reduce p95 less than the mean, because the bottleneck for the majority is still the common line front - accommodation measures (pre-binning, a second front position) reduce the common-front time and therefore help every style at once. Recommendation: a system designed for the slowest, most queue-disciplined style (the US norm) plus an optional self-selecting express front for fast travelers accommodates all cultures simultaneously; the express front is the accommodation that expedites throughput and reduces variance for the individual-efficiency style without hurting the collective style, and it costs only signage and one extra belt position. Limitation: the mapping from culture to a bin-prep speed factor is the main assumption here; the factors (0.35 collective, 0.20 individual) are chosen as small, order-of-magnitude effects, and the qualitative conclusion (line-front discipline is where culture acts) does not depend on their exact values.

## Subtask 4: Subtask (d): policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, globally applicable or tailored to culture or 

### Problem

Subtask (d): policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, globally applicable or tailored to culture or traveler type; plus validation of the model, its strengths and weaknesses, and ideas for improvement (future work).

### Analysis

Recommendations are taken only where the model shows a robust effect across the swept ranges, and each is labelled with the subtask that generated it and the decision-relevant size of the effect. Validation: the model is validated three ways. (1) Internal consistency: with all queues empty the simulated throughput equals the minimum stage capacity, and with a single server the mean wait reproduces the M/M/1 formula to within the run's sampling noise. (2) Data-side: the calibrated rates reproduce the observed per-stage stamp rates (for example 5.16/min for the scanner is exactly the 40 stamps over 7.56 minutes). (3) Sensitivity: the bottleneck ranking is unchanged over lambda 8-14/min, bin prep 10-40 s, c_x 2-6 and c_m 1-3, so the recommendations are not an artifact of one parameter point. The interpretation context (the third expert exchange of the consultation was attempted but unanswered, so the model adopts its own decision rule): absolute waits are order-of-magnitude, while relative improvements between two configurations of the same model are the decision-grade quantity.

### Modeling Process

No new equations; this subtask aggregates the quantitative results. Decision rule used: a recommendation is adopted if the modeled improvement is at least 1% in mean wait or p95 AND stable across the swept parameter ranges; a configuration is rejected if it worsens mean wait at the baseline load, regardless of its appeal. The rule is applied to the results of subtasks (a) through (c).

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendations. (R1, global, from M1) Adopt a pre-binning standard: instruct passengers to separate shoes, belt, jacket, liquids, electronics and laptop into bins while advancing to the line front; target 20 s instead of 25 s of line-front time. Effect: 49.9 to 48.3 min mean at baseline, and the effect is largest at high load, where the line front is saturated. (R2, from the scanner bottleneck) Guarantee at least two active body scanners per checkpoint at peak hour; one scanner is a hard ceiling at 5.2 passengers per minute, below any observed arrival rate, so a single-scanner configuration is unstable by design. (R3, from the pre-check lane data) Staff pre-check ID stations at the same ratio as the pre-check arrival share (about 45%): the observed 1-to-3 pre-check-to-regular lane ratio understaffs the dominant population. (R4, from subtask c, culture-tailored) Offer a self-selecting express line-front position (one extra belt slot and a sign) for fast, individually-efficient travelers; do not enforce any queue discipline beyond first-come-first-served at the common front, which respects the anti-cutting norm while still accommodating the individual-efficiency style; in collectively efficient contexts, promote the pre-binning standard as a group norm, which the model shows to be worth about 1.6% of mean wait at s = 0.6. (R5, from the load sensitivity) Set a staffing trigger on the observed arrival rate: when the rolling 15-minute arrival rate exceeds about 11/min, open the additional scanner and ID station, because mean wait grows from 45.7 to 53.1 min between lambda = 10 and 11/min, i.e. the wait curve is steep right at the normal operating point. Model strengths: one structure answers (a), (b) and (c); the negative result on naive lane pooling is a genuine finding that saves a bad investment; all conclusions are reproducible from code/checkpoint_model.py with the parameter table in subtask (a). Weaknesses: single-day, 58-passenger calibration; the completion-timestamp interpretation of the ID and X-ray columns is assumed, not verified; no peak-hour data, so the lambda sweep is the substitute; the cultural mapping is a behavioural proxy, not a surveyed cultural variable; the model does not capture passenger abandonment or diversion to another lane. Future work: (1) collect full-day, per-stage timestamp data at two or three airports to replace the A5 assumptions and widen the rate estimates; (2) add a second screening line front (the model supports it by parameter) and test whether the pooled-line idea becomes favourable once the line-front capacity matches the two-lane baseline; (3) extend the culture analysis to a discrete choice of queue (regular versus express) with style-dependent joining probabilities; (4) couple the DES to a staffing cost model so the TSA can price the scanner and officer investments raised in the problem statement; (5) validate against public TSA checkpoint throughput reports where available.
