## Subtask 1: Part (a): Develop a model to explore the flow of passengers through a security checkpoint and identify bottlenecks and p

### Problem

Part (a): Develop a model to explore the flow of passengers through a security checkpoint and identify bottlenecks and problem areas in the current process. Scope: characterize the two passenger streams (TSA Pre-Check and Regular), the per-stage service rates, and the shared equipment (ID-check officers, X-ray belt, millimeter-wave scanners), then locate the stages where demand exceeds capacity.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) each open screening lane is a parallel server and passengers clear one lane at a time, so a lane pool with c open lanes is an M/c queue; (2) the recorded ~10-minute window is a PEAK surge, not a representative hourly mean, so I bracket it by also evaluating at a scaled representative off-peak demand (15% of peak, the 'normally short wait' regime) and a moderate peak (50%); (3) the ID-check, X-ray and belt-to-belt columns have many missing rows (9/7, 11/4, 29 of ~58), which I treat as sensor dropout (missing-at-random), so I use only non-null rows to estimate each stage's rate — this cannot bias the bottleneck direction, only the absolute service-time estimate, which I bound with a literature range. Method: an M/M/c steady-state queueing model per lane pool plus a utilization scan of the shared single-server equipment. M/M/c is sound here because arrivals are approximately Poisson (passengers arrive independently) and service is memoryless to first order; I report the utilization rho=lambda/(c*mu) as the primary diagnostic because it is exact and does not depend on the exponential-tail approximation that the wait-time formulas do.

### Modeling Process

Measured from the dataset (analyze_data.py): Pre-Check arrival rate lambda_pre=391.8 pax/h, Regular arrival rate lambda_reg=278.1 pax/h (total 669.9 pax/h at the recorded peak); ID-check officer rate mu_id=871 pax/h (~4.1 s/interaction); mmWave scanner exit rate 309.4 pax/h (~11.6 s); X-ray belt exit rate 477.5 pax/h (~7.5 s); belt-to-belt passenger handling 28.6 s (mean). Calibrated per-lane service times: regular lane s_reg=55 s/pax (~65 pax/h), Pre-Check lane s_pre=30 s/pax (~120 pax/h), ID station s_id=8 s/pax. Parameter table (empirical inputs): s_reg=55 s, interval [45,65], source: doi:10.3390/aerospace7060069 (Data-Driven Analysis of Airport Security Checkpoint Operations) plus dataset col H belt-handling 28.6 s; s_pre=30 s, interval [25,40], source: doi:10.1002/hfm.20616 (checkpoint queuing network / discrete-event simulation) and problem statement (Pre-Check skips shoes/belt/jacket/computer); s_id=8 s, interval [5,10], source: doi:10.1109/access.2019.2946736 plus dataset cols C/D; per-officer mu_id=871 pax/h, from dataset col C (9 non-null rows); lane counts c_pre=1, c_reg=3 from problem statement ('one Pre-Check lane per three regular lanes'). Model: for a pool with arrival lambda (pax/h) and c servers of service time s (s/pax), single-server rate mu=3600/s pax/h, utilization rho=lambda/(c*mu); if rho>=1 the queue is unstable (wait grows without bound). For rho<1 the Erlang-C waiting probability is P(wait)=(a^c/c!)*(c/(c-a))*P0 with a=lambda/mu and P0 the normalization, mean queue wait Wq=P(wait)/(c/s*3600^{-1}-lambda/3600) s, P95 wait=-ln(0.05/P(wait))/(c/s- lambda/3600) s from the exponential tail, mean number in system L=lambda/3600*(Wq+s). Solution procedure: compute rho for each pool and each shared equipment stage; flag rho>0.85 as a near-bottleneck and rho>=1 as a binding (infeasible) bottleneck.

### Outcome Analysis

At the recorded peak (670 pax/h total): the single Pre-Check lane has rho=392/120=3.26 and the Regular 3-lane pool rho=278/195=1.43 — both UNSTABLE, i.e. demand exceeds capacity and waits diverge. The shared X-ray belt (478 pax/h) runs at 670/478=1.40 utilization and the mmWave scanner (309 pax/h) at 670/309=2.17 — both above 100%, so they are hard shared ceilings. The ID-check station is NOT a bottleneck (2 officers at 670/1742=0.38). At the representative off-peak (15%, ~100 pax/h) the system is stable: Pre-Check lane rho=0.49 (mean wait 28.8 s, P95 134 s), Regular pool rho=0.21 (mean wait 0.7 s), X-ray belt 0.21, mmWave 0.32. Conclusion: the bottleneck is demand-dependent and the recorded window is exactly the 'unexplained long lines' anomaly — a surge in which the single Pre-Check lane (and the shared belt/scanner) saturate while off-peak the same checkpoint has short waits. The Pre-Check pool is the most fragile: it overshoots one lane's capacity even at half the recorded surge (196 pax/h > 120 pax/h). Limitations/biases: the 10-minute window is a single sample, so 392/278 pax/h is a point estimate of a peak with no confidence interval; the missing-value columns force me to bound service times with a literature range rather than estimate them from a full day; M/M/c assumes Poisson arrivals and exponential service, which overstates the tail relative to the observed (more regular) inter-arrival spacing (median gap 4.8 s vs mean 9.2 s for Pre-Check), so the true P95 is likely somewhat lower than reported at high utilization.

### Problem

Part (b): Develop two or more modifications to the current process to improve throughput and reduce wait-time variance, and model how each modification changes the process.

### Analysis

I evaluate four candidate modifications against a common baseline at a moderate peak (50% of the recorded surge, Pre-Check 196 / Regular 139 pax/h), chosen because that is the regime where the fixed configuration is near capacity but still feasible, so the before/after contrast isolates each change. The two headline modifications are (M1) adding open screening lanes and (M2) adding a dedicated Pre-Check lane; I also model (M3) a Pre-Check-style speedup of the Regular lanes (drop the shoe/belt/jacket/computer-removal step, cutting per-lane service from 55 s to 40 s) and (M4) the combined package. Each is re-run through the same M/M/c engine, so the comparison is apples-to-apples: same demand, same arrival process, only the configuration or service time changes.

### Modeling Process

Baseline at moderate peak: c_pre=1, c_reg=3, s_pre=30 s, s_reg=55 s. M1: c_reg -> 4 and 5. M2: c_pre -> 2. M3: s_reg 55->40 s (throughput per regular lane 65->90 pax/h, +38%). M4: c_pre=2, c_reg=4, s_reg=40 s. For each, recompute rho, P(wait), Wq, P95 for both pools and the passenger-weighted mean wait tw=(lambda_pre*Wq_pre+lambda_reg*Wq_reg)/(lambda_pre+lambda_reg) and worst-pool P95. The variance-reduction claim is read off the P95 (95th-percentile wait) and the mean queue length L, not just the mean wait, because the problem asks to reduce VARIANCE in wait time.

### Outcome Analysis

At the moderate peak the Regular pool is the operative queue (the single Pre-Check lane is still infeasible at 196 pax/h, the Part-a finding). Baseline Regular: rho=0.71, mean wait 31.7 s, P95 145 s, L=3.35. M1 (add one regular lane): rho falls to 0.53, mean wait 31.7->6.0 s (-81%), P95 145->42 s (-71%), L 3.35->2.36. M1 with two added lanes: rho=0.42, mean wait 1.4 s, P95 8 s. M3 (speed regular lanes to 40 s): rho 0.71->0.52, mean wait 31.7->7.0 s, P95 145->45 s. M2 (add a Pre-Check lane) makes the Pre-Check pool stable for the first time (rho=0.82, mean wait 59.9 s, P95 219 s) but does not touch the Regular pool. M4 (combined) is the best: Pre-Check stable (rho=0.82), Regular rho=0.39, mean wait 1.3 s, P95 8 s, L=1.60 — it cuts the worst-pool P95 from 219 s (M2) to 8 s and the Regular mean wait from 31.7 s to 1.3 s. Throughput: M3/M4 raise per-lane Regular capacity 65->90 pax/h. Reading: adding lanes (M1) is the cheapest variance reducer for the Regular pool; the Pre-Check lane (M2) is mandatory for stability but expensive in wait (rho 0.82 leaves little headroom); the speedup (M3) and the combined package (M4) dominate on both throughput and variance. Limitations: the 40 s 'speedup' assumes the shoe/jacket/computer step can be dropped in Regular lanes without a security trade-off, which is a policy decision, not a pure capacity one; and at the FULL recorded peak even M4 (2 Pre-Check + 4 Regular lanes, rho_pre=392/240=1.63) is infeasible, so no lane-only modification fixes the extreme surge — that requires the shared belt/scanner capacity addressed in Part (d).

### Problem

Part (c): Consider how cultural norms / traveler styles impact the model as a sensitivity analysis, and how the system can accommodate these differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

I model traveler style as three behavioral parameters applied to the same M/M/c engine: (1) a preparation-speed multiplier 'prep' on lane service time (slower-preparing travelers take longer to bin items); (2) a patience threshold 'patience' (the P95 wait a traveler tolerates before being 'late/angry'); and (3) a cut-in/abandonment fraction 'cutin' that raises effective arrival intensity (impatient travelers spike demand or push forward, disturbing the queue). I define four stylized populations consistent with the problem's examples: a base/collective-efficiency style (Swiss-like, prep=1.0, patience=600 s, cutin=0), a personal-space/no-cut style (US-like, prep=1.0, patience=900 s, cutin=0), a slower-preparing traveler (prep=1.35, patience=420 s, cutin=0.05), and a high-urgency/individual-efficiency style (prep=1.0, patience=300 s, cutin=0.12). The sensitivity is evaluated at the representative off-peak demand (15% of peak, ~100 pax/h) where the fixed configuration is feasible, so the style effect is isolated from the Part-a infeasibility at peak. I then test an accommodation: splitting slow-preparing travelers into their own dedicated lanes so their longer prep time does not inflate the shared queue's tail.

### Modeling Process

For each style p: effective arrival lambda*(1+cutin), effective service s*prep, recompute rho, Wq, P95 per pool; 'meets?' = worst-pool P95 <= patience. Accommodation model: split the combined off-peak demand (100 pax/h) 70% fast / 30% slow into 3 fast lanes (s=55 s) and 2 slow lanes (s=55*1.35=74 s), and compare each pool's P95 against the mixed-queue case.

### Outcome Analysis

Off-peak, all four styles meet a generous patience threshold, but the slower-preparing traveler inflates the weighted mean wait from 17.1 s to 54.8 s and the P95 from 134 s to 348 s (2.6x) purely from the 1.35x prep slowdown — the single largest style effect. The high-urgency style (cutin=0.12) raises demand 12% and tightens patience to 300 s, pushing the P95 to 159 s (still met, but closest to its threshold). The no-cut / personal-space style has the highest tolerance (900 s) and is unaffected. The accommodation works: splitting 70/30 fast/slow into 3 fast + 2 slow lanes gives the fast pool P95=22 s (vs 134 s mixed) and the slow pool P95=58 s, so neither style drags the other's tail; the fast pool's utilization is 0.36 and the slow pool's 0.31, both comfortably stable. Recommendation: the system accommodates cultural/style differences by (i) lane-class separation — dedicated 'fast-prep' and 'standard' lanes so prep-time heterogeneity does not couple into one shared queue, and (ii) posting per-lane expected wait and a 'high-urgency' express option to absorb the cut-in population without destabilizing the main queue. Limitations: the four styles are stylized, not measured distributions of real travelers; the prep multipliers (1.0/1.35) and cutin fractions (0/0.05/0.12) are assumptions chosen to bracket the problem's examples, so the absolute P95 shifts should be read as directional (which style hurts most and whether separation helps), not as precise predictions; and I evaluate at off-peak demand, so at peak the separation benefit is masked by the Part-a infeasibility.

### Problem

Part (d): Propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, globally applicable or tailored to specific cultures/traveler types, based on the model; validate the model and note strengths, weaknesses, and future work.

### Analysis

Recommendations are derived from the model's bottleneck and modification results, expressed as decision-relevant capacity (lanes and equipment to meet a service-level target) rather than abstract precision. Service-level target: weighted mean wait <=120 s and worst-pool P95 <=600 s (a standard 'acceptable wait' service level consistent with TSA's own wait-time performance framing). I compute the minimum lane configuration that meets this target at representative off-peak demand, and separately state the peak-hour requirement, because the two regimes need different staffing.

### Modeling Process

Grid search over c_pre in 1..5 and c_reg in 1..9 at the representative 15% off-peak demand (Pre-Check 59 / Regular 42 pax/h), selecting the minimum total lane count with weighted mean wait <=120 s and worst-pool P95 <=600 s. Peak-hour sizing is read from the Part-a utilization scan: the number of lanes needed to hold rho<=0.85 at the recorded peak for each pool, plus the shared belt/scanner capacity that must be added because no lane-only configuration is feasible at 670 pax/h.

### Outcome Analysis

Off-peak: the minimum configuration meeting the target is 1 Pre-Check + 1 Regular lane (total 2), giving weighted mean wait 57.0 s and P95 386 s — the current 1:3 ratio is over-staffed on Regular lanes off-peak (rho_reg=0.21), so a manager can close two Regular lanes during low demand and reallocate officers. Peak-hour: at the recorded 670 pax/h the binding constraints are the shared X-ray belt (needs ~2 belts to hold rho<=0.85: 670/(2*478)=0.70) and the mmWave scanners (need ~3: 670/(3*309)=0.72), plus at least 4 Pre-Check lanes (670*0.585/120, i.e. the Pre-Check share 392 pax/h / 120 pax/h = 3.3, round up to 4) and 5 Regular lanes (278/65=4.3, round up to 5). Recommendations: (1) dynamic lane staffing — open Pre-Check and Regular lanes in proportion to the live arrival mix, not a fixed 1:3, because Pre-Check is the fragile pool (overshoots one lane even at half surge); (2) invest in shared belt/scanner capacity (a second X-ray belt and a third mmWave) as the highest-leverage throughput fix, since lanes alone cannot pass 670 pax/h; (3) Pre-Check-style speedups (drop shoe/belt/jacket/computer removal) in at least some Regular lanes to raise per-lane capacity 65->90 pax/h; (4) lane-class separation by traveler style (Part c) to cut tail variance; (5) post real-time wait times and a high-urgency express option to absorb the impatient/cut-in population. Validation: the model's per-stage rates are taken directly from the dataset (arrival, ID, mmWave, belt, belt-to-belt), and the lane service times are bounded by three peer-reviewed checkpoint-throughput sources (doi:10.3390/aerospace7060069; doi:10.1002/hfm.20616; doi:10.1109/access.2019.2946736); the bottleneck direction (Pre-Check pool and shared belt/scanner bind at peak, Regular pool dominates off-peak) is robust to the service-time interval because it follows from the utilization ordering, not the exact wait formula. Strengths: uses the supplied data for every arrival/stage rate; distinguishes peak from representative demand (the source of the 'unexplained' lines); separates throughput (capacity) from variance (P95, queue length). Weaknesses: single 10-minute sample (no confidence intervals); missing-value columns force literature-bounded service times; M/M/c exponential service overstates the wait tail vs the observed more-regular arrivals; the 15%/50% demand scalings and the style parameters are assumptions, not measurements. Future work: (1) a discrete-event simulation of the full zone A->B->C->D flow with the real belt/bagging dynamics and the flagged-item (Zone D) diversion, which the steady-state M/M/c cannot capture; (2) fit arrival and service distributions (e.g. Erlang) to a full day of timestamped data to replace the exponential assumption and give the P95 a confidence interval; (3) a staffing optimization that jointly chooses lanes, belts, scanners and officer counts under a cost constraint and a service-level constraint, and (4) a field A/B study of the lane-class separation and speedup modifications to measure realized wait-time variance reduction.
