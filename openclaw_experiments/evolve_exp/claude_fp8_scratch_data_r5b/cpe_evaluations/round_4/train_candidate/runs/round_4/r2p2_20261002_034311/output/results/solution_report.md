### Problem

Data preparation and cleaning for the three Amazon review files (hair_dryer.tsv, microwave.tsv, pacifier.tsv): establish what the data are, what was repaired, and the quality of each input column, so that every later measure is reproducible from the supplied files.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) each row is one customer review of one product, with review_id globally unique (verified: 0 duplicates in all three files); (2) star_rating is the customer's satisfaction score 1-5 on the same ordinal scale across files; (3) helpful_votes/total_votes are cumulative vote counts as of extraction, so they are censored (a review with 0 votes may simply be new or unviewed); (4) review_date is the writing date in M/D/YYYY; (5) the files cover different time spans (hair dryer 3/2002-8/2015, microwave 6/2004-8/2015, pacifier 4/2003-8/2015), so cross-product comparisons are made on rates and shares, not raw counts. Repair steps: whitespace/line-break collapse in text fields; star_rating and vote counts coerced to numeric (0 bad parses); vine and verified_purchase normalized to Y/N (values other than exactly 'Y' treated as N); dates parsed strictly as M/D/YYYY (0 unparseable, so none dropped); duplicate review_id rows removed (0 found). Text features engineered: character and word counts of headline and body, exclamation-mark count, ALL-CAPS word count. No rows were dropped; final N = 11,470 (hair dryer), 1,615 (microwave), 18,939 (pacifier).

### Modeling Process

### Problem

Identify the ratings- and review-based measures most informative for Sunshine Company to track after launch (Marketing Director question 1).

### Analysis

Goal: rank candidate monitors by how well each separates successful from failing products and how stable each is over time. Approach: for each candidate measure, compute (i) its distribution across products in the three categories, (ii) its association with a binary success label (product mean star >= 4.0), and (iii) its month-to-month autocorrelation (persistence, which determines how often it can move enough to be actionable). Candidates: mean star, low-star share (1-2 stars), high-star share (4-5 stars), review count, helpfulness ratio (voted subset only), median total votes, sentiment ratio from a fixed lexicon, mean body length, exclamation rate, ALL-CAPS rate, verified-purchase share, vine share. The success model below is the formal device; the ranking is read off its standardized coefficients plus the autocorrelations computed here.

### Modeling Process

Unit of analysis: product (product_parent) with >= 3 reviews; N = 940 products across the three categories, 683 labeled successful (mean star >= 4.0), baseline success rate 0.727. Features per product p: m_p = mean star, l_p = share of 1-2 star reviews, h_p = mean helpfulness ratio over voted reviews, s_p = (mean positive lexicon hits + 1)/(mean negative hits + 1) (add-1 smoothing), e_p = mean exclamation count per review, c_p = mean ALL-CAPS word count, b_p = mean body character count. Model: logistic regression logit(P(success)) = beta0 + beta'x, fit by MLE (liblinear/L-BFGS, 2000 iterations max). Standardized coefficients (z-scored features): mean_star 7.06, low_share -0.96, body_len 0.27, exclaim 0.16, sentiment_ratio 0.15, caps -0.36, help_ratio -0.24. Model accuracy 0.981 (against baseline 0.727). Persistence of the candidate monitors, measured as lag-1 autocorrelation of the monthly series per category: monthly mean star autocorr 0.27 (hair dryer), 0.23 (microwave), 0.16 (pacifier); the low-star share series is comparable. Parameter table (from supplied data): success threshold = 4.0 mean stars (task-defined 'highly rated' boundary); minimum product review count = 3; lexicon = fixed word list of ~50 positive and ~50 negative sentiment terms applied with whole-word matching to headline+body. Source: task dataset; thresholds are internal to this analysis.

### Outcome Analysis

Mean star rating and low-star share are the two dominant informative measures: mean star has by far the largest coefficient, and low-star share is the only other feature with a coefficient of comparable magnitude in the opposite direction. This redundancy is informative, not wasteful: the mean star is dominated by the 4/5 mass (58-67% of all reviews are 5-star), so it moves slowly, while the low-star share is a tail statistic that reacts faster to a new defect class. Recommended tracking set, in order of priority: (1) low-star share (1-2 stars) - most responsive early warning; (2) mean star rating - the headline reputation number shoppers and buyers see; (3) helpfulness ratio among voted reviews - measures whether the reviews that surface are credible; (4) sentiment ratio (positive/negative lexicon hits) - the text-side early warning; (5) review velocity (new reviews per week) - volume anomalies; (6) median total votes - engagement depth. Limitations: the success label is built from the same star ratings as the main feature, which inflates the coefficient of mean star (it is near-circular by construction); the honest reading is that low-star share, helpfulness ratio, and sentiment ratio are the informative *predictive* measures, while mean star is the *reporting* measure. Autocorrelations of 0.16-0.27 mean monthly values are persistent but not rigid: a 3-month moving signal is stable, a 1-month spike is noise. Bias note: products in the files are biased toward those that sold enough to be sampled; a dead product that never accumulated 3 reviews is invisible, so the 'failing' class here means 'persistently low-rated', not 'unsellable'. ## Subtask 3: Identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns that suggest a product's reputation is increasing or decreasing (M

### Problem

Identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns that suggest a product's reputation is increasing or decreasing (Marketing Director question 2).

### Analysis

Goal: define reproducible time-based reputation indicators per product and per category, and test whether the three categories show rising or falling reputations over their observed windows. Approach: for each category, build the monthly series of mean star and low-star share, then (a) fit a Theil-Sen robust slope (median of pairwise slopes, subsampled to 1500 pairs for long series) to get stars-per-year and low-share-per-year trends; (b) split the monthly series into thirds and compare first-third vs last-third means (robust to a single anomalous month); (c) compute a 12-month rolling mean (min 3 observations) as the operational dashboard indicator. Product-level: split each product's history at its median date and compare halves, requiring >= 8 reviews and >= 30 days in each half.

### Modeling Process

Let m_t be the set of reviews in calendar month t, with mean star mu_t = mean{star in m_t} and low share la_t = share{star <= 2 in m_t}. Category trend: beta_mu = TheilSen(mu_t, year_t), beta_la = TheilSen(la_t, year_t). Thirds comparison: T1 = mean(mu_t) over first third of months, T3 = mean(mu_t) over last third; delta = T3 - T1. Rolling indicator: R_t = mean(mu_{t-11..t}), L_t = mean(la_{t-11..t}), computed with min_periods=3. Product level: for product p with reviews sorted by date and median split date d_p, compare h1 = stats before d_p vs h2 = stats after, with rate normalized by days in the half. Parameter table (computed from supplied data): hair_dryer: beta_mu = +0.055 stars/yr, beta_la = -0.007/yr, T1 = 3.562, T3 = 4.083, delta = +0.521 stars; microwave: beta_mu = +0.026, beta_la = +0.000, T1 = 3.503, T3 = 3.431, delta = -0.072 stars; pacifier: beta_mu = +0.039, beta_la = -0.004, T1 = 3.878, T3 = 4.322, delta = +0.444 stars. Source: task dataset.

### Outcome Analysis

Reputation is increasing in two of the three categories and flat-to-slightly-declining in the third. Hair dryers: mean star rose from 3.56 (first third of months) to 4.08 (last third), robust slope +0.055 stars/year, low-star share drifting down - a maturing, improving category. Pacifiers: 3.88 to 4.32, +0.44 stars across the series, low-star share falling - also improving, and the healthiest of the three (11% low-star share). Microwaves: 3.50 to 3.43, slope +0.026 (statistically negligible) with low-star share flat at ~32% - the weakest and most structurally troubled category; its first-half vs second-half product split shows the same pattern (see Q4). Recommended operational definition for a single product's reputation direction: reputation is DEcreasing if the 12-month rolling low-star share exceeds the category baseline (15% hair dryer / 32% microwave / 11% pacifier) for 3+ consecutive months, or if the first-vs-second-half mean-star gap is below -0.3 stars with >= 8 reviews per half; reputation is INCreasing if the 12-month rolling mean star is rising and the low-star share is at or below baseline. Limitations: the categories have different observation windows (2002-2015, 2004-2015, 2003-2015) and the monthly series are uneven in volume (some months have a handful of reviews), so the rolling window uses min_periods=3 and the robust slope; a category's 'trend' here is a market-trend, partly reflecting Amazon marketplace growth over the period rather than pure product quality. The pacifier long tail (5,432 products, 3.5 reviews each) makes its monthly series a mixture of many short-lived products; its improving trend most plausibly reflects the retirement of bad products rather than design improvement. ### Problem

Do specific star ratings incite more reviews? In particular, are customers more likely to write a (low-star) review after seeing a series of low star ratings? (Marketing Director question 4).

### Analysis

Goal: test whether a product's recent low-rating history predicts increased subsequent review volume (an 'incitement' effect). Design: within each product (product_parent) with enough history, split the review timeline at its median date; classify products by whether the first half had a high low-star share (>= 25% of first-half reviews at 1-2 stars); compare the review rate (reviews per day) in the second half versus the first half, overall and for low-star reviews specifically. Normalization by days-in-half controls for the products' differing lifetimes. This isolates the causal-looking comparison (same product, before vs after a low-star period) from cross-product confounds.

### Modeling Process

For product p with reviews sorted by date, n_p >= 8, median split date d_p, halves h1 = {reviews < d_p}, h2 = {reviews >= d_p}, each spanning >= 30 days and containing >= 3 reviews: rate1 = |h1|/days(h1), rate2 = |h2|/days(h2), d_rate = rate2/rate1 - 1; low-rate analog with only 1-2 star reviews; group A = {h1 low-star share >= 0.25} (rough early period), group B = complement. Report median d_rate and median d_low_rate per group. Parameter table (computed from supplied data): hair_dryer - group A (n=20): median rate change +171%, median low-rate change +55%, second-half mean star 3.71, second-half low-share 0.25; group B (n=103): +135%, +233%, 4.18, 0.14. microwave - A (n=24): +16%, +35%, 2.33, 0.62; B (n=18): +84%, +120%, 3.99, 0.18. pacifier - A (n=26): +133%, -41%, 3.86, 0.21; B (n=261): +93%, +148%, 4.36, 0.11. Source: task dataset.

### Outcome Analysis

The data do not support a clean 'low ratings incite more reviews' mechanism. In the hair-dryer category, products with rough early periods did speed up in review volume (+171% vs +135%), but the effect is small relative to the overall growth of review activity on Amazon during the sample period (every product's review rate rises from first to second half, a marketplace-wide time trend, so the honest comparison is the gap between groups, which is ~36 percentage points - modest). In microwaves the pattern is reversed: rough products *slowed down* (+16% vs +84%), and crucially their reputation did not recover (second-half mean star 2.33 vs 3.99 for smooth products) - a burst of low ratings there marks a persistent product defect, after which buyers simply stop. In pacifiers rough products show a large volume jump (+133%) but their second-half low-star rate *falls* (-41%), i.e. the extra reviews are not low-star; again no evidence that the low ratings themselves caused the writing. The consistent, defensible conclusion: a cluster of low star ratings is best read as a *diagnostic* signal (something is wrong with the product) rather than a *generative* one (it causes more reviews). Two caveats: (1) the first-to-second-half rate increase is present even in smooth products, so the time trend of the marketplace is the dominant driver of review volume, and the incitement effect, if any, is a small perturbation on top of it; (2) we cannot observe what shoppers saw before writing (the listing's visible rating history), so the design tests correlation with a product's own past ratings, not with the displayed aggregate. ### Problem

### Analysis

Goal: quantify, per descriptor, how strongly its presence in a review is associated with the 1-2 star band versus the 4-5 star band, across the three categories.
