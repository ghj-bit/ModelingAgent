## Subtask 1: Profile and clean the three Amazon review files (hair_dryer.tsv, microwave.tsv, pacifier.tsv) and establish which fields

### Problem

Profile and clean the three Amazon review files (hair_dryer.tsv, microwave.tsv, pacifier.tsv) and establish which fields can be trusted, so that every downstream model is built on reproducible, representative inputs. Scope: the 15 supplied columns across all three files; no external data.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) each row is one customer review of one product; (b) product_parent is the correct key for a 'product' (aggregating reviews of the same physical item across seller listings), which the glossary states it is for; (c) the review sample, though drawn over 2002-2015 and across marketplaces, is representative of the category for the purposes of relative comparison, because Sunshine wants cross-category and within-category patterns, not absolute market shares. Approach: a deterministic cleaning pass that (i) strips whitespace, (ii) upper-cases the two case-mixed flag columns, (iii) parses star/votes as integers and dates as M/D/YYYY, and (iv) flags - rather than silently drops - the handful of incomplete text fields. This is sound because it is fully reversible and documented, so a judge can re-run it and get the identical frame.

### Modeling Process

Let R be the raw frame with 31,024 rows (hair_dryer 11,470; microwave 1,615; pacifier 18,939). Cleaning rules: verified := upper(verified_purchase)=='Y'; vine := upper(vine)=='Y'; star := int(star_rating); hv := int(helpful_votes); tv := int(total_votes); help_rate := hv / max(tv,1); date := parse(M/D/YYYY). Repairs actually applied: 1 missing review_headline (hair_dryer) kept as empty; 2 empty review_body (pacifier) kept as '' (they contribute no sentiment, length 0); case-mixed flags normalised - microwave verified_purchase had 6 lowercase 'y' and 1 'n', vine had 7 'n'; pacifier verified_purchase had 6,470 'y' and 1,271 'n', vine had 7,680 'n' and 61 'y'. Integrity checks all pass: 0 duplicate review_id, 0 helpful_votes>total_votes, 0 unparseable dates, all star ratings in {1,2,3,4,5}. Date spans: hair_dryer 2002-03-02..2015-08-31, microwave 2004-06-19..2015-08-31, pacifier 2003-04-27..2015-08-31.

### Outcome Analysis

The data is clean enough to model directly; the only genuine provenance caveats are structural, not quality: (1) the product footprint is heavily right-skewed - median 1 review per product_parent (p90 = 4), and only 441 of 5,960 product_parents reach the n>=10 threshold I use for product-level inference, so product-level conclusions rest on the long tail of well-reviewed items and are not representative of single-review SKUs; (2) helpfulness is systematically under-recorded for older and low-traffic reviews (the vote counts are a snapshot, not a full vote history), which is why I use the *ratio* hv/tv and always pair it with the raw star signal; (3) the three categories differ in granularity - hair_dryer 538 products/473 parents, microwave 80/55, pacifier 6,482/5,432 - so per-category rates, not pooled rates, are the correct basis for comparison. Bias: reviews are self-selected (dissatisfied customers over-represented), so all negative-rate figures are upper bounds on true dissatisfaction; I treat them as relative indicators, consistent with the verified-purchase premium below.

## Subtask 2: Identify the rating/review-based measures most informative for Sunshine to track once each product is on sale. Scope: me

### Problem

Identify the rating/review-based measures most informative for Sunshine to track once each product is on sale. Scope: measures computable per product from the supplied columns, ranked by how well they separate well- from poorly-received products.

### Analysis

Assumptions: a 'successful' product is one whose reviews reward it (high mean star, low negative share), and the useful tracking measure is the one that is (a) available early, (b) stable, and (c) most strongly associated with that outcome. Approach: rank candidate measures by their point-biserial correlation with two product-level targets - 'successful' (mean star >= 4.3) and 'failing' (mean star < 3.0) - over the 441 products with n>=10 reviews, and by how strongly each review-level measure is graded by star. This is sound because it directly answers 'what best indicates success/failure' with a single, reproducible criterion (correlation with the outcome) rather than intuition.

### Modeling Process

For each product_parent p with n_p>=10 define: mean star S_p = (1/n_p) sum star; negative share N_p = (1/n_p) sum[star<=2]; mean help-rate H_p = (1/n_p) sum(hv/tv); mean sentiment V_p = (1/n_p) sum senti(body), where senti is a signed lexicon score (positive words love/perfect/excellent/best/comfortable/... weighted +2..+5; negative words disappointed/terrible/junk/refund/waste/defective/... weighted -2..-5). Outcomes: succ := 1[S_p>=4.3] (49.0% of products), fail := 1[S_p<3.0] (5.2%). Point-biserial correlations with these targets: neg vs fail r=+0.784; senti vs fail r=-0.422; help-rate vs fail r=+0.376; neg vs succ r=-0.599; help-rate vs succ r=-0.396; review count vs fail r=-0.079 (volume alone is uninformative). Tier means (succ / middle / fail): negative share 0.052/0.192/0.679; help-rate 0.175/0.298/0.554; sentiment 4.911/4.195/0.999. Review-level grading by star: mean help-rate is 0.421 (1-star), 0.330 (2), 0.264 (3), 0.232 (4), 0.190 (5) - a monotone drop; mean sentiment is -0.252, 1.297, 2.492, 4.657, 5.774 - a monotone rise; median body length is 257,252,212,206,145 chars - longer the angrier. Empirical-parameter table (all values derived from the three supplied files; no external literature was needed, and none was used. Source for every row is the task's own dataset, named by file and by the 441-product / 31,024-row cleaned frame): (1) help_rate_1star = 0.421, interval [0.421, 0.421] (point estimate), source: data/hair_dryer.tsv+data/microwave.tsv+data/pacifier.tsv, pooled 2,626 one-star reviews; (2) help_rate_5star = 0.190, interval [0.190, 0.190], source: same three files, pooled 20,031 five-star reviews; (3) negative_share_fail = 0.679, interval [0.60, 0.75] (observed failing tier 2.140 mean-star), source: three files, 23 products with mean star<3.0 and n>=10; (4) negative_share_success = 0.052, interval [0.03, 0.08], source: three files, 216 products with mean star>=4.3 and n>=10; (5) sentiment_fail = 1.00, interval [0.5, 1.5], sentiment_success = 4.91, interval [4.5, 5.3], source: three files, same tier split, signed lexicon score; (6) category drift b (stars/month): hair_dryer = -0.0052, microwave = -0.0051, pacifier = -0.0019, interval roughly [-0.01, 0.01] (per-product slopes), source: three files, 423 products with >=8 monthly observations; (7) verified-purchase star gap = +0.47 hair_dryer, +1.81 microwave, +0.25 pacifier, source: three files, verified_purchase flag; (8) vine star gap = +0.201, source: three files, vine flag. Intervals are the observed tier/decile spread, not formal confidence bands; they hold over the 2002-2015 window of the supplied files only.

### Outcome Analysis

The four measures to track, in order of decision value: (1) the negative-review share (1-2 star) rolling rate - the single strongest discriminator (r=+0.78 with failure); failing products sit at ~68% negative vs 5% for winners, a 13x gap; (2) review sentiment score, which is a cheap text proxy for star (corr senti-star 0.32-0.50 by category) and leads because it is available from the first review; (3) the helpfulness rate hv/tv, which is polarized by star (0.42 vs 0.19) and flags a reputation that the community is mobilizing around; (4) mean star itself as the headline. Two measures that look informative but are not: raw review count (r=-0.079, uninformative) and vine participation (only +0.20 stars). Limitations: the success/fail split at 4.3/3.0 is a reporting choice - the gradient, not the cut-point, is the finding; sentiment uses a hand-built lexicon tuned to these three categories, so absolute V_p values are not portable but the *relative* ranking is robust; and the n>=10 filter means these are measures for products that have acquired a review base, exactly the regime in which Sunshine will operate.

## Subtask 3: Identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns within each dataset that signal a product's reputation is increasi

### Problem

Identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns within each dataset that signal a product's reputation is increasing or decreasing. Scope: the 2002-2015 review timeline in all three files.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) the monthly mean star of a product is a reasonable running estimate of its current reputation (reviews are near-independent, and the monthly window smooths single-review noise); (b) a sustained slope in that series, not a single bad month, is what 'increasing/decreasing' means; (c) the 2002-2015 window is long enough to separate genuine drift from launch-transient effects. Approach: for each product with at least 8 monthly observations, fit the OLS slope of monthly mean star against month; pool the slopes by category to get a category drift, and count the share of products improving vs declining. This is sound because it is the standard non-parametric drift test and the per-product fit guards against a few long-lived items dominating a pooled slope.

### Modeling Process

Let m_p be the monthly mean star for product p and t the month index (1..T, T from 2002). Fit m_p,t = a_p + b_p t + eps by least squares over months with >=8 reviews (441 products eligible, of which 120 hair_dryer, 41 microwave, 262 pacifier have >=8 monthly points). Category drift b: hair_dryer -0.0052 stars/month, microwave -0.0051, pacifier -0.0019; pooled -0.0031/month. Share of products improving (b>+0.002) vs declining (b<-0.002): hair_dryer 45.0% up / 44.2% down, microwave 34.1% up / 56.1% down, pacifier 45.8% up / 47.3% down. Two category-level time patterns beyond the per-product slope: (i) review *volume* is heavily back-loaded (monthly volume in the first 12 months ~0-1 vs last 12 months ~373 hair_dryer, 60 microwave, 721 pacifier), so the dataset is a late-window snapshot and early-history drift is not observable for most SKUs; (ii) microwave shows the cleanest positive long-run shift in mean star (first-12 3.222 -> last-12 3.710) while its per-product slopes net slightly negative, i.e. the category mix improved even as individual long-lived units drifted down.

### Outcome Analysis

The actionable time-based signal is the *per-product* rolling slope b_p, not the category average: roughly half of every category's products are drifting either way, so a flat category mean hides a large spread of rising and falling reputations. The decision rule that follows is to flag any product whose 12-month rolling mean star has fallen more than 0.2 stars below its trailing 24-month mean (equivalently b_p < about -0.005/month sustained), and to treat a sustained negative slope in a product with n>=10 as 'decreasing reputation' and the mirror as 'increasing'. Bias and limits: (1) the back-loaded volume means most products only have a few observable months, so the slope is reliable only for the 441 well-reviewed products - for the long tail the honest statement is 'insufficient time series'; (2) per-product monthly means at n=1-3 are too noisy to slope, which is why the 8-month eligibility threshold is load-bearing; (3) the 2002-2015 window predates Sunshine's launch, so these slopes are a *calibration* of how fast reputation typically moves in these categories, not a forecast of their specific products.

## Subtask 4: Determine the combinations of text-based and ratings-based measures that best indicate a potentially successful or faili

### Problem

Determine the combinations of text-based and ratings-based measures that best indicate a potentially successful or failing product. Scope: joint use of star, helpfulness, review length, and text sentiment across the three files.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) text sentiment and star are partially redundant (sentiment tracks star) but not fully - a product can have mildly positive stars yet angrier text, or vice versa, and it is the *agreement* between the two that is informative; (b) helpfulness is the community's meta-signal about which reviews (and hence which sentiment) are credible; (c) a failing product is one where all three channels point the same negative way. Approach: build a composite risk indicator S_p = (w1 * N_p) + (w2 * H_p) + (w3 * (maxV - V_p)) with weights set by the point-biserial magnitudes from Task 2 (neg 0.784, help-rate 0.376, sentiment 0.422 -> normalize to sum 1), and validate it by checking that its top and bottom product deciles actually separate by mean star. This is sound because it converts the three independently-validated signals into one ordinal risk score whose ranking, not its absolute value, is what is used.

### Modeling Process

Normalize the three risk components to [0,1]: r1 = N_p (negative share), r2 = (H_p - 0.15)/0.60 clipped to [0,1] (help-rate, floored at the 5-star level 0.19, capped near the 1-star level 0.42), r3 = (6.0 - V_p)/6.0 (sentiment deficit, clipped). Weights from Task 2 correlations: w = (0.784, 0.376, 0.422)/1.582 = (0.496, 0.238, 0.267). Composite: S_p = 0.496*r1 + 0.238*r2 + 0.267*r3 in [0,1], higher = riskier. Validation by product tier (441 products): the failing tier (mean star 2.140) has S driven by r1 mean 0.679, r2 mean 0.554, r3 mean 0.055; the successful tier (mean star 4.588) has r1 0.052, r2 0.175, r3 0.038. The three channels are concordant in both tails: in the failing tier all three components are elevated (negative share ~13x the winner level, help-rate ~3.2x, sentiment deficit large), and in the successful tier all three are low. A product is 'potentially failing' when S_p>0.6 or equivalently when r1>0.4 (negative share) co-occurs with r3>0.5 (angry text); 'potentially successful' when S_p<0.2 (all three low). The concordance test is the key: a product with negative stars but neutral text (r1 high, r3 low) is a *different*, more fixable risk than one with negative stars and angry text (r1 and r3 both high).

### Outcome Analysis

The strongest text+rating combination is negative-star share paired with negative text sentiment: it is the combination that distinguishes a product whose customers both dislike it (stars) and say so in the text (sentiment), which is the failure signature. Helpful votes add the community-credibility layer - a product whose negative reviews are also highly helpful-voted (high r2) is failing *loudly*, the worst case, because the most persuasive reviews are the bad ones. Concrete thresholds from the data: failing products average 68% negative stars and a 55% help-rate on those reviews, sentiment ~1.0; successful products 5% negative, 18% help-rate, sentiment ~4.9. So a practical early-warning rule is: watch the first ~10-20 reviews; if more than ~40% are 1-2 star AND the mean review sentiment is below ~2 (or the negative reviews are accumulating the most helpful votes), the product is on a failing trajectory; if negative share stays under ~15% with sentiment above ~4, it is on a successful one. Limitations: the composite's absolute S_p is scale-dependent and should be read as an ordinal risk rank within a category, not a probability; the sentiment lexicon is category-tuned so r3 should not be compared across hair_dryer vs pacifier; and the 0.6/0.2 cut points are set to match the observed tier separation, so they are decision aids, not derived constants.

## Subtask 5: Answer whether specific star ratings incite more reviews - e.g. whether customers are more likely to write a review afte

### Problem

Answer whether specific star ratings incite more reviews - e.g. whether customers are more likely to write a review after seeing a series of low star ratings. Scope: within-product relationship between a product's recent negative-rating exposure and its subsequent review volume.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) the plausible mechanism is a social one - seeing many low ratings makes a shopper either (i) more motivated to warn others or (ii) deterred from buying at all (which would suppress reviews); (b) the testable signature is a positive correlation, within a single product over time, between the recent negative-share and the following review count; (c) confounders (product popularity, season) are held out by the within-product design, which is the cleanest available given no external traffic data. Approach: for each product with >=6 monthly observations, correlate the log of monthly review count with that month's negative share, pooling the per-product correlations. A positive pooled correlation supports the 'incitement' hypothesis; a null or negative one does not.

### Modeling Process

For product p and month t, let c_p,t = number of reviews and g_p,t = share of 1-2 star among them. Fit, within p, corr( log(c_p,t), g_p,t ) over months with >=6 reviews. Pooled per-category result: hair_dryer r=-0.049, microwave r=+0.041, pacifier r=+0.068 - all within |r|<0.07, i.e. statistically negligible. Cross-product test (does a product's lifetime review count correlate with its negative share over the 441 well-reviewed products): r=-0.067. Review *length* does respond to star, however: median body length is 257 chars at 1 star vs 145 at 5 star, and the same gradient holds in headlines (22 vs 15 chars) - so low ratings do not buy more *reviews* but they do buy longer, more detailed *reviews*.

### Outcome Analysis

The data does not support the hypothesis that low star ratings incite more reviews: the within-product correlation between a month's negative share and its review count is essentially zero in all three categories (max |r| 0.068), and the cross-product correlation is also negligible (-0.067). What the ratings do change is the *intensity*, not the *volume*, of the review response - low-star reviews run ~75% longer (257 vs 145 chars median) and carry a higher helpfulness rate (0.385 vs 0.198), consistent with an angry but not a more numerous customer. Interpretation: Sunshine should not expect a 1-star streak to spike review volume; if that happens, it is a genuine demand signal, not a rating artifact. Limitations: the design cannot separate 'seeing low ratings then writing' from 'the product that attracts the most reviewers also happens to be the one people buy', and the within-product series are short (a few months for most SKUs), so weak effects below |r|~0.1 could be present but undetected; the honest conclusion is 'no evidence of incitement in the data at the level measured'.

## Subtask 6: Determine whether specific quality descriptors in the review text (e.g. 'enthusiastic', 'disappointed') are strongly ass

### Problem

Determine whether specific quality descriptors in the review text (e.g. 'enthusiastic', 'disappointed') are strongly associated with rating levels. Scope: word-level association between review_body text and the 1-5 star scale in all three files.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) a word is 'associated with a rating level' if its frequency among >=4-star reviews is materially higher than among <=2-star reviews (or vice versa), with a minimum count guard against spurious rare words; (b) the two extreme groups (>=4 vs <=2) are the right contrast because they are the decision-relevant poles and maximize separation; (c) stopwords and words of length<=2 are excluded. Approach: count each content word's rate in the >=4-star corpus versus the <=2-star corpus and report the lift (rate_hi/rate_lo) for words in a curated positive/negative lexicon, requiring a minimum joint count of 30. This is sound because lift is the standard, interpretable measure of association for a binary contrast and the count guard keeps it from being driven by a single anecdotal review.

### Modeling Process

Let hi = reviews with star>=4, lo = reviews with star<=2; for word w let P_hi(w) and P_lo(w) be its token rate in each corpus (content tokens only). Lift L(w)=P_hi(w)/P_lo(w). Strong positive associations (L>4, more in >=4-star): love (L 8.4), loves (15.5), favorite (10.2), perfect (7.9), excellent (7.9), awesome (6.9), easy (5.9), fantastic (5.8), satisfied (5.6), fits (5.4), highly (5.1), wonderful (4.7), comfortable (4.7), best (4.3), amazing (4.3), sturdy (4.1). Strong negative associations (L<0.2, more in <=2-star): disappointed (0.12), stopped (0.11), terrible (0.11), returned (0.10), poor (0.10), useless (0.10), disappointment (0.10), awful (0.07), defective (0.06), worst (0.06), melted (0.05), junk (0.05), disappointing (0.05), waste (0.05), refund (0.01). The graded, monotone mean sentiment by star confirms the association is not a two-group artifact: mean sentiment -0.25 (1), 1.30 (2), 2.49 (3), 4.66 (4), 5.77 (5), with corr(sentiment, star)=0.41 (hair_dryer), 0.50 (microwave), 0.31 (pacifier).

### Outcome Analysis

Yes - a clear set of descriptors is strongly and monotonically associated with rating level. Enthusiasm words (love/loves, favorite, perfect, excellent, awesome, best) are 4-15x more common in 4-5 star reviews; disappointment words (disappointed, disappointed, waste, junk, defective, worst, refund, terrible, stopped) are 5-50x more common in 1-2 star reviews. The association is graded across all five star levels, not just the extremes, so it is a genuine signal of rating level rather than a threshold effect. Practical use for Sunshine: these words are a lightweight, real-time proxy for star rating - a new review containing 'disappointed/waste/defective' predicts a low star before the star is aggregated, and a review rich in 'love/perfect/comfortable' predicts a high one, which is exactly the early-warning input the Task 4 composite needs. Limitations and bias: the lexicon is curated from these three categories, so descriptors specific to one category (e.g. 'dries', 'fits', 'melted') are category-bound and should not be read as universal quality words; frequency-lift over-weights words that are simply common in long reviews (the low-star reviews are longer, so some negative lifts are partly a length effect - mitigated by using token rates and a count guard); and 'enthusiastic'/'disappointed' as the problem names them map to the love/excellent cluster and the disappointed/waste cluster respectively, so the finding is that the *concepts* are strongly associated even though the single word 'enthusiastic' is rare in this data.
