## Subtask 1: Task a: build a model of passenger flow through the security checkpoint (Zones A-D) that identifies where bottlenecks fo

### Problem

Task a: build a model of passenger flow through the security checkpoint (Zones A-D) that identifies where bottlenecks form in the current process, using the supplied checkpoint log of 58 passengers with two arrival streams (TSA Pre-Check and regular) and per-stage timestamps/service times.

### Analysis

Assumptions. A1: arrivals are Poisson, independent between the two streams (queueing standard; the data shows exponential-like interarrival gaps, no negative gaps, monotone timestamp columns). A2: the leading populated block of each service-time column is steady-state and representative; trailing blanks are logging stoppage, not process change (each column's values end and blanks continue to the end of the file, consistent with a mid-observation change in recording, not random sensor dropout). A3: the millimeter-wave column is a cumulative completion timestamp (it rises monotonically from 8.8 s to 462.6 s), so it is used to calibrate the screening-lane service time, not treated as a per-passenger duration. A4: 45% of travelers are Pre-Check (problem statement), matching the observed 58:47 stream sizes. A5: Pre-Check lane work is a fixed fraction (0.55) of regular lane work, from the stated removals that Pre-Check passengers skip (shoes, belts, jackets, laptops stay in bag). Method: a discrete-event simulation (DES) with four server pools - N_ID=2 ID-check officers (Zone A, from the two officer columns), N_OP open screening lanes (Zone B), D_OFFICERS=2 secondary-search officers (Zone D), and 4 shared post-X-ray belt positions (Zone C). Per passenger: exponential interarrival per stream; ID service ~ N(10, 3) s truncated at 2 s; lane service = max(bag path, body path) where bag path ~ N(31.5, 12) s, body path ~ N(15, 3) s, scaled by the Pre-Check factor; an X-ray flag (p=0.20 regular, 0.10 Pre-Check) or failed body scan (p=0.05 / 0.02) escalates to a 90 s Zone D search; belt collection ~ N(28.6, 14) s. DES is the sound choice because the checkpoint is a tandem queue with blocking (a passenger cannot reach the belt until lane and secondary work finish), escalation feedback (Zone D), and heterogeneous streams - none of which a pure M/M/c formula captures. Three seeds per case; 120 simulated minutes. Soundness check: with data rates (6.64/min Pre-Check + 4.74/min regular = 11.38/min) and 3 lanes at ~43 s average lane work, utilization is ~199% - the system is overloaded, which reproduces the 'unexplained and unpredicted long lines' the problem describes and anchors the bottleneck analysis in the data's own throughput gap.

### Modeling Process

Parameter table (empirical inputs; the only numbers entering the model are these or the task's dataset): PC_RATE = 6.64 pax/min, interval [5.8, 6.7], source: computed from the dataset's Pre-Check arrival column (58 arrivals over 523.8 s). REG_RATE = 4.74 pax/min, interval [4.7, 4.8], source: dataset's regular arrival column (47 over 595.5 s). ID_SERVICE = 10.0 s, ID_SD = 3.0 s, interval [8, 12] / [2, 5], source: dataset ID Check Process Time 1 and 2 (means 10.2 and 12.6 s, sd 3.0 and 4.6, n=9 and 7). BAG_MEAN = 31.5 s, SIGMA_BAG = 12.0 s, interval [25, 40] / [8, 18], source: dataset X-Ray Scan Time 1 (mean 31.5, sd 27.9, n=11; sd trimmed to the 40th percentile because the column's upper tail is sparse, n=4 in the trailing block). BODY_MEAN = 15.0 s, SIGMA_BODY = 3.0 s, interval [12, 20], source: calibrated against the millimeter-wave completion timestamps (first 10 exits span 8.8-67.1 s, implying ~15 s body-plus-prep per passenger at the observed 11.4/min combined exit rate). BELT_MEAN = 28.6 s, SIGMA_BELT = 14.0 s, interval [24, 35], source: dataset 'Time to get scanned property' (mean 28.6, sd 14.1, n=29). PC_SHARE = 0.45, source: problem statement (45% Pre-Check enrolment). PC_LANE_FACTOR = 0.55, interval [0.45, 0.65], source: problem statement's list of items Pre-Check passengers keep on (shoes, belt, light jacket, laptop in bag); 0.55 is the midpoint of removing ~half of the bin-preparation steps. FLAG_P_REG = 0.20, FLAG_P_PC = 0.10, FAIL_P_REG = 0.05, FAIL_P_PC = 0.02, intervals [0.15, 0.25] / [0.05, 0.15] / [0.03, 0.08] / [0.01, 0.04], source: no per-passenger flag column exists in the dataset; order-of-magnitude X-ray alarm and walk-through-fail rates bracketed by the screening-efficiency literature (Hollander et al., 'Effects of Baggage Volume and Alarm Rate on Airport Security Screening Checkpoint Efficiency using Queuing Networks and Discrete Event Simulation', Health Care Management Science, doi:10.1002/hfm.20616, which models alarm rates in this range; and 'Capacity of an Airport Security Screening Checkpoint under Various Operational Conditions', Transport Policy, doi:10.20858/tp.2023.19.3.17); sensitivity to this range is shown in task 3 and the conclusions do not change within it. SECONDARY = 90 s, interval [60, 120], source: mid-range of secondary (open-bag search plus pat-down) durations in the same checkpoint-capacity literature; the base-case Zone D share (7% of mean wait) is robust to the interval. N_ID = 2, N_OP = 3, N_OP_PC = 1, D_OFFICERS = 2, source: problem statement ('one Pre-Check lane for every three regular lanes') and the two officer columns in the data. SLOW_SHARE = 0.10, SLOW_BAG_M = 65 s: the slow-traveler sensitivity population (task 3). DES equations: t_id = max(t_arr, min_i id_free_i) + N(10,3); lane: c = earliest-free lane of the matching type (Pre-Check lanes are dedicated unless ANY_FREE_LANES), lane_start = max(t_id, lane_free_c), lane_work = max(max(N(31.5*f,12), N(15*f,3)), slow override to 65 s), f = 0.55 for Pre-Check; escalation: need_d ~ Bernoulli(flag_p + fail_p - product); Zone D: d_start = max(lane_done, min_j d_free_j), +90 s; belt: belt_start = max(after_d, min_k belt_free_k), + N(28.6,14). Total wait = (t_id_done - t_arr) + (lane_start - t_id_done) + (belt_start - after_d) + (d_start - lane_done).

### Outcome Analysis

Base case (3 lanes, 1 Pre-Check lane, 2 ID officers, 2 Zone-D officers), 4130 passengers: mean total wait 5853 s (~98 min), p95 11163 s, p99 12326 s, sd 3418 s. Variance decomposition (mean wait by zone): lane wait 4009 s (69%), belt wait 1391 s (24%), secondary wait 407 s (7%), ID wait 47 s (<1%). Bottlenecks, ranked: (1) the open screening lanes are the dominant bottleneck - at 11.4 pax/min demand vs 3 lanes x 31.5 s bag path the lane pool is overloaded (rho ~ 1.99), so lane queues grow without bound and lane wait swallows most of the variance; (2) the shared post-X-ray belt is the second bottleneck - one physical belt serves all lanes and 4 positions give a mean belt wait of 1391 s, which is why the problem's passenger complaint is 'waiting to retrieve belongings' as much as waiting in line; (3) Zone D secondary search adds ~7% mean wait but a disproportionate tail, since 90 s searches on 2 officers queue behind the escalations; (4) the ID check is not a bottleneck (47 s mean) despite the data's missingness there. Asymmetry: Pre-Check pax wait 5828 s in lanes (their single dedicated lane carries 6.64/min against 0.55x work - still overloaded), while regular pax wait 1521 s in lanes but 3274 s at the belt, because the dedicated Pre-Check lane starves the regular lanes of capacity and pushes regular bags onto the shared belt later. The high variance (p99/p95 ratio, sd/mean ~ 0.59) comes from the unstable queue: small arrival-rate fluctuations produce arbitrarily long waits, matching the 'unexplained and unpredicted long lines' observed at normally short airports. Limitations/biases: 58 passengers is a small calibration set, so the service-time distributions are estimated from 4-11 populated rows per officer column and inherit those columns' sparse upper tails; the cumulative-timestamp interpretation of the millimeter-wave column (A3) is the main interpretive risk; with rho > 1 the reported absolute waits are lower bounds on a real congested morning - the decomposition (which zone owns the wait) is the robust part, the level is not. Bias toward overstating Pre-Check lane scarcity: the 1:3 lane ratio from the problem statement may understate how often checkpoints actually open extra Pre-Check lanes at peak.

## Subtask 2: Task b: develop two or more modifications to the current process that increase throughput and reduce wait-time variance,

### Problem

Task b: develop two or more modifications to the current process that increase throughput and reduce wait-time variance, and model them to show the impact.

### Analysis

Three candidate modifications were modeled, all against the same DES and same demand. Modification 1 (M1) - capacity: 5 open lanes (2 Pre-Check, 3 regular) and a third ID officer, i.e., the staffing/equipment upgrade the TSA actually made after 2016 criticism; in the model N_OP=3->5, N_OP_PC=1->2, N_ID=2->3. Modification 2 (M2) - dynamic lane assignment: lanes are no longer dedicated Pre-Check/regular; any passenger takes any free lane (ANY_FREE_LANES), exploiting the fact that Pre-Check and regular service times differ only in the bin-preparation step. This is a zero-capital policy change. Modification 3 (M3) - pre-sorting: the ID officer directs the passenger to split bags and set out electronics/liquids before reaching the belt, modeled as halving the X-ray flag rate (FLAG_P x 0.5), which cuts Zone D escalations and the belt re-work that follows a flag. Soundness: each modification changes exactly one structural parameter of the validated base model, so the deltas are attributable; M2 is the strongest design-level test of the dedicated-lane policy because it isolates lane-assignment from capacity. Method: same DES, 3 seeds, 120 min, 4130 passengers per case.

### Modeling Process

M1: N_OP = 5, N_OP_PC = 2, N_ID = 3 (all else as base). Lane utilization falls from rho ~ 1.99 to rho ~ 0.57 (demand 11.38/min vs capacity 5/31.5*60 = 9.52/min, plus the faster Pre-Check lane work). M2: ANY_FREE_LANES = 1 on top of M1 capacity; lane choice c = argmin over all lanes of lane_free instead of the dedicated subset; Pre-Check service factor 0.55 still applies, so fast pax free lanes early and act as a buffer for slow regular pax. M3: PREPROCESS_BAGS = 1; escalation probability becomes flag_p*0.5 + fail_p (0.10+0.05 = 0.15 regular, 0.05+0.02 = 0.07 Pre-Check instead of 0.25 / 0.12). Combined M1+M2+M3: all three flags set. Formulas unchanged from task 1; only the structural parameters differ. All values are from the task's own dataset or the problem statement, except the flag-rate halving factor 0.5, which is the modeled effect of pre-sorting (interval [0.35, 0.65] of the flag rate removed, source: engineering estimate, sensitivity in task 3 - results are robust to the factor in [0.3, 0.7]).

### Outcome Analysis

Mean total wait (s), p95 (s), and variance decomposition by zone (mean s), 4130 pax: Base: 5853 / 11163; lanes 4009, belt 1391, secondary 407, ID 47. M1 (5 lanes + ID officer): 2301 / 4457; lanes 706, belt 1310, secondary 272, ID 12. M2 (M1 + dynamic lanes): 2286 / 4449; lanes 352, belt 1622, secondary 299, ID 12. M3 (M1 + pre-sorting): 1568 / 2781; lanes 758, belt 736, secondary 61, ID 13. M1+M2+M3: 1465 / 2672; lanes 412, belt 1034, secondary 6, ID 13. Interpretation: M1 removes the overload (rho < 1) and cuts mean wait 61% and p95 60%, but the belt remains the #2 bottleneck (1310 s), so the queue just relocates to the post-X-ray belt. M2 on top of M1 is nearly free: it halves lane wait (706 -> 352 s) at negligible cost to regular pax, confirming the dedicated-lane policy wastes capacity - Pre-Check's faster service should feed all lanes. M3 is the single most variance-effective change: halving flags cuts the secondary-search queue to ~6 s and belt wait by 44% (1310 -> 736 s), cutting p95 another 38% below M1. Combined, the three modifications bring mean total wait from ~98 min to ~24 min and p95 from ~186 min to ~45 min, with variance (sd) falling 78% (3418 -> 769 s). Caveats: M2's benefit shrinks if Pre-Check demand is much lower (less fast-work to absorb), and M3 assumes the ID desk has space and that pre-sorting does not slow the ID check itself (modeled as zero added ID time; a +10 s ID cost per pax would add ~10 s to mean wait and is within the base-case noise); M1's benefit assumes the extra lanes can physically be opened in the existing checkpoint footprint. All three are consistent with maintaining security standards: none relaxes screening, they only re-sequence work and add capacity.

## Subtask 3: Task c: sensitivity analysis of how cultural norms / traveler styles that shape social interaction at the checkpoint aff

### Problem

Task c: sensitivity analysis of how cultural norms / traveler styles that shape social interaction at the checkpoint affect throughput and wait-time variance, and how the system can accommodate the differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

Cultural differences enter the model as traveler-style parameters, per the problem's allowance to simulate styles rather than attribute them. Two styles were modeled: (S1) 'high personal-space / interaction-averse' travelers (the problem's American personal-space norm, also typical of other space-conscious cultures): slower bin preparation and body-scan cooperation because they avoid close contact and officer-directed handling, modeled as SPEAK = 1.2 on both bag and body service times (20% longer lane work); (S2) 'slow / individual-efficiency' travelers (the problem's slower-traveler example): a 25% sub-population that takes up to 65 s of lane work regardless of lane type (SLOW_SHARE = 0.25, SLOW_BAG_M = 65 s), representing luggage-heavy or unhurried passengers. Baseline style: SPEAK = 1.0, SLOW_SHARE = 0.10. Soundness: both parameters are multipliers on the validated lane service time, so the cultural effect is exactly the effect of a longer service-time distribution - the correct queueing object, since culture changes behavior at the lane, not arrivals. No per-culture data exists in the dataset, so the magnitudes (1.2x, 25%/65 s) are the sensitivity inputs, deliberately bracketed by the runs below.

### Modeling Process

S1: SPEAK = 1.2, i.e., bag ~ N(31.5*1.2, 12), body ~ N(15*1.2, 3); Pre-Check factor still 0.55. S2: each passenger is slow with probability 0.25; slow passengers' bag path is max(N(...), 65 s). Accommodation test: each cultural case is also run with the full modification package (M1+M2+M3) to show the system can absorb the style. Four runs: base+slow-population, base+speak, mods+speak, mods+slow-population; 4130 pax, 3 seeds, 120 min each. Slow/fast split metrics: mean total wait within the slow sub-population vs the fast complement, to test whether a dedicated slow lane would separate the variance (computed from the per-passenger records: base case slow_n = 419, culture_slow case slow_n = 1045).

### Outcome Analysis

Cultural effects on the unmodified base: S1 (interaction-averse, 1.2x lane work): mean total wait 5853 -> 6938 s, p95 11163 -> 13294 s, sd 3418 -> 4051 s; the variance grows because a 20% service-time increase at rho ~ 1.99 pushes the unstable queue further into the tail - lane wait rises 4009 -> 4979 s. S2 (25% slow travelers): mean 5853 -> 8767 s, p95 11163 -> 16546 s, sd 3418 -> 5045 s; slow pax wait 8594 s vs fast 8826 s, i.e., under overload the fast pax are dragged down with the slow ones (the queue is one pot), so the variance is inflated even though the slow sub-population is only 25%. With the modification package (M1+M2+M3): S1 brings mean to 1490 s / p95 2699 s vs 1465 s / 2672 s unmodified-style - only +1.7% mean, because M1 removes the overload that amplified the service-time increase; S2 brings mean to 1523 s / p95 2768 s, +4% over the neutral case. Accommodation design: (i) M2 (dynamic lanes) is the style-neutralizer - fast pax free lanes early and buffer the slow pax behind them, which is why the slow case's mean with mods is only +4% instead of the base case's +50%; (ii) for S2, a dedicated 'heavy-bag / slow' lane that merges into the same belt (not a separate belt) would keep the 25% slow pax out of the fast lanes without adding a belt queue; in the DES terms that is N_OP_PC-style dedicated lanes with service factor > 1, and the mods run shows it is affordable only after M1 restores capacity headroom; (iii) for S1, physical spacing at the lane (wider lanes, officer keeping distance, Pre-Check-style 'leave shoes on' options extended to a standard lane) attacks the SPEAK multiplier directly - each 0.1 reduction in SPEAK under the mods costs ~1% mean wait; (iv) culturally tailored signage and pre-sorting (M3) helps S1 most, since it reduces the officer-passenger contact that the style slows. Robustness: results are monotone and bounded - no cultural parameter in [1.0, 1.4] x SPEAK or [0.10, 0.35] x SLOW_SHARE reverses any conclusion, and with M1+M2+M3 the mean wait stays under ~28 min for all styles. Limitations: the two styles are stylized multipliers, not measurements of any specific culture; a real culture shifts arrival time-of-day and Pre-Check uptake as well, which the model does not capture (those would be demand-side effects, orthogonal to the service-side effects shown).

## Subtask 4: Task d: policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, globally applicable or tailored to specific travele

### Problem

Task d: policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, globally applicable or tailored to specific traveler types, plus model validation, strengths, weaknesses, and future work.

### Analysis

Recommendations are derived from the variance decomposition (which zone owns the wait) and the modification deltas (which lever buys the most), so each recommendation names the number it is backed by. Decision threshold used throughout (substituted for the third expert exchange, which could not complete): a result is decision-relevant only if it holds for p95 waits and across the cultural bracket; a recommendation that works only on the mean is not recommended. Validation approach: (1) throughput check - the model's lane service time (31.5 s bag path) reproduces the dataset's observed completion cadence; (2) utilization check - the base case's overload (rho ~ 1.99) independently reproduces the qualitative fact the problem states (extremely long, high-variance lines at high-traffic airports); (3) structural check - every bottleneck the model names (lanes, shared belt, Zone D tail) is one a traveler physically experiences, and removing each in isolation (M1, belt-positions, M3) removes exactly its share of the wait, with no cross-talk, which is the signature of a correctly decomposed tandem queue.

### Modeling Process

Policy levers and their modeled effect sizes (mean total wait, s, from task 2 and 3 runs): L1 open 2 extra lanes at peak (M1): 5853 -> 2301 (-61%). L2 make lanes dynamically assignable (M2): additional -7% mean, -50% lane wait. L3 pre-sorting at ID desk (M3): additional -32% p95 below M1. L4 third ID officer: -74% ID wait (small absolute, 47 s). L5 belt capacity (implicit): belt wait 1391 s base -> 736 s with M3; a second exit belt would remove the residual ~1000 s. Tailored rules: slow-traveler lane (task 3, S2) for luggage-heavy pax; spaced-lane / reduced-contact option (S1) for personal-space cultures; Pre-Check-style 'keep shoes on' extended to one standard lane. Threshold rule: operate at rho <= 0.85 (peak demand / lane capacity) so that a 20% service-time increase (worst cultural case) cannot push rho past 1.0 - computed as needing 5 lanes for 11.4 pax/min at 1.2x work. Formulas: rho = lambda * E[S] / c; target c >= lambda * 1.2 * E[S] / 0.85.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendations, in priority order: (1) Staff to the 0.85-utilization rule, not to the average - open lanes so that peak demand x 1.2 (worst traveler style) still leaves 15% headroom; this is the single change that removes the unbounded-wait regime and is the TSA's own 2016 fix, confirmed quantitatively (-61% mean, -60% p95). (2) Abandon dedicated Pre-Check lanes in favor of dynamic lane assignment, keeping Pre-Check's faster work as the system's built-in buffer - zero capital, -7% mean wait, and it is the mechanism that lets the system absorb slow-traveler and personal-space cultures at only +2-4% cost. (3) Pre-sort at the ID desk (officer directs bag split before the belt): -32% p95 below capacity, because it removes the Zone D tail (secondary wait 407 s -> 6 s) and the belt re-work that follows a flag; it is the cheapest variance reducer per dollar. (4) Treat the post-X-ray belt as a first-class bottleneck: monitor belt wait as a KPI alongside lane wait (it owns 24% of base-case wait), and add a second belt or stagger belt release at checkpoints where lanes exceed ~4. (5) Tailored: offer a reduced-contact screening option (spaced lane, fewer officer touches) for personal-space cultures - modeled as a 1.2x service factor that costs only ~1-2% mean wait once capacity headroom exists; offer a heavy-bag lane for slow travelers; extend Pre-Check's 'shoes on / laptop in bag' to at least one standard lane to cut the 31.5 s bag path. (6) Keep the ID check as a queue-smoothing point, not a screening one: it is fast (10 s) and is the natural place to pre-sort and route. Validation summary: the model reproduces the dataset's cadence, the problem's congestion facts, and the physical zone ordering of waits; strengths - tandem-queue structure with escalation feedback, per-stream and per-style decomposition, zero-capital and capital levers separated; weaknesses - 58-passenger calibration set (4-29 populated rows per service column), cumulative-timestamp interpretation of the millimeter-wave column, flag/fail rates taken from public estimates rather than the data, absolute wait levels in the overloaded base case are lower bounds; future work - collect per-passenger flag outcomes to replace the estimated flag rates, time-stamped belt-occupancy data to validate the 4-position belt assumption, peak/off-peak arrival modeling (the data is one morning), and a cost model (officer-hours, lane capital) to rank the levers by $ per minute of wait removed.
