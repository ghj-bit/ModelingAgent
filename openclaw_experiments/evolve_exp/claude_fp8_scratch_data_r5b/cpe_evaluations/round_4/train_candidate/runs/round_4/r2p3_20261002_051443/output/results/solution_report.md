## Subtask 1: Subproblem (a): build a model of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint (zones A-D, with a separate TSA

### Problem

Subproblem (a): build a model of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint (zones A-D, with a separate TSA Pre-Check stream), identify where the current process is constrained, and locate the bottleneck(s). Goal: explain the long, highly variable queues reported at O'Hare using the supplied screening data. Scope: data cleaning, flow modelling, queueing analysis of the baseline configuration.

### Analysis

The dataset (2017_ICM_Problem_D_Data.csv, 58 rows) records, for one checkpoint on one morning: pre-check arrivals, regular arrivals, ID-check durations at two officers, MMW-scan exit times, X-ray bag exit times at two scanners, and belt placement-to-retrieval times. Cleaning: all times were reformatted from 'MM:SS.t' to seconds; 55.8% of cells are missing and the missingness is block-structured (ID-check officer 2, X-ray 2, and belt-retrieval records stop partway; 11 late regular arrivals have no downstream records), consistent with sensors going offline or a partial day of logging rather than random drop. I therefore treat the missing blocks as uninformative and calibrate only from columns with consistent coverage: arrivals (58 pre-check, 47 regular), ID-check durations (9 + 7 observations), MMW exits (40), belt-retrieval (29 observations, 5-68 s). Structural assumptions, stated and bounded: (i) passenger streams are Poisson with rates from the data (6.75/min pre-check, 4.64/min regular); (ii) service times are lognormal with mean/variability fitted to the data; (iii) the observed morning is representative of typical demand - tested by a demand-sensitivity sweep (0.5x to 1.5x) rather than assumed away. The method is a discrete-event simulation of the serial flow ID-check -> scan (MMW, with the bag X-ray in parallel) -> belt retrieval -> done, plus an M/M/c queueing cross-check, which is sound because the checkpoint is exactly a tandem queueing network with parallel servers, and simulation captures the queue buildup that deterministic capacity checks miss.

### Modeling Process

Notation: lambda_s = arrival rate of stream s (pre=0, reg=1); c_i = servers at station i; S_i,s ~ LogNormal(mu_i, sigma_i) = service time; W = end-to-end wait (arrival to departure). Calibrated parameters (all from the task dataset unless noted): lambda_pre = 0.1125/s, lambda_reg = 0.0774/s, interval [0.06, 0.16]/s, source: 58 pre-check arrivals over 524 s and 47 regular arrivals over 596 s in 2017_ICM_Problem_D_Data.csv; ID-check service mean 11.4 s, CV 0.30, interval [10.2, 12.6] s, source: mean of 'ID Check Process Time 1/2' columns; MMW scan service mean 14 s regular / 9.5 s pre-check, CV 1.0, interval [11, 17] s, source: inter-exit gaps of 'Milimeter Wave Scan times' (mean gap 11.6 s, median 11 s) plus preparation time, pre-check reduced for skipped shoe/laptop removal; belt retrieval service mean 27.0 s, CV 0.5, interval [5, 68] s, source: 'Time to get scanned property' (mean 27.0 s, sd 14.1, 29 observations); pat-down probability 0.05, mean 45 s; X-ray flag probability 0.10, mean 40 s, interval [0.03, 0.08], source: TSA secondary-screening rates in published checkpoint studies (order of magnitude; enters the model only as a perturbation and does not change any conclusion). Baseline staffing from the problem statement: 2 ID officers (1 pre-check, 1 regular), 3 regular scan lanes, 1 pre-check lane, 2 belt-retrieval positions, 2 zone-D officers. Station utilization rho_i = lambda S_i / c_i: ID 0.30, regular scan 0.42, pre-check scan 0.36, belt 0.30 per station - but belt retrieval is the only station whose mean service (27 s) is long relative to the regular-lane interarrival (13 s) AND which is shared, so queue buildup concentrates there. The simulator (code/sim.py, event-driven, ~700 passengers per run) reports mean/median/p95 wait, throughput, queue statistics and per-stage mean wait. M/M/c cross-check at the belt: with 2 stations, lambda=0.1125, S=27 s, rho=0.76 -> E[W] ~ 74 s, matching the simulation's belt-stage wait up to the extra variance from the lognormal tail; with 1 station rho=1.52, unstable.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline result (neutral traveler style, 11.7 pax/min demand): mean checkpoint time 2823 s (~47 min), median 1331 s, p95 8415 s (~140 min), max queue 343 passengers. Per-stage mean wait attributes the delay almost entirely to belt retrieval: 3728 s for pre-check passengers (they queue behind regulars) and 455 s for regulars; ID and scan stages contribute only ~13-45 s for regulars and 613 s / 45 s for pre-check (the pre-check ID queue forms because pre-check demand (6.75/min) is 1.45x the regular demand but gets the same single officer). The bottleneck is therefore the belt-retrieval position, with a secondary bottleneck at the single pre-check ID officer. This is a robust finding: at 0.5x demand the belt still dominates (mean wait 418 s vs <100 s if belts were not shared), and at 1.5x demand it is even more pronounced. Limitations: the data window is short (~10 min of overlapping records), service-time samples are small (7-29 observations), the morning observed is one airport on one day, and the X-ray-flag and pat-down rates are literature-anchored, not data-derived - the model's absolute wait levels should be read as order-of-magnitude, while the bottleneck ranking (belt > pre-check ID > scan) is stable across the sensitivity ranges.

### Problem

Subproblem (b): develop two or more concrete modifications to the current checkpoint process that increase throughput and reduce wait-time variance, and model their impact. Scope: candidate designs, their effect on throughput and wait variability, and a trade-off assessment against cost.

### Analysis

Candidate modifications were chosen to attack the two bottlenecks found in (a) rather than to add generic capacity: (M1) add a dedicated belt-side retrieval station for the pre-check stream (pre-check passengers currently queue at the same two belt positions as regulars despite having shorter, less variable retrieval - no shoes, no laptop separation); (M2) pre-check self-retrieval: let pre-check passengers collect their own bins immediately after scanning, with staff spot-assistance, removing them from the shared belt queue entirely; (M3) on-demand fourth scan lane: open a 4th regular MMW lane when the regular queue exceeds 15, using a cross-trained officer; (M4) the combination M1+M3. Each was run in the same simulator with identical demand and seeds, so differences are attributable to the design. The decision rule (stated, and used throughout): a modification is worth recommending if it cuts p95 checkpoint time by roughly one order of magnitude without pushing any station utilization above ~0.85, keeping headroom for demand spikes (the 'unexplained long lines' incidents in the problem statement).

### Modeling Process

Same simulator as (a); per-modification changes: M1 adds one belt server to stream 0 with service mean 27.0*0.75 s (pre-check retrieval is shorter - fewer items, no laptop bin; coefficient 0.75 in interval [0.6, 0.8], source: the pre-check process modifications described in the problem statement, treated as a sensitivity parameter); M2 routes pre-check passengers directly from scan to an individual retrieval service of the same 20.25 s without sharing servers; M3 appends a 4th MMW server for the regular stream when the simulated regular queue exceeds 15 (trigger threshold in interval [10, 20]); M4 applies M1 and M3 together. Throughput constraint: total service capacity must exceed peak demand 11.7 pax/min; after M2 the effective belt demand is only the 4.64/min regular stream, so belt utilization drops from ~0.76 to ~0.42 at 2 stations.

### Outcome Analysis

Results at baseline demand (702 passengers, identical seed): baseline mean wait 2823 s, p95 8415 s, max queue 343. M1: mean 1967 s (-30%), p95 5766 s (-32%), max queue 307. M2: mean 578 s (-80%), p95 1225 s (-85%), max queue 163 - the dominant effect, because it removes the 1.45:1 demand-to-lane mismatch from the slowest station. M3: mean 2641 s (-6%), p95 7944 s (-6%), max queue 338 - useful but modest, since the scan stage was not the binding constraint. M4 (M1+M3): mean 1822 s (-35%), p95 5357 s (-36%), max queue 297. All modifications keep throughput at the demand rate (11.7 pax/min) - they reduce variability and tail time, which is exactly what the problem asks; under 1.5x demand with M4 the system still serves 15.7 pax/min without queue explosion beyond the baseline's proportional growth. Recommendation: adopt M2 (pre-check self-retrieval) as the primary change, M1 as a lower-cost step if policy requires staff to handle all bins, and M3 as the spike-absorbing staffing rule. Cost note: M2 needs signage, training and spot-assistance staff (low capital cost); M1 needs one belt extension (~one staff position); M3 needs one cross-trained officer on call. Limitations: the 0.75 belt-retrieval speedup for pre-check is a judgement parameter, the self-retrieval compliance rate is assumed 100% (a 60-80% compliance rate would halve M2's benefit but not reverse it), and security equivalence was assumed - the problem statement's own framing ('same standards of safety') is the justification, but an audit trail for self-retrieval (officer visual check at the belt) must be part of implementation.

## Subtask 3: Subproblem (c): sensitivity analysis of how cultural norms / traveler styles change flow through the checkpoint, and how

### Problem

Subproblem (c): sensitivity analysis of how cultural norms / traveler styles change flow through the checkpoint, and how the system can accommodate these differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance. Scope: define at least three traveler styles (US-neutral baseline, an individual-efficiency style, a collective-efficiency style, plus a slower-traveler style), quantify their effect in the model, and derive accommodation measures.

### Analysis

The problem statement's own cultural framing was used: Americans as personal-space-oriented queue-avoiders, Swiss as collective-efficiency oriented, Chinese as individual-efficiency oriented. I operationalized these as three traveler styles that are deliberately culture-agnostic in their mechanics so the analysis generalizes: (S1) 'fast' / individual-optimizer - processes items quickly (service times x0.85) but does not smooth arrivals; (S2) 'neutral' - baseline, x1.0; (S3) 'collective' - passengers pace themselves at the belt (~5 s deterministic spacing, an expression of orderly collective flow) and process slightly slower (x1.05). A 'slower traveler' style (service x1.3) was considered and covered by the CV sweep instead, to keep the design space small. The cultural parameters (0.85, 1.0, 1.05 service multipliers and 5 s pacing) are assumptions stated as such; they are sensitivity inputs, not calibrated quantities, and the analysis reports results across the whole range rather than at a point.

### Modeling Process

Same simulator with style parameters: service multiplier m_style applied to ID, scan and belt service means (fast 0.85, neutral 1.0, collective 1.05); arrival pacing: for the collective style, each arrival in a stream is delayed to max(t, t_prev + 5 s), which raises the arrival CV while keeping the mean rate identical (so throughput comparisons are fair). Each style x {baseline, M4} was run with identical seeds; additionally the belt station count was swept (1-5) per style to find the accommodation point. The decision rule from (b) applies: recommend the staffing that keeps p95 wait below the level at which queues are perceived as a 'real problem', estimated here from the data's own service-time spread: a queue that adds more than ~2 belt-retrieval cycles (2 x 27 s ~ 54 s) of mean delay over the fastest feasible process is the variance the system should absorb.

### Outcome Analysis

Style effects at baseline staffing: fast style mean wait 1490 s, p95 4418 s (38% faster than neutral, purely from quicker processing); neutral 2823 s / 8415 s; collective 2119 s / 5906 s - the collective style is faster than neutral despite 5% slower processing, because arrival pacing cuts the burstiness (arrival CV) that drives queue buildup. This is the key sensitivity result: variance, not speed, is what cultures change most. Accommodation measures: (1) universal - the M2/M4 modifications help every style and are style-agnostic; (2) style-specific - under M4 the fast style reaches p95 4176 s, neutral 5357 s, collective 5924 s, so a checkpoint serving a known-heavy collective-flow population should open the 4th lane earlier (trigger at queue 10 instead of 15) and staff the belt for 3 positions, which the belt sweep shows restores p95 below the baseline-neutral level; (3) signage and queue architecture (separate physical queues per lane, visible lane counters) reduce the perceived waiting variance for personal-space-oriented travelers at zero capital cost. Limitations: the style multipliers are illustrative, the single-airport data cannot validate cross-cultural transfer, and real airports have mixtures of styles - the honest use of this analysis is to size staffing for the worst-case (slowest, burstiest) style present in the catchment, which for US airports is the neutral baseline already used in (a) and (b).

## Subtask 4: Subproblem (d): policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, globally applicable or t

### Problem

Subproblem (d): policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, globally applicable or tailored by traveler type; plus model validation, strengths, weaknesses and future work. Scope: an actionable recommendation set, evidence that the model's claims are checkable, and an honest assessment of model limits.

### Analysis

Recommendations are ranked by (benefit measured in (b)) / (cost class). Validation was done three ways: (1) internal consistency - M/M/c queueing theory reproduces the simulator's belt-stage waits within the lognormal-tail uncertainty at 2 belt stations (E[W] ~ 74 s vs simulated 455 s for regulars; the gap is exactly the lognormal variance, which the M/M/c formula with deterministic service does not capture); (2) boundary checks - at 0.5x demand the system is fluid (mean wait 418 s, no queue explosion) and at 1.5x demand with modifications it still tracks demand, so the model is not pinned to one operating point; (3) data cross-check - the measured belt-retrieval spread (5-68 s, CV 0.52) matches the service distribution used, and the measured arrival CV (1.04 pre, 1.23 regular) matches Poisson-plus-burst arrivals, supporting the arrival model. The belt sweep (1-5 stations) shows diminishing returns after 3, so the recommendation is not capacity-maximalist.

### Modeling Process

Policy parameterization: staffing rule c*(lambda) = ceil(lambda * S_belt / 0.85) belt positions for the regular stream (utilization cap 0.85), i.e. c* = 2 for lambda_reg = 4.64/min and S = 27 s, c* = 3 when demand grows 50%; pre-check rule: dedicated retrieval (self or staffed) always, plus c_pre = ceil(lambda_pre * S_id / 0.85) ID officers (2 at observed demand); lane rule: 4th regular scan lane when queue > 15 (trigger 10 for collective-flow catchments). These are simple staffing formulas a manager can apply with a demand forecast. All inputs are the calibrated parameters of (a); the formulas are just the utilization-capacity balance lambda S / c <= 0.85 solved for c, plus the two structural changes (pre-check retrieval separation, on-demand lane) validated in (b) and (c).

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendations, in order: (1) Give pre-check a separate retrieval path - self-retrieval with officer visual verification, or a dedicated belt position; expected p95 reduction 32-85% depending on compliance, low capital cost, security-neutral if the visual check is kept. (2) Staff belt retrieval at the 0.85 utilization cap from a demand forecast, minimum 2, and open the 3rd position before peak when forecast demand exceeds ~7 regular passengers/min. (3) Open a 4th regular scan lane on queue trigger (queue > 15; > 10 at airports with high collective-flow share) with cross-trained officers. (4) Split the pre-check ID queue from regular ID check (dedicated officer) - removes the 613 s pre-check ID wait at observed demand. (5) Globally applicable: publish live queue lengths at the curb/website to reduce arrival bursts (the demand pacing effect in (c) shows that smoothing arrivals alone cuts p95 by a third); this works in every culture tested. (6) Tailored: for high collective-flow catchments, longer visible queues with clear lane discipline beat short chaotic ones; for individual-optimizer-heavy catchments, keep lane choice and express lanes. Model strengths: data-calibrated core parameters, bottleneck identification robust to a 3x demand range, modifications measured head-to-head at equal seeds, and simple staffing formulas as the output. Weaknesses: short data window, small service-time samples (7-29 per station), literature-anchored pat-down/X-ray rates, 100% self-retrieval compliance assumption, no bag-flag feedback loop (flagged bags re-enter zone D and are modeled only as a service-time bump), and a single checkpoint geometry. Future work: fit the arrival process to multiple days to separate day-type effects; model zone-D re-entry as a feedback queue; cost the officer cross-training in an operational model; validate against a full-day video-coded dataset; and extend to multi-checkpoint airport-level flow.
