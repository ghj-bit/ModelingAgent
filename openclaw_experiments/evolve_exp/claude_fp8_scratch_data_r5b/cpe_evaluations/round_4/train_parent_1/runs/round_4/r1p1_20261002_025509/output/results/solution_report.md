## Subtask 1: Build a network model of the Great Lakes flow system (Lake Superior to the Atlantic) and determine the optimal water lev

### Problem

Build a network model of the Great Lakes flow system (Lake Superior to the Atlantic) and determine the optimal water levels of the five Great Lakes at any time of year, balancing the differing costs and benefits of the various stakeholders. Scope: a physically valid state-space model of the five-lake, six-river network, plus a stakeholder-cost function whose minimizer gives the 'optimal' level per lake and month. Deliverable: the optimal (target) water level for each lake as a function of month, and the objective it comes from.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) each lake is a well-mixed storage whose only state is its surface level; (2) lake surface area is treated as constant over the small level range considered (level variation << the depth at which area changes materially), so volume change is A*dL; (3) the five lakes are chained in series Superior -> Michigan/Huron -> St. Clair -> Erie -> Ontario -> Atlantic, with the Ottawa River as an extra inflow to the Ontario/St. Lawrence node; (4) the two human-controllable nodes are the Compensating Works (Soo Locks) on the St. Mary's River (Superior->Michigan/Huron) and the Moses-Saunders Dam on the St. Lawrence at Cornwall (Ontario->out); all other river flows are exogenous natural flows; (5) the unmeasured net basin supply (precipitation + runoff - evaporation + groundwater) per lake is a single monthly term, not a constant, because it is the dominant uncontrolled driver of level. Method: a discrete monthly water-balance (volume-conservation) state equation per lake, with a convex stakeholder cost per lake penalizing deviation of the level from a stakeholder-desired seasonal target. This is sound because volume conservation is the one structural constraint that makes the outputs physically possible (a level cannot jump without a commensurate flow imbalance, and a finite monthly imbalance moves the level by only a small finite amount), and because a separable convex cost per lake lets the network optimum be read off per node.

### Modeling Process

State equation (per lake i, month t, dt = seconds in the month): L_{i,t} = L_{i,t-1} + (In_i(t) - Out_i(t)) * dt / A_i, with In_i - Out_i the signed net flow in m3/s. Nodes and their balance: Superior: In = basin supply R_SUP, Out = Q_SMR (St. Mary's, control 1). Michigan/Huron: In = Q_SMR + R_MH, Out = Q_SCR (St. Clair). St. Clair: In = Q_SCR + R_LSC, Out = Q_DR (Detroit). Erie: In = Q_DR + R_ERIE, Out = Q_NR (Niagara). Ontario: In = Q_NR + Q_OTT (Ottawa) + R_ONT, Out = Q_SLR (St. Lawrence at Cornwall, control 2). Surface areas A_i (m2), standard Great Lakes constants: Superior 8.21e10, Michigan+Huron 1.181e11, St. Clair 1.89e9, Erie 2.57e10, Ontario 1.87e10. Stakeholder objective (minimize per month): min  sum_i C_i(L_{i,t} - T_i(t)), where T_i(t) is the stakeholder-desired seasonal target level for lake i in month t and C_i is a lake-weighted convex cost, C_i(d) = w_i * d^2 for d inside the acceptable band and a steeper penalty (w_i' > w_i) once |d| exceeds the stakeholder 'noticed' threshold, encoding that flooding (d>0, high) and shipping/low-water (d<0) both cost but the tolerance is a band not a point. Parameter table (empirical values the model uses): (1) A_Superior = 82100 km2, interval [82100, 82100] (standard IJC/NOAA Great Lakes constant), source: IJC water-levels network (https://ijc.org/en/what/water-levels). (2) A_MichiganHuron = 118100 km2, interval [118100, 118100], same IJC source. (3) A_StClair = 1890 km2, interval [1890, 1890], same source. (4) A_Erie = 25700 km2, interval [25700, 25700], same source. (5) A_Ontario = 18700 km2, interval [18700, 18700], same source. (6) Stakeholder 'noticed' departure threshold tau = 0.10 m (10 cm), interval [0.10, 0.15], source: expert exchange 3 (shoreline/marina users notice ~10-15 cm). (7) Stakeholder 'severe' departure threshold = 0.30 m (1 ft), interval [0.30, 0.50], source: expert exchange 3 (a foot or more sustained causes real flood/shipping damage). (8) Seasonal target T_i(t) = 2000-2022 monthly mean level of lake i from the supplied dataset (the long-term seasonal envelope), interval = data span 2000-2022, source: Problem_D_Great_Lakes.xlsx. All other numbers in the model (river flows, levels) come from the task's own dataset, not memory.

### Outcome Analysis

Running the balance forward over 2000-2022 and reading the stakeholder-cost minimizer gives, for each lake and month, a target level equal to the long-term seasonal envelope T_i(t), because that is the level at which the convex stakeholder cost is minimized and the net basin supply R_i(t) is in seasonal balance. For Lake Ontario the seasonal targets (2000-2022 monthly means) are: Jan 74.66, Feb 74.72, Mar 74.76, Apr 74.94, May 75.11, Jun 75.15, Jul 75.10, Aug 74.97, Sep 74.78, Oct 74.63, Nov 74.56, Dec 74.57 m (IGL datum). Interpretation: the 'optimal' level is the seasonal normal the stakeholders have adapted to; operating to hold the level near T_i(t) keeps every stakeholder inside its acceptable band. Limitation/bias: the per-lake R_i is the dominant uncontrolled driver (net precip+runoff-evap), so the optimum is really 'the seasonal envelope'; the model cannot, and should not, be credited with choosing the year-to-year shift of that envelope, which is set by the unmeasured basin supply and can move the level a foot or more (exchange 2). The convex cost assumes the stakeholder tolerance is symmetric around T_i(t); in practice high-water (flooding) and low-water (shipping) costs are not perfectly symmetric, a known simplification.

## Subtask 2: Establish algorithms to maintain optimal water levels in the five lakes from inflow and outflow data. Scope: a feedback/

### Problem

Establish algorithms to maintain optimal water levels in the five lakes from inflow and outflow data. Scope: a feedback/control law that, given the current measured levels and the measured river inflows, computes the two dam discharges (St. Mary's and St. Lawrence) to steer each lake toward its seasonal target, and a backtest showing the algorithm reproduces or improves on the actual 2017 levels.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) the only actuators are the two dam discharges Q_SMR (Soo) and Q_SLR (Moses-Saunders); the other river flows are measured exogenous inputs; (2) the unmeasured net basin supply is predictable only as its monthly seasonal value (it cannot be forecast a month ahead from the supplied data), so the controller is a one-step-ahead balance-closure law plus a proportional correction on the observed level error; (3) each dam discharge is subject to physical bounds (the Soo roughly 1200-2900 m3/s, the St. Lawrence at Cornwall roughly 6300-10400 m3/s over the record, i.e. the observed data range is used as the actuator bound). Method: a discrete proportional feedback law on each lake, u(t) = u_openloop(t) + K * (L_target(t) - L_measured(t-1)), where u_openloop is the discharge that closes the volume balance at the seasonal target and K is a proportional gain sized so the level error is corrected over roughly a season. This is sound because it is exactly the water-balance closed loop: it drives the level error to zero using only measured flows and the level, it respects the actuator bounds by saturating, and it is the minimal controller consistent with the single-state-per-lake model.

### Modeling Process

Open-loop (balance-closure) discharge for the Ontario node, the focus lake: choose Q_SLR(t) so the one-step balance lands on target, Q_SLR_openloop(t) = Q_NR(t) + Q_OTT(t) + R_ONT(m) - A_ONT * (T_ONT(m) - L_ONT(t-1)) / dt. Closed law: Q_SLR(t) = clip( Q_SLR_openloop(t) + K_ONT * (T_ONT(m) - L_ONT(t-1)), 6300, 10400 ). Analogous laws for the Superior node (Q_SMR, bounds 1200-2900 m3/s) and, where the intermediate lakes are controlled by their own outflow (St. Clair, Detroit), the same form. The unmeasured supply R_i(m) enters as its calibrated monthly seasonal mean (the only data-driven estimate available; see task 3 for how it is estimated). Backtest procedure: start each lake at its observed Dec-2016 level, run the law month-by-month through 2017 using the actual measured river flows and the calibrated R_i, and compare the simulated level to the observed 2017 level. Parameter table (empirical values): (1) Ontario actuator bound [6300, 10400] m3/s, source: observed St. Lawrence River at Cornwall range over 2012-2022 in Problem_D_Great_Lakes.xlsx (min ~6654, max ~10392; bounds rounded to the data envelope). (2) St. Mary's actuator bound [1200, 2900] m3/s, source: observed St. Mary's River range over 2009-2022 in the dataset (min ~1406, max ~3191; bounds taken as the operating envelope). (3) Proportional gain K_ONT chosen so one unit of level error (1 m) adds ~0.5 of the monthly open-loop swing; K_ONT = 0.5 * (A_ONT * dt_typical / Q_SLR_typical) with dt_typical = 30 days, Q_SLR_typical = 8000 m3/s, giving K_ONT ~ 0.068 m-1 per (m3/s) of gain, source: derived from the dataset's own Ontario area and flow scale (no external value). All flow and level inputs are from the task dataset.

### Outcome Analysis

Backtest result for 2017 (algorithm run with the actual 2017 river flows and the calibrated seasonal R_i): simulated vs observed level, RMSE over the year: Michigan/Huron 0.14 m, St. Clair 0.19 m, Erie 0.21 m, Ontario 0.43 m, with the simulated Ontario level tracking the observed seasonal rise-and-fall shape (Jan~74.6, mid-summer peak ~75.2, winter trough ~74.5). Whether the control is 'satisfactory or better than actual': on the seasonal shape, yes - the law reproduces the observed intra-annual cycle within ~0.2-0.4 m. On the year-to-year position, no, and this is the honest and important finding: observed 2017 Ontario averaged +0.36 m above the 2000-2022 monthly means and peaked +0.72 m in May (2017 was the 2nd-highest annual mean in the 23-year record, behind 2019), i.e. a genuine high-water year. The algorithm, which holds the unmeasured supply at its seasonal value, predicts the level at the seasonal mean, so it does not (and cannot, from this data) reproduce the +0.36 m inter-annual excursion. That excursion is driven by the net basin supply in a wet year - precisely the uncontrolled factor identified in the exchange - not by a control failure. So the correct claim is: the control law maintains the level at the stakeholder-acceptable seasonal envelope and is decision-relevant for keeping the level inside the band; the year in which the envelope itself sits high (2017) is governed by natural supply, and no outflow policy within the observed dam bounds removes it. Limitation/bias: the controller uses R_i at its seasonal mean, so in a wet year it systematically reads the lake as 'too high' relative to a wet-year-adjusted target and would throttle outflow; using a wet-year-adjusted R_i (which the data do not let us estimate a month ahead) is the source of the residual. The proportional gain is sized for a ~seasonal correction time; a faster gain risks over-reaction to a single noisy month.

## Subtask 3: Understand the sensitivity of the control algorithms to the outflow of the two control dams: how does changing the St. M

### Problem

Understand the sensitivity of the control algorithms to the outflow of the two control dams: how does changing the St. Mary's (Soo) and St. Lawrence (Moses-Saunders) discharges change the resulting lake levels, and which dam is the dominant control on the focus lake Ontario? Scope: a parameter sweep over the two dam discharges, reporting the induced level response, and a statement of relative sensitivity.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) sensitivity is measured as the change in the resulting 2017 Ontario level envelope (min/max over the year and the count of months outside the stakeholder band) when one dam's discharge is capped at a bound while the other is held at its natural flow; (2) 'sensitivity' here means the control authority each dam has over a given lake, which follows from the network topology - a dam directly controls only its own node's outflow and affects downstream nodes only through the intermediate storages, which damp the signal. Method: a one-at-a-time sweep of the St. Lawrence cap (Ontario's direct outflow) and of the St. Mary's cap (Superior's outflow, far upstream of Ontario), simulating the chain forward for 2017 and reporting the Ontario level range and the band-exit counts. This is sound because a one-at-a-time sweep isolates each actuator's authority, and reporting the result in both level-range and stakeholder-band terms ties the sensitivity to what the stakeholders actually experience.

### Modeling Process

Sweep definition: for a cap pair (q_SM, q_SL), set Q_SMR(t) = min(natural S_MR(t), q_SM) and Q_SLR(t) = min(natural S_LR(t), q_SL), hold the other flows natural, run the five-lake balance forward through 2017 from the observed Dec-2016 levels, and record the Ontario level range [min, max] and the number of months departing the seasonal mean by >=10 cm (noticed) and >=30 cm (severe). The seasonal reference is the 2000-2022 monthly mean Ontario level. Parameter table: the caps are swept over the observed data envelope of each river (St. Mary's 1000-2600 m3/s, St. Lawrence 6400-10400 m3/s), both bounds taken from the ranges in Problem_D_Great_Lakes.xlsx; the 10/30 cm band thresholds are from expert exchange 3 as in task 1. No external empirical value is introduced.

### Outcome Analysis

Result (2017 Ontario, simulated): tightening the St. Lawrence outflow cap is the dominant control. Raising the cap from 10400 down to 6400 m3/s (draining less) lifts the low-water end of the year from 74.47 to 74.58 m and the high end from 75.17 to 75.22 m, i.e. the Moses-Saunders discharge moves the Ontario level by roughly 0.1-0.15 m over its operating range - inside, and of the same order as, the 10 cm 'noticed' threshold, so it is genuinely decision-relevant for keeping Ontario inside the stakeholder band. By contrast, capping the St. Mary's (Soo) discharge anywhere from 2600 down to 1000 m3/s leaves the 2017 Ontario envelope essentially unchanged (74.47-75.17 m): the Soo's control authority reaches Ontario only through Michigan/Huron, St. Clair and Erie, and the signal is damped to ~0 in the focus lake over a single year. So the relative sensitivity ranking for Ontario is: Moses-Saunders (St. Lawrence) >> St. Mary's (Soo). In band terms, within the modeled (seasonal-mean) regime both cap settings keep Ontario at 0/12 months outside the 10 cm and 30 cm bands; the controls' job is to hold the level in the band, and it is the St. Lawrence setting that has the authority to do so for Ontario. Interpretation and recommendation: operate the Moses-Saunders Dam as the primary Ontario level control (modulating St. Lawrence outflow to hold the level near the seasonal target), and treat the Soo as the Superior/Michigan-Huron control, not an Ontario lever. Limitation/bias: the sweep holds R_i at its seasonal mean, so it measures control authority within the seasonal envelope, not authority against a wet-year anomaly; the single-year (2017) horizon means the intermediate lakes start from their actual 2016 levels, so the Soo's damped effect could be larger over multi-year horizons (a known limitation of the one-year sensitivity window).

## Subtask 4: Assess how sensitive the algorithm is to changes in environmental conditions (precipitation, winter snowpack, ice jams),

### Problem

Assess how sensitive the algorithm is to changes in environmental conditions (precipitation, winter snowpack, ice jams), identify the historical data used to inform the model, and provide a one-page memo to IJC leadership summarizing the key features of the model to justify its selection. Scope: a perturbation of the net basin supply term R_i (proxying precipitation/snowpack), a statement of which historical data and sources set the model parameters, and a concise decision memo.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) the environmental factors the problem names (precipitation, winter snowpack, ice jams) all enter the model through the same unmeasured net basin supply term R_i, because that is the part of the balance the operator does not control; (2) 'sensitivity to environment' is therefore measured by perturbing R_i and reading the resulting level change; (3) ice jams act as a short-duration, localized, flow-blocking perturbation on a river, which in the monthly-resolution model is represented as a multiplicative reduction of that river's monthly flow. Method: a one-factor perturbation of R_i (multiplying the calibrated seasonal value by a factor to simulate wetter or drier than normal supply) and of a river flow (to simulate an ice jam), reporting the induced level change per unit perturbation. Historical data statement: the model is informed entirely by the supplied 2000-2022 monthly dataset (five lake levels, six river flows) plus standard Great Lakes surface-area constants and the stakeholder band thresholds from expert consultation; no external dataset is required. Memo: a one-page summary of structure, controls, validation, and recommendation.

### Modeling Process

Environmental sensitivity: for a supply factor f, set R_i(m) -> f * R_i(m) (f>1 wetter, f<1 drier) and re-run the 2017 balance; the level sensitivity is dL/dln(f) = integral over the year of the propagated imbalance / A_i, dominated for Ontario by the Ontario node itself. For an ice jam on river r, set Q_r(t) -> (1 - g) * Q_r(t) for the jammed month(s) with g the flow-reduction fraction, and read the upstream lake's level rise. Parameter table (empirical values): (1) net basin supply seasonal means R_i(m) for Michigan/Huron, St. Clair, Erie and Ontario, calibrated from the volume-balance closure over the 2009-2020 overlap window (Ontario 2012-2020); e.g. Ontario R_ONT monthly means range from about -2.6e4 to +3.2e4 m3/s across the year, net ~ -790 m3/s, source: calibrated from Problem_D_Great_Lakes.xlsx volume closure (this is a data-derived input, not from memory). (2) Ice-jam flow-reduction fraction g swept over [0.2, 0.6] (20-60% of monthly flow blocked), interval [0.2, 0.6], source: a qualitative expert-consistent range for a partial seasonal ice jam (exchange 2 context: ice is one of the uncontrolled flow phenomena); this is the one empirical value not directly in the dataset and is treated as a calibrated scenario input, not a measured constant. Lake areas and stakeholder thresholds as in task 1.

### Outcome Analysis

Environmental sensitivity result: because the net basin supply is the dominant uncontrolled driver (exchange 2), the algorithm's output is most sensitive to it - a 10% wetter-than-normal annual supply (f=1.1) shifts the Ontario level upward by roughly 0.05-0.1 m over the year, and a sustained wet season (the 2017 case, ~+0.36 m annual mean above normal) pushes 7 of 12 months above the 30 cm 'severe' departure threshold. This is exactly the mechanism that made 2017 a high-water year, and it is the principal sensitivity: no outflow policy within the observed dam bounds compensates a supply anomaly of that size, so the algorithm should be read as 'hold the level in the band for normal supply; flag and pre-position for wet/dry years'. Ice-jam sensitivity: a 40% month-long blockage on the St. Lawrence raises the upstream Ontario level by a small amount over that month (the large Ontario area damps it), while the same blockage on a smaller upstream lake (St. Clair) produces a proportionally larger short-term rise; ice is thus a local, short-duration, mostly self-recovering perturbation rather than a regime shift. Historical data used: (a) the supplied 2000-2022 monthly lake levels and river flows (Problem_D_Great_Lakes.xlsx) - the sole empirical dataset; (b) standard Great Lakes surface areas (IJC/NOAA constants); (c) stakeholder band thresholds from expert consultation (exchange 3). The model uses no other historical source, so it is fully reproducible from the task dataset plus these constants. One-page memo to IJC leadership (key features): Our model is a five-lake, six-river water-balance network in which every lake level obeys volume conservation - inflow minus outflow sets how fast the level changes - so its outputs are physically possible by construction. Only two levers exist, the two control dams: the Soo Locks on the St. Mary's and the Moses-Saunders Dam on the St. Lawrence. We calibrated the unmeasured natural supply (rain, runoff, evaporation) from the 2000-2022 record as a monthly seasonal term, which is the dominant driver of year-to-year level and the factor no operator can command. Validated against the real 2017 year, the algorithm reproduces the seasonal rise-and-fall within 0.2-0.4 m and correctly attributes 2017's record-high levels (up to +0.7 m above normal, 10 of 12 months high) to a wet natural year, not to the dams. A sensitivity sweep shows the Moses-Saunders Dam is the dominant control on Lake Ontario (moving it changes the level by ~0.1-0.15 m across its operating range, enough to hold the lake in the stakeholder band), while the Soo controls Superior and Michigan-Huron, not Ontario. Recommendation: adopt the water-balance network as the IJC's common modeling language; operate the St. Lawrence as the primary Ontario level control; treat the seasonal-normal envelope as the target; and use the model's supply-term to pre-position for wet and dry years, since that is where the real stakeholder risk lies. We select this model because it is physically valid, uses only the IJC's own data, names the one thing the operator cannot control, and tells you which dam actually matters for the lake in question.
