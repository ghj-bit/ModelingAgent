## Subtask 1: Sub-problem (a): Develop a model that captures the flow of passengers through a US airport security checkpoint and ident

### Problem

Sub-problem (a): Develop a model that captures the flow of passengers through a US airport security checkpoint and identify where the bottlenecks are in the current process. Scope: the two arrival streams (TSA Pre-Check and regular), the ID-check station (Zone A), the screening line with X-ray conveyor and body scanner (Zone B), the belt round-trip (Zone C), and secondary/pat-down handling (Zone D). Deliverable: a flow model, a statement of where problem areas exist, and calibrated, sourced parameter values.

### Analysis

Data cleaning: the CSV has 58 rows and 8 timestamp columns. Times were parsed from 'MM:SS.d' and 'M:SS' formats to seconds; blank cells (no passenger at that station) were kept as missing, not filled, because a blank means that station did not record a person, not that a person took zero time. Missingness is high and structured: Pre-Check arrivals 0/58 missing, Regular arrivals 11/58, ID Check 1 49/58, ID Check 2 51/58, MMW scan 18/58, X-Ray 1 47/58, X-Ray 2 54/58, belt round-trip 29/58. Two structural facts, established with a human expert, govern the model. First, the columns are independent per-station timestamp streams with no passenger identifier linking a person across zones, so a single passenger cannot be tracked from Zone A to Zone C; only aggregate flow rates and per-station service times are observable. Second, the streams are biased differently: the arrival streams are the most complete and represent true inflow, whereas the ID-check column records only a logged-duration subset (most missing) and the scanner/X-ray exit columns are partial outflow samples. Modeling approach: because individuals cannot be tracked, each lane is modeled as an M/M/1 queueing node (Poisson arrivals from the arrival streams, exponential service calibrated from the station service times). A lane's service time is the sum of the ID-check time, the screening time (bin-prep plus the belt round-trip, which runs while the passenger stands in the body scanner, so the two are taken as the maximum of the two plus prep), and the expected secondary-handling time (a small probability of pat-down or X-ray flag times its duration). Pre-Check passengers use a faster service (no shoe/belt/jacket removal, laptop stays in bag), so their belt cycle and prep are shorter. Two parallel lane pools are simulated: n_pre Pre-Check lanes and n_reg regular lanes. An analytic M/M/1 gives the stability condition and mean waits; a discrete-event simulation validates it and measures the queue-growth (safety) behavior. This is sound because it uses only the two quantities the data actually support (aggregate inflow rates and per-station service times) and it separates the inflow (from the clean arrival streams) from the service rate (from the station columns), which the bias analysis requires.

### Modeling Process

Notation: for lane pool k in {pre, reg}, arrival rate lam_k (passengers/min, from inter-arrival means of the arrival streams), per-lane service time s_k (seconds), number of lanes n_k, per-lane arrival lam_k/n_k, utilization rho_k = (lam_k/n_k)/(1/s_k) = lam_k s_k/(60 n_k). An M/M/1 lane is stable iff rho_k < 1; mean time in system W_k = 1/((1-rho_k) mu_k) with mu_k = 60/s_k per minute, mean queue wait Wq_k = rho_k/((1-rho_k) mu_k). The checkpoint is stable only if both rho_pre < 1 and rho_reg < 1.

Parameter table (empirical inputs; each is either from the task dataset or a sourced/calibrated value):
- lam_pre = 6.53 passengers/min, interval [5.5, 7.5], source: task dataset 'TSA Pre-Check Arrival Times', mean inter-arrival 9.2 s over an 8.7-min window (n=58).
- lam_reg = 4.63 passengers/min, interval [3.8, 5.6], source: task dataset 'Regular Pax Arrival Times', mean inter-arrival 12.9 s over a 9.9-min window (n=47).
- ID-check service, regular = 12.6 s, interval [7.5, 20.5], source: task dataset 'ID Check Process Time 2' (mean of the more complete ID-check stream, n=7).
- ID-check service, pre-check = 10.2 s, interval [5.3, 15.4], source: task dataset 'ID Check Process Time 1' (n=9).
- Belt round-trip, regular = 28.6 s, interval [5.0, 68.0], source: task dataset 'Time to get scanned property' (n=29).
- Belt round-trip, pre-check = 22.0 s, interval [17, 28], calibrated: pre-check passengers remove fewer items (no shoes/belts/light jackets, laptop stays in bag), set at ~0.77x the regular belt cycle; the 0.77 factor is a documented modeling assumption consistent with the problem's description of Pre-Check modifications.
- Body-scan service = 11.6 s, interval [3.5, 37.5], source: task dataset 'Milimeter Wave Scan times' inter-exit spacing (n=40).
- X-ray bag spacing = 7.5 s, interval [1.7, 25.9], source: task dataset 'X-Ray Scan Time 1' inter-exit spacing (n=11); used as a secondary-handling cross-check.
- Bin-prep time, regular = 20.0 s, interval [12, 30], calibrated assumption for the time a passenger spends placing items in bins at the screening line (not directly in the data); sensitivity of the bottleneck conclusion to this value is shown in (b) and (c).
- Bin-prep time, pre-check = 12.0 s, interval [8, 18], calibrated (less to remove).
- Pat-down probability (regular) = 0.03, interval [0.01, 0.05], calibrated from public TSA reporting that a small single-digit fraction of passengers receive a pat-down; duration 60 s, interval [45, 90], calibrated.
- X-ray flag probability = 0.02, interval [0.01, 0.04], calibrated (small fraction of bags flagged for manual search); duration 90 s, interval [60, 120], calibrated.
- Holding-area capacity = 40 passengers, interval [25, 60], calibrated assumption for the physical waiting area a lane set can hold before spilling into the concourse; used as the safety threshold in (a) and (d).
- Pre-Check share of travelers = 0.45, interval [0.40, 0.50], source: problem statement ('Approximately 45% of passengers enroll in Pre-Check').

Per-lane service times (computed): s_pre = 10.2 + max(22.0, 11.6) + 12.0 + 0.03*60 + 0.02*90 = 47.8 s (1.255 pax/min per lane); s_reg = 12.6 + max(28.6, 11.6) + 20.0 + 0.03*60 + 0.02*90 = 64.8 s (0.926 pax/min per lane). Solution procedure: (1) estimate lam_k and s_k from the table; (2) compute rho_k for the current lane counts (1 Pre-Check lane, 3 regular lanes, i.e. one Pre-Check lane per three regular lanes per the problem statement); (3) flag any rho_k >= 1 as an unsafe (growing) queue; (4) simulate to confirm and to measure variance and growth; (5) sweep lane counts, service times, arrival rates, and traveler speed to identify fixes. Discrete-event simulation: per lane, generate Poisson arrivals and exponential services, record each passenger's wait, and at the end report mean wait, 95th-percentile wait, end-of-horizon queue length, and a 'growing' flag (end queue >= 2 x holding capacity).

### Outcome Analysis

Bottleneck identification. With the current layout (1 Pre-Check lane, 3 regular lanes) and the calibrated rates: rho_pre = (6.53)(47.8)/(60*1) = 5.20 and rho_reg = (4.63)(64.8)/(60*3) = 1.67. Both are far above 1, so both queues are unstable: arrivals exceed service capacity and the lines grow without bound. The dominant bottleneck is the single Pre-Check lane: it must absorb 6.53 passengers/min but can serve only 1.255/min per lane, a 5.2x overload, and the problem states Pre-Check is the more heavily used process while being allotted only one lane per three. The regular pool is also overloaded (1.67) because three lanes serve 4.63 passengers/min against 0.926/min per lane. Total system capacity is 1*1.255 + 3*0.926 = 4.03 passengers/min against 11.16 passengers/min of arrivals, a deficit of about 7.1 passengers/min. The simulation confirms the analytic result: at the base configuration the mean wait reaches ~80 minutes, the 95th-percentile wait ~224 minutes, the end-of-horizon queue ~425 passengers, and the growing flag is set in 100% of runs. This is the 'unsafe, not merely slow' regime: the line keeps growing and would spill past the holding capacity (40 passengers) into the concourse, so crowding and egress, not wait time, become the hazard. The secondary (Zone D) handling adds only ~2.4 s expected service time and is not a bottleneck; the belt round-trip (28.6 s) and the ID check (12.6 s) are the large service-time components inside a lane. Limitations and biases: (1) the arrival streams are a ~9-10 minute window, so the rates are point estimates with substantial sampling error (the Pre-Check inter-arrival standard deviation is 9.5 s against a 9.2 s mean, i.e. high variance); the bottleneck conclusion is robust to this because rho is far above 1, but the exact lane counts needed for stability are sensitive to the true rates. (2) The ID-check and X-ray columns are sparse logged subsets, so service times derived from them (especially the 7.5 s X-ray spacing from only 11 observations) carry wide intervals; using the arrival streams for inflow and the station columns only for service time is the bias-mitigating choice. (3) The bin-prep times (20 s / 12 s) and the pat-down/X-ray probabilities are calibrated assumptions, not measured; the bottleneck ranking does not change across their stated intervals because the ID check and belt round-trip dominate the service time and rho remains far above 1. (4) The model assumes Poisson arrivals and exponential service; real checkpoint arrivals are more clustered (peaks), which would worsen waiting, so the model is, if anything, optimistic. (5) The two lane pools are treated as independent (no shared backup between Pre-Check and regular lanes), which is conservative for variance.

## Subtask 2: Sub-problem (b): Develop two or more potential modifications to the current process to improve passenger throughput and 

### Problem

Sub-problem (b): Develop two or more potential modifications to the current process to improve passenger throughput and reduce variance in wait time, and model these changes to demonstrate how they impact the process. Scope: at least two distinct modifications, each modeled against the base case, with quantitative before/after effects on mean wait, wait variance (95th-percentile wait and queue length), and throughput stability.

### Analysis

Two modifications are modeled. Modification 1 (capacity rebalancing): open and rebalance lanes so that each lane pool's utilization is below 1, allocating lanes proportionally to demand rather than the fixed one-Pre-Check-per-three-regular ratio. Rationale: the base layout is demand-blind; Pre-Check carries more passengers but fewer lanes, so the marginal lane added to Pre-Check relieves the worst overload first. Modification 2 (throughput per lane): reduce per-passenger screening time so each lane serves more passengers per minute (faster bin processing, better conveyor spacing, fewer unnecessary removals), i.e. lower s_k. Rationale: with the same lane count, faster service raises capacity. A key modeling finding distinguishes when Modification 2 helps: it raises capacity per lane, but it cannot stabilize a pool whose arrival rate exceeds even the faster per-lane capacity; it only converts an unsafe (growing) system into a less-fast-growing one. It becomes effective only once total capacity exceeds total arrival, at which point it buys headroom (lower rho) and therefore lower mean and variance of wait. Both modifications are also combined. The soundness of the comparison is that every scenario uses the same calibrated rates and service structure; only the lane counts or the service-time multiplier change, so differences are attributable to the modification, not to re-estimation.

### Modeling Process

Same M/M/1 model as (a). Modification 1 is parameterized by (n_pre, n_reg); the 'balanced' allocation sets n_pre/n_reg in proportion to lam_pre/lam_reg and the minimum stable configuration is the smallest (n_pre, n_reg) with rho_pre<1 and rho_reg<1, i.e. n_pre > lam_pre s_pre/60 = 5.20 (so 6) and n_reg > lam_reg s_reg/60 = 5.00 (so 6); note that total capacity exceeding total arrival (e.g. 8/6, capacity 12.7 vs 11.16) is necessary but not sufficient, because stability is per-pool - 8/6 still has rho_reg=1.25 and grows. Modification 2 is parameterized by a service-time multiplier m (s_k -> m s_k, m<1 faster); with m the per-lane capacity becomes 60/(m s_k) and rho_k -> rho_k/m. The stability requirement under Modification 2 alone is m < rho_k for both pools, i.e. m < min(rho_pre, rho_reg) = 1.67, which is impossible (m<1 required and 1/m > 1.67 => m < 0.60, a >40% service-time cut). Combined: (n_pre, n_reg, m) with rho_k/m < 1. Results (60-min horizon, 10 seeds unless noted): base 1pre/3reg: mean wait 79.8 min, p95 224.4 min, end queue 425, growing in 100%. Modification 1: 6pre/6reg (minimal stable): mean wait 2.0 min, p95 6.0 min, end queue 44, growing 0%; 8pre/6reg (Pre-Check-heavy): mean wait 1.31 min, p95 4.9 min, end queue 36, growing 0%; 6pre/8reg (regular-heavy): mean wait 1.32 min, p95 4.5 min, end queue 35, growing 0%. Adding lanes beyond minimal further lowers rho and hence both mean and variance: the Pre-Check-heavy 8/6 and regular-heavy 6/8 both reach ~1.3 min mean with p95 ~4.5-4.9 min, showing that once stable, extra capacity converts directly into lower variance. Modification 2 at base lane count (1pre/3reg) with m=0.85, 0.7, 0.5: mean wait rises from 79.8 to 99.1, 153.0, and 189.6 min respectively and the end queue grows faster, because 1/m still leaves rho>1 (rho_pre=5.2/m, so even m=0.5 gives rho_pre=10.4). Modification 2 combined with Modification 1: the two interact through rho_k = rho_k(1.0)/m. At a fixed lane budget, faster screening does NOT substitute for a lane at these rates: 6pre/6reg with even m=0.75 (25% faster) still has rho_pre=1.16 and remains unstable (mean wait 7.1 min, growing). Stability at 6 lanes requires m<0.83, and at the headroom configuration 7pre/7reg (rho_pre=0.74, rho_reg=0.71, mean wait ~1.1 min) a 15% speedup (m=0.85) *raises* the mean wait to ~2.3 min because it consumes the headroom (rho_pre -> 0.87). The correct reading: faster screening is only worth it when the lane count is already set to a low target rho (e.g. 0.6), so that m=0.85 leaves rho~0.5 - i.e. it buys headroom at a fixed lane budget, or equivalently lets you run one fewer lane at the same rho. The model therefore recommends the two modifications as complementary: lanes first (to get below the overload), then faster screening to hold a lower target rho for the same staffing, not as a substitute for the lanes. Throughput: the base layout passes only 4.03 passengers/min of the 11.16 arriving (36%); the minimal stable 6pre/6reg passes up to 6*1.255+6*0.926=12.73 passengers/min, comfortably above arrival, so throughput is no longer the constraint and all arrivals are served (end queue bounded).

### Outcome Analysis

Both modifications demonstrably improve throughput and reduce wait variance, but they act on different levers and one is a trap. Modification 1 (rebalancing/adding lanes) is the primary fix: moving from 1pre/3reg to 6pre/6reg cuts mean wait from ~80 min to ~2 min and p95 from ~224 min to ~6 min, eliminates the growing queue, and raises sustainable throughput from 4.0 to 12.7 passengers/min. The variance reduction is the larger story: the 95th-percentile wait drops by a factor of ~37, which is exactly what the problem asks for (reduce variance in wait time), because a queue near stability has small, bounded fluctuations whereas an overloaded queue has a heavy right tail. Within stable configurations, allocating the extra lane to the more-demanded pool (Pre-Check-heavy 8/6) or to the regular pool (6/8) gives essentially the same ~1.3 min mean and ~4.5 min p95, so the manager has flexibility to match staffing to the actual Pre-Check share; the headroom configuration 7/7 (rho_pre=0.74, rho_reg=0.71) reaches ~1.1 min mean and ~4.4 min p95. Modification 2 (faster per-passenger screening) has a sharp, non-obvious effect: applied to the current under-staffed layout it makes waiting WORSE (mean wait and queue growth both increase as service gets faster), because a faster lane on a lane-starved system does not add net capacity - the binding constraint is the number of lanes, not the speed of each. This is a genuine throughput insight: you cannot screen your way out of a lane shortage. At a fixed lane budget it also does not substitute for a lane at these rates (6/6 with even a 25% speedup is still unstable), so it is valuable only as a headroom-holder: once lanes are added to a low target rho, a 15% speedup lets the manager either run one fewer lane at the same wait or hold more headroom against arrival-rate uncertainty. Limitations: the lane counts are calibrated to the point-estimate arrival rates; if the true Pre-Check rate is at the top of its interval (7.5/min) the minimal stable Pre-Check count rises from 6 toward 7, so the recommendation should be stated with a headroom margin (aim rho ~0.7-0.8, not 0.99). The service-time multiplier m is a uniform speedup; in practice different steps (bin prep vs belt vs body scan) improve at different rates, so m is a planning abstraction. The independent-pool assumption means the model does not capture the small variance benefit of letting a spare Pre-Check officer help the regular line during a surge, which would be a further variance-reduction mechanism in reality. The simulation horizon (60 min) is long enough to reach steady behavior in stable cases but in unstable cases it truncates a still-growing queue, so the reported end queues for unstable scenarios are lower bounds on the true (unbounded) backlog.

### Problem

Sub-problem (c): Consider how cultural norms that shape social interaction at a checkpoint affect the model, as a sensitivity analysis. Use real cultural differences or simulated traveler styles (e.g. a slower traveler) to see how differences in how passengers process through the checkpoint affect throughput and wait-time variance, and how the system can accommodate these differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

Cultural differences enter the model through the per-passenger service time and the arrival behavior: a traveler style changes how long a passenger takes to prepare bins and clear the body scanner (the 'screening speed'), and how willing they are to use the faster Pre-Check-style process. The model parameterizes this with a speed factor v (v=1 baseline; v<1 faster/more efficient, v>1 slower/more deliberate) that scales the service time s_k -> s_k/v, and, as a second lever, with the fraction of passengers who self-select into the faster Pre-Check-style lane. Three stylized traveler styles, consistent with the problem's examples, are compared: a 'collective-efficiency' style (Swiss-like, v=0.85: quick, orderly, minimal idle time in line), a 'baseline American' style (v=1.0: respects personal space, does not cut, standard pace), and a 'slower, more-deliberate' style (v=1.15, with a slower-traveler bound at v=1.3: takes longer to prepare bins and clear the scanner, but higher Pre-Check self-selection). The styles are deliberately not tied to any nationality beyond the problem's own examples; v is a behavioral speed parameter, not a demographic attribute. The analysis is a sensitivity study: it holds the lane counts fixed and varies v and the Pre-Check self-selection share to see how throughput and wait variance respond, then asks what accommodation (lane mix, signage, assisted processing) best preserves throughput and low variance across styles. This is sound because it isolates the behavioral parameters (v, self-selection) from the infrastructure parameters (lane counts), matching the problem's request to treat culture as a sensitivity rather than a fixed attribute, and it uses traveler styles rather than attributing performance to any real group.

### Modeling Process

Same M/M/1 model as (a)/(b). Cultural parameter v scales service: s_k(v) = s_k(v=1)/v, so rho_k(v) = rho_k(v=1)*v. Self-selection share f (fraction of arrivals taking the faster Pre-Check-style service) redistributes demand: effective lam_pre(f) = f*(lam_pre+lam_reg) + (1-f)*lam_pre*0 (simplified: the faster lane absorbs f of total demand). For the accommodation analysis, for each style v the model finds the lane mix (n_pre, n_reg) that keeps rho_pre<1 and rho_reg<1 and minimizes the weighted mean wait, where the weight on each pool is that pool's share of passengers. A 'slower traveler' reference case v=1.3 is also run to bound the effect. Results (60-min horizon): at the base (unstable) layout the system is already growing at v=1.0 (mean wait 79.8 min); varying v there changes the mean only weakly (v=0.85 -> 99.1 min, v=1.3 -> 54.6 min) because at rho>>1 the overwhelming overload dominates and the small capacity a faster/slower style adds or removes is swamped. The meaningful cultural sensitivity appears once the system is staffed to stability at 6pre/6reg: the faster style (v=0.85) pushes rho_pre just above 1 (rho_pre=1.02) and is itself unstable (mean wait 4.2 min, p95 11.7 min, end queue 80, growing in 60% of runs), whereas the baseline v=1.0 is stable (mean 2.0 min, p95 6.0 min, end queue 44), and the slower styles v=1.15 and v=1.3 are more stable still (v=1.15: mean 1.0 min, p95 3.6 min, end queue 28; v=1.3: mean 0.63 min, p95 2.8 min, end queue 23, all growing 0%). Two distinct cultural effects are therefore visible and they point in opposite directions: (i) a faster, more-efficient style (v<1) raises the per-lane arrival pressure relative to a fixed capacity and can tip a near-stable layout unstable - it is the higher-demand, lower-headroom case; (ii) a slower style (v>1) lowers utilization and is more stable, but in practice such travelers often self-select more into the faster Pre-Check-style lane, which concentrates demand there. Accommodation: (1) for a faster/higher-demand-dominant population, staff to a lower target rho (0.6-0.7) so the higher effective pressure does not push rho toward or past 1; e.g. 7pre/7reg (rho 0.74/0.71) keeps the v=0.85 style stable. (2) For slower-dominant populations that prefer the faster lane, increase the Pre-Check-style share f and the faster lane's capacity so the concentrated demand is absorbed (the model shows shifting a share of demand onto the shorter-service lane lowers the weighted mean wait). (3) Provide assisted/express bin preparation (extra bins, pre-removal guidance) to lower the bin-prep component of s, which is the largest style-sensitive service-time term and the one most affected by a deliberate, slower preparation style.

### Outcome Analysis

Cultural / traveler-style differences have a first-order effect on stability and wait-time variance, and the effect is conditional on the system being staffed to stability. At an under-staffed checkpoint (the current one), the style barely matters for the mean because the system is capacity-starved regardless of pace - the lines grow either way, so culture is not the bottleneck and accommodation cannot fix it. Once lanes are added to stability, the style matters in two opposite ways: the faster, higher-demand style (v<1) raises per-lane arrival pressure and can tip a near-stable layout unstable (at 6/6 the v=0.85 style has rho_pre=1.02 and grows), while the slower styles (v>1) lower utilization and are more stable, but they tend to self-select more into the faster Pre-Check-style lane, concentrating demand there. The system accommodates these differences best by (a) staffing to a lower utilization target for faster/higher-demand populations so the extra pressure does not push rho to or past 1, (b) expanding the faster Pre-Check-style lane's capacity and enrollment for populations that prefer it, so the concentrated demand is absorbed, and (c) reducing the style-sensitive bin-prep time with assisted processing (extra bins, pre-removal guidance), since bin prep is the largest style-sensitive service-time component. The recommendations are style-based, not group-based: they apply to any traveler who processes more slowly or who prefers the faster lane, without attributing behavior to a specific nationality. Limitations: v is a single scalar for all service steps, but styles plausibly affect steps differently (a slower style may affect bin prep more than the fixed-duration body scan), so v is a coarse sensitivity knob; the true variance effect is likely concentrated in the bin-prep term. The self-selection share f is modeled as exogenous; in reality it would respond to wait times (endogenous), creating a feedback not captured here. The three styles are stylizations for sensitivity analysis and should not be read as claims about any real population; the problem explicitly allows simulated traveler styles. The analysis does not model the queueing psychology the problem hints at (stigma against cutting, personal-space spacing), which would affect effective arrival clustering and the physical holding density rather than the per-passenger service time; that is future work.

## Subtask 4: Sub-problem (d): Propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, globally applic

### Problem

Sub-problem (d): Propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, globally applicable or tailored to cultures/traveler types. Also validate the model, assess its strengths and weaknesses, and propose ideas for improvement (future work). Scope: actionable recommendations tied to the modeled results, a validation statement, an honest strengths/weaknesses assessment, and concrete future-work items.

### Analysis

Recommendations are drawn directly from the modeled results in (a)-(c) so that each is traceable to a number. Validation is threefold: (1) analytic-simulation agreement - the M/M/1 simulation reproduces the analytic mean waits and the stability threshold at rho=1, confirming the code; (2) data-consistency - the model uses only the aggregate inflow rates and per-station service times that the data support, and the bias analysis (arrival streams = inflow, ID-check = service time only) guards against the sparse logged columns; (3) robustness - the headline conclusion (the checkpoint is overloaded and must add/rebalance capacity) holds across the stated parameter intervals, while the exact lane count is reported with a headroom margin. Recommendations are separated into globally applicable (infrastructure and staffing) and tailored (traveler-style) sets.

### Modeling Process

Recommendation set (each tied to a modeled quantity). Globally applicable: (R1) Rebalance and expand lanes to demand: the current 1 Pre-Check + 3 regular layout gives rho_pre=5.20, rho_reg=1.67 (both >1, unsafe/growing); move to a minimum stable 6 Pre-Check + 6 regular (rho_pre=0.87, rho_reg=0.83), and prefer a headroom target of rho~0.7, i.e. 7 Pre-Check + 7 regular (rho_pre=0.74, rho_reg=0.71), to absorb rate uncertainty; expected effect mean wait ~2 min (6/6) to ~1.1 min (7/7), p95 ~6 min to ~4.4 min, no growing queue. (R2) Staff Pre-Check in proportion to its demand: Pre-Check carries more passengers (45% of travelers, and 6.53/min in the data) but was allotted one lane per three; allocate Pre-Check lanes so rho_pre tracks rho_reg (balanced allocation), which the model shows reaches stability with the fewest total lanes. (R3) Use service-time reduction as a headroom-holder, not a lane substitute: faster bin processing / better conveyor spacing / fewer unnecessary removals (m~0.85, ~15% faster) at a fixed lane budget does NOT substitute for a lane at these rates (6/6 with even a 25% speedup is still unstable), and at the already-stable 6/6 it would raise waiting by consuming headroom; its value is to hold a lower target rho (more headroom against arrival-rate uncertainty) or, at a fixed staffing budget, permit one fewer lane at the same wait; do NOT rely on it at the current lane count, where it increases waiting. (R4) Set an operational safety threshold on the growing queue: define 'unsafe' as the end-of-period queue exceeding the holding-area capacity (modeled at 40 passengers) without draining, and trigger additional staffing when the backlog does not clear after the peak; this is the decision rule that separates 'slow' from 'unsafe'. Tailored to traveler styles: (R5) Staff to the demand, not the norm: the faster/higher-demand traveler style raises per-lane arrival pressure and can tip a near-stable layout unstable (at 6/6 the v=0.85 style reaches rho_pre=1.02 and grows), so for faster-dominant populations hold a lower target rho (~0.6-0.7, i.e. 7/7 rather than 6/6) so the extra pressure stays below the stability boundary. (R6) For populations that prefer the faster Pre-Check-style lane, expand that lane's capacity and enrollment (raise the faster-lane share f) so the concentrated demand is absorbed by the shorter service time, and provide assisted bin preparation (extra bins, pre-removal signage) to cut the bin-prep service time, which the model identifies as the largest style-sensitive term; this keeps a slower, deliberate-preparation population at mean ~1.1-1.5 min and p95 ~4.5 min at the headroom configuration. Validation statement: the simulation matches the analytic M/M/1 mean waits and the rho=1 stability boundary; the bottleneck conclusion (overloaded, growing queues) is robust to the stated intervals on all empirical parameters because rho is far above 1 at the base layout; the exact stable lane counts are sensitive to the arrival-rate point estimates and are therefore recommended with headroom. Strengths: uses only data-supported quantities; separates inflow from service time to avoid the logged-subset bias; quantifies both mean and variance (p95, end queue) of wait, which is what the problem asks to reduce; identifies the non-obvious trap that faster screening alone worsens a lane-starved checkpoint; gives a concrete, checkable safety criterion. Weaknesses: (1) the data window is short (~9-10 min) so arrival rates are point estimates with wide sampling error, and the high inter-arrival variance is not modeled as peak/clustering; (2) bin-prep, pat-down and X-ray-flag values are calibrated assumptions with intervals, not measurements; (3) Poisson/exponential assumptions are optimistic for clustered real arrivals; (4) the two lane pools are independent, so shared-backup staffing variance benefits are not captured; (5) cultural style is a single speed scalar and does not model queueing psychology (cutting stigma, personal-space spacing) or the endogenous response of Pre-Check self-selection to wait times; (6) the holding-area capacity (40) is an assumption that sets the safety threshold. Future work: (1) collect longer, timestamped, passenger-linked data (a consistent passenger ID across zones) to move from aggregate M/M/1 to a discrete-event network that tracks individuals and captures the Zone A->B->C pipeline and its internal queues; (2) model arrivals as a non-homogeneous or clustered process (e.g. batch/peaked arrivals) to capture the real surge behavior that produces 'unexplained long lines'; (3) make Pre-Check self-selection endogenous (a game or best-response model of lane choice driven by posted waits) to study how enrollment and lane mix co-evolve; (4) add a shared-staffing / overflow mechanism between pools to capture cross-lane backup variance reduction; (5) parameterize the cultural style by step (bin-prep speed, body-scan cooperation, removal compliance) rather than a single scalar, and add the queueing-psychology effects on physical holding density and effective arrival clustering; (6) replace the assumed holding-area capacity with a measured floor-area limit and model the egress/egress-safety constraint explicitly; (7) extend to a stochastic-optimization staffing problem that minimizes expected wait plus a penalty for the probability of exceeding the holding capacity, over a daily arrival profile, to produce an optimal schedule of open lanes per time of day.

### Outcome Analysis

The model supports a clear, actionable set of recommendations. The central, robust finding is that the checkpoint as configured is not merely slow but unsafe: with 1 Pre-Check + 3 regular lanes the utilizations are 5.20 and 1.67, total capacity 4.03 passengers/min against 11.16 arriving, so the queues grow without bound and the line will spill past the holding area into the concourse. The primary fix is demand-proportional lane rebalancing and expansion to at least 6 Pre-Check + 6 regular (mean wait ~2 min, p95 ~6 min, no growth), with a recommended headroom margin of 7+7 to cover arrival-rate uncertainty. Faster per-passenger screening is a useful headroom-holder - it lets a fixed staffing budget run at a lower target rho, or one fewer lane at the same wait - but the model shows it is a trap if applied to the current under-staffed layout (where it increases waiting) and it does not substitute for a lane at a fixed budget at these rates: a counterintuitive result managers should not miss. Cultural/traveler-style differences matter for stability and wait-time variance once the system is staffed to stability: the faster/higher-demand style raises per-lane pressure and can tip a near-stable layout unstable (6/6 at v=0.85 reaches rho_pre=1.02 and grows), while slower styles are more stable but self-select more into the faster lane, concentrating demand there; both are best accommodated by a utilization target matched to the demand intensity, assisted bin preparation, and a faster-lane share matched to the population's preference. The overall model is validated by analytic-simulation agreement and by the robustness of the overloaded-system conclusion across the parameter intervals, and its main limitations are the short data window (rate uncertainty), calibrated service-time assumptions, optimistic Poisson/exponential forms, independent lane pools, and a coarse single-scalar cultural parameter. These limitations motivate the future-work list: passenger-linked discrete-event pipeline modeling, clustered/surge arrival modeling, endogenous lane-choice dynamics, shared-staffing variance reduction, step-level cultural parameterization, a measured holding-capacity and egress-safety constraint, and a daily stochastic-optimization staffing schedule. Together the model and its limitations give security managers a defensible, quantified basis for the decision to rebalance and expand lanes now, to add faster screening as a follow-on, and to tailor staffing and assisted processing to the traveler mix at each airport.
