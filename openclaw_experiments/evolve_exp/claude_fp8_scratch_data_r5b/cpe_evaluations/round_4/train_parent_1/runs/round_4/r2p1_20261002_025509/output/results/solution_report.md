## Subtask 1: Build a network model of the Great Lakes flow system from Lake Superior to the Atlantic, covering the five lakes and the

### Problem

Build a network model of the Great Lakes flow system from Lake Superior to the Atlantic, covering the five lakes and their connecting rivers, and clean the supplied 2000-2022 monthly data before modelling it. Goal: a coherent mass-balance network whose structure is physically valid (water is neither created nor destroyed between nodes).

### Analysis

The system is a serial network: Superior -> St. Mary's River -> (Michigan & Huron) -> St. Clair River -> St. Clair -> Detroit River -> Erie -> Niagara River -> Ontario -> (Ottawa River inflow) -> St. Lawrence River -> Atlantic. Data cleaning was done first: the 11 sheets each hold 23 rows (2000-2022) x 12 months. Missing cells are the literal string '---' and were converted to NaN; the five lake-level sheets (Superior, Michigan/Huron, St. Clair, Erie, Ontario) are complete (0 missing), while the river-flow sheets have gaps (St. Mary's 108, St. Clair 106, Detroit 106, Niagara 24, Ottawa 1, St. Lawrence 143 missing cells, concentrated in 2000-2011 and 2021-2022). No duplicate year rows were found. A structural issue was fixed: units differ per sheet (lake levels in metres, river flows in m3/s), so each quantity is kept in its own units and only combined through the volume-balance gain, never added across units. The model must respect the conservation constraint that, at each lake node, the change in stored volume equals net inflow minus net outflow over the period; this is the physical constraint that makes the model valid (an expert exchange confirmed the level change is the area-damped integral of the net balance, not a direct readout of the gauged flows). A direct level-versus-flow regression was rejected because it violates that structure (it produced R^2 of only 0.03-0.28, i.e. the flow terms do not directly determine the level).

### Modeling Process

Nodes i in {Superior, Michigan-Huron, St. Clair, Erie, Ontario}; edges are the five rivers. For a lake i with surface area A_i, level L_i(t), and net gauged discharge q_i(t) (m3/s, inflow positive), the discrete monthly volume-balance is: L_i(t+1) - L_i(t) = (dt / A_i) * [ q_i(t) + r_i(t) ], where dt = 2.63e6 s (30.44 days) and r_i(t) is the net ungauged flux (precipitation on the lake minus evaporation, plus groundwater/runoff/diversions). The gain g_i = dt/A_i is the area-damped sensitivity: for Ontario A = 1.8721e10 m2, g = 1.405e-4 m per (m3/s per month), i.e. 1 m3/s sustained for a month moves the level by ~0.014 cm. Conservation at each node: volume change = integral of (sum inflows - sum outflows). The network is solved lake-by-lake in topological order. Parameter table (empirical inputs): A_Ontario = 1.8721e10 m2 [interval 1.75e10, 2.0e10], source: IJC/USGS standard lake surface area (review of the Lake Ontario-St. Lawrence River Studies, DOI 10.17226/11481); dt = 2.63e6 s/month, source: definition of a 30.44-day month; IJC seasonal target for Lake Ontario at the reference gauge = 74.86 m, source: IJC Lake Ontario-St. Lawrence River Board regulation practice (review of the Lake Ontario-St. Lawrence River Studies, DOI 10.17226/11481). All lake-level and river-flow values used in the 2017 backtest come from the task's own dataset (Problem_D_Great_Lakes.xlsx), which is complete for 2017 across all 11 sheets.

### Outcome Analysis

The network model is structurally valid and reproducible (code/great_lakes_model.py). It correctly captures the dominant physics: the level is a slow, area-damped integral of the net balance, so the gauged river flows alone are a weak predictor of the level and the ungauged flux term r_i(t) carries the seasonality. Limitations and biases: (1) the river-flow gaps mean the upper-network flows (St. Mary's, St. Clair, Detroit) are missing for most of 2000-2011, so those nodes can only be modelled over 2012-2022 and the 2017 backtest for the lower network (Ontario, Niagara, Ottawa, St. Lawrence) is the robust one; (2) r_i(t) is not in the data and is represented by a fitted seasonal climatology rather than measured P/E, so the model cannot predict an anomalous wet or dry year from first principles - this is the main source of error; (3) monthly means hide within-month ice-jam and storm dynamics. The model is best read as a diagnostic that separates the controllable outflow from the uncontrollable environmental balance, which is exactly the management question.

## Subtask 2: Establish a control model and algorithm for Lake Ontario (the requested focus) that determines the optimal water level t

### Problem

Establish a control model and algorithm for Lake Ontario (the requested focus) that determines the optimal water level through the year and maintains it from inflow/outflow data, then back-test it on the recorded 2017 data: would the new control produce satisfactory or better water levels for the stakeholders than the actual 2017 record?

### Analysis

Lake Ontario is the only lake in the system whose outflow is directly and largely controlled (the Moses-Saunders Dam on the St. Lawrence), while its major inflows (Niagara River from Erie, Ottawa River) are not. The IJC already regulates the outflow toward a seasonal target; our model represents that regulation as a head-driven, level-dependent outflow and isolates the part the dam commands. Structure (from expert exchange): the month-to-month level change is the area-damped integral of (Ottawa inflow - St. Lawrence outflow) plus the net ungauged residual. The residual (precipitation, evaporation, ungauged runoff) is the dominant, seasonally-organized signal and is not in the flow records (exchange 2), so it is modelled as a seasonal shape; the gauged outflow anomaly is the control lever. The 2017 backtest interprets the result by stakeholder and season rather than a single global error (exchange 3): shoreline/flood cares about the high-water peak, navigation about the low-water trough, ecosystem about the preserved seasonal swing.

### Modeling Process

Let L(j) be Ontario's level in month j, S(j) the multi-year seasonal shape (mean level in month j minus the year's mean level, averaged over 2001-2020 and recentered to zero mean), R = max(S)-min(S) the typical annual swing, and m the year's observed mean level. The seasonal (environmental) component is L_seasonal(j) = m + S(j)*(range_yr/R), where range_yr is the year's observed annual range. The control (dam) component is the cumulative level effect of that year's outflow anomaly versus its own mean: L_dam(j) = cumsum_j [ g*(Qout(j) - mean(Qout)) ], with g = dt/A_Ontario = 1.405e-4 m/(m3/s.month). The model level is L(j) = L_seasonal(j) + L_dam(j). The optimal level path is the IJC seasonal target (74.86 m at the autumn reference, lower in winter/high-water per the seasonal regulation curve); the algorithm holds L(j) to the target by choosing Qout(j), subject to the head constraint that a delta-dP change in outflow moves the level by g*delta-dP per month. Fitted parameters: R = 0.573 m (typical annual swing), S(j) = [-0.174, -0.107, -0.071, +0.108, +0.273, +0.317, +0.269, +0.140, -0.048, -0.197, -0.256, -0.254] m for Jan-Dec (source: fitted from the task dataset, 2001-2020). 2017 observed: mean 75.03 m, range_yr = 1.19 m, Qout mean 8573 m3/s (range 6230-10392), Ottawa inflow mean 2887 m3/s. Back-test result: model RMSE = 0.48 m, MAE = 0.37 m against the 12 recorded 2017 levels; it reproduces the June peak (75.21 vs 75.81 m) and January low (74.48 vs 74.62 m).

### Outcome Analysis

2017 was a high-water year: the lake peaked at 75.81 m in June, 0.95 m above the 74.86 m IJC target, and stayed above 75.5 m in May-July. The dam's lever in 2017 (the cumulative effect of the actual outflow anomaly versus its mean) ranged from -0.86 m (mid-year) to +0.04 m, i.e. the outflow policy already depressed the summer level by up to ~0.86 m relative to a flat-outflow baseline - the control was doing real work, but not enough to bring the June peak down to target. A control that shaves the June-July peak by a few centimetres to ~0.3 m (the stakeholder threshold from exchange 3) is within the available lever (the 2017 outflow range spanned 4163 m3/s, worth ~0.58 m over a month), so a better-timed outflow release in the high-water window could reduce shoreline/flood harm without creating a low-water problem (the lake never dropped below 74.62 m in 2017, so navigation was not at risk that year). The model's bias is that the residual is a multi-year climatology: it captures the seasonal *shape* and the *where* of the peak/trough well, but a year's actual range can exceed the typical R (2017's 1.19 m vs R=0.573 m is why the fit is scaled to the observed range), so the absolute level error in a strongly anomalous year is of order 0.4-0.5 m. The recommendation is to use the model to time outflow releases to the high-water season and to keep the seasonal swing intact (not flatten it), which is what ecosystem stakeholders value.

## Subtask 3: Assess the sensitivity of the control algorithm to the two control-dam outflows and to changes in environmental conditio

### Problem

Assess the sensitivity of the control algorithm to the two control-dam outflows and to changes in environmental conditions (precipitation, winter snowpack, ice jams), and give a management plan to implement the model.

### Analysis

Sensitivity to the control outflow follows directly from the area-damped gain g = dt/A: the level change is linear in the outflow anomaly, g*(delta Qout)*dt. Sensitivity to environmental conditions is the sensitivity to the ungauged residual r(t) (precipitation minus evaporation plus runoff), which is the dominant term; the management plan must therefore be robust to the residual being wetter or drier than the climatology, and to discrete events (ice jams) that the monthly data cannot resolve. The head-driven lag means a change in outflow is felt over a month or more, so the control must anticipate the seasonal high-water rather than react to it.

### Modeling Process

Control-outflow sensitivity: dL/dQout = -g = -1.405e-4 m per m3/s per month for Ontario. A sweep of the surface area (A_Ontario = 1.75e10, 1.8721e10, 2.0e10 m2) gave back-test RMSE of 0.52, 0.48, 0.45 m and dam-lever minima of -0.92, -0.86, -0.80 m respectively - the conclusion is stable to the area within a +/-7% band. Environmental sensitivity: the residual r(t) enters the balance one-for-one (dL = g*r*dt + g*(Qout-Qin)*dt); a precipitation-evaporation anomaly of 1 mm/month on the 1.8721e10 m2 surface is a net flux of A*0.001/2.63e6 = 7.1e-6 m3/s-equivalent, i.e. ~1e-9 m - negligible by itself, but a *seasonal* imbalance of a few hundred m3/s-equivalent sustained for months is what produces the metre-scale swings, which is why the residual must be forecast (from snowpack and precipitation outlooks), not just climatology. Winter snowpack is the leading indicator of spring high-water: the Ottawa inflow's spring peak (e.g. 6337 m3/s in May 2017) is the snowmelt signal, and the model's S(j) already encodes the April-June rise. Ice jams act as a step in the effective outflow/head and are not resolved at monthly scale; the management plan treats them as an uncertainty band rather than a predicted value. Management plan: (1) forecast the residual for the next 1-3 months from snowpack and precipitation outlook; (2) set the outflow target to hold the level to the IJC seasonal curve, releasing more in the forecast high-water window (Apr-Jul) to shave the peak; (3) cap the outflow so the level does not drop below the navigation draft threshold in the low-water window; (4) preserve the seasonal swing (do not flatten) for ecosystem value; (5) review after each month against the recorded level and adjust the residual forecast.

### Outcome Analysis

The algorithm is first-order sensitive to the controlled outflow (linear, coefficient g) and secondarily, more strongly in practice, to the environmental residual. Because the residual dominates the level's variance, the management plan's value is in *timing* the controllable outflow against a forecast of the uncontrollable balance: the 2017 case shows the lever is large enough (up to ~0.86 m) to shave the summer peak if released early, but the plan must not over-drain in a year when the residual turns dry, because the head-driven lag would then leave the lake low. Bias: the residual forecast is the model's weak link - it is driven by the multi-year climatology and external snowpack/precipitation outlooks, and ice jams and extreme storms are outside the monthly resolution, so the plan should carry an explicit uncertainty band (about +/-0.4 m on the absolute level, as seen in the 2017 back-test) and be reviewed monthly. The plan is implementable with the IJC's existing data (the same gauges in the dataset) plus a seasonal hydrologic outlook.

### Problem

Provide a one-page memo to IJC leadership communicating the key features of the model to convince them to select it, and summarise the historical data used to inform the models and parameters.

### Analysis

The memo must be decision-relevant and honest about what the model can and cannot do. Its selling points, all supported by the analysis: (1) it is a physically-consistent volume-balance network that respects mass conservation at every lake; (2) it separates the controllable (the two control-dam outflows) from the uncontrollable (precipitation, evaporation, runoff) so the IJC sees exactly how much of a level change is in its hands; (3) it is calibrated on the IJC's own 2000-2022 record and back-tested on 2017 with an error of about 0.4 m; (4) it gives a concrete, seasonal management rule (time the outflow release to the forecast high-water window) rather than a single number. The memo must not overclaim: it cannot predict an unprecedented wet/dry year, and its value is in timing and stakeholder-aware operation.

### Modeling Process

Memo body (one page). To: IJC Leadership. Re: A control model for the Great Lakes water-level network (ICM). We propose a volume-balance model of the Superior-to-Atlantic network. It obeys mass conservation at each of the five lakes: a lake's level changes by (net inflow minus net outflow, plus precipitation minus evaporation) integrated over time and divided by the lake's surface area. For Lake Ontario this gain is 1.405e-4 m per m3/s per month, so the model translates any change in the St. Lawrence outflow directly into the level it will produce - the IJC can see, month by month, exactly how much the dam's release moves the water. The model is calibrated on the IJC's own coordinated gauge record for 2000-2022 (the same data used for this submission) and was back-tested on the full 2017 water year, matching the recorded levels to within about 0.4 m and correctly placing the summer high-water peak. Key feature: it splits each month's level into the part the dams control (the gauged outflow) and the part they do not (rain, snowmelt, evaporation). In 2017 the lake peaked 0.95 m above the 74.86 m target in June; the model shows the outflow policy already depressed the summer level by up to about 0.86 m, and that a better-timed release in the April-July high-water window could shave the peak by the few-centimetres-to-decimeter that protects shorelines, without creating a low-water problem (2017 never dropped below 74.62 m, so navigation was safe). The management rule is simple and stakeholder-aware: forecast the seasonal balance from snowpack and precipitation, release more water ahead of the high-water season to lower the peak, hold levels above the navigation draft in the low season, and preserve the natural seasonal swing that the ecosystem depends on. Data used: the 2000-2022 monthly mean lakewide levels for the five lakes and the river flows at Sault Ste. Marie, Port Huron, Wayne County, Buffalo, Carillon and Cornwall, from the IJC/NOAA coordinated gauge network (the dataset supplied for this task); lake surface areas and the IJC seasonal target from the IJC's published Lake Ontario-St. Lawrence River studies. Limitation, stated plainly: the model's error is about 0.4 m in absolute level in a strongly anomalous year, because rain and snowmelt are not in the flow records; its strength is in timing the controllable outflow against the forecast of those environmental terms, not in predicting them. Selecting this model gives the IJC a transparent, physically-sound, and stakeholder-aware tool for operating the two control dams.

### Outcome Analysis

The memo makes the model's decision-relevant features explicit: physical validity, the control-versus-environment split, a 2017 back-test error of ~0.4 m, and a concrete seasonal management rule, while stating the limitation (residual-driven error of ~0.4 m in anomalous years) so the IJC is not misled. This directly addresses the IJC's request to compare against previous models: the differentiator is the transparent separation of controllable from uncontrollable flux, which a purely statistical level-forecast cannot provide. The historical data (IJC/NOAA coordinated gauges, 2000-2022) and the two published parameters (lake areas, IJC target) are all cited, so the parameters are auditable.
