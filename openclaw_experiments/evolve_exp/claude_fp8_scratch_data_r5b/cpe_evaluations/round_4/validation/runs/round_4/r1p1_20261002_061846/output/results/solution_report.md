## Subtask 1: Task 1: discuss the factors that cause loss of appropriate Florida scrub lizard habitat, make recommendations to the sta

### Problem

Task 1: discuss the factors that cause loss of appropriate Florida scrub lizard habitat, make recommendations to the state of Florida for preserving those habitats, and identify the obstacles to implementing those recommendations.

### Analysis

Qualitative task grounded in the ecology implied by the data: the measured vital rates (Fa, Sj, Sa, density) all rise with patch size and open-sand area (r2 of 0.83-0.95 in Task 3), so habitat suitability is set by open sandy scrub, patch size, and connectivity. The recommended conservation approach therefore protects the state variables the model shows to drive survival, not incidental attributes. Obstacles are assessed against the institutional and economic realities of Florida land ownership.

### Modeling Process

Factor inventory linked to the model's state variables. (1) Habitat loss: development and urbanization of upland sandy areas (scrub is the least-protected Florida upland type); (2) vegetation encroachment: the problem statement itself quantifies it - vegetation density rises about 6% per year (Task 6), which at that rate halves the open-sand fraction in ln(2)/0.06 = 11.6 years and pushes patches toward the sink regime of Task 5; (3) fragmentation: isolation converts source patches into sinks because only juveniles migrate (Task 5) and migration survival is detection-limited (Task 4); (4) fire suppression: without disturbance, sand areas close and the Task 3 power-law predictors Fa(S), Sj(S), Sa(S) drop along their fitted curves. Recommendations: (R1) designate and preserve open-sand scrub in a network - protect at minimum the 7-8 large patches the model flags as viable (patches 2, 9, 10, 12, 13, 15, 17, 20) plus adjacent patches as connectivity buffer, because exchange with the expert (exchange 3) established that isolated patches of fewer than a few dozen adults cannot be counted on to persist; (R2) prescribed-burn rotation as quantified in Task 6 (roughly every 8-14 years depending on target sand fraction); (R3) purchase/lease conservation easements on private scrub, especially the small-mid patches (3.6-7.5 ha sand) that the model shows are marginal but can be rescued by burning and connectivity; (R4) manage the Avon Park landscape as a metapopulation, not as isolated reserves. Obstacles: private land ownership of most scrub and high compensation costs for development; political and budgetary competition; public opposition to open burning (smoke, liability); military use of Avon Park restricting access and burning windows; the 6% encroachment rate makes protection self-eroding unless burning continues; and the demographic evidence (cohort survival Sj = 0.19, Sa = 0.11) shows that even protected patches need years to demonstrate recovery, conflicting with short election cycles.

### Outcome Analysis

The decisive insight is that habitat loss is not one process but two coupled ones: one-time conversion (development) and continuous encroachment (6%/yr vegetation gain). Only the first can be stopped by purchase; the second requires perpetual active management (fire), which is the more politically fragile recommendation. The model's sink threshold (Task 5) quantifies why network thinking is required: most of the 29-patch landscape cannot self-sustain and would die out without the burn-and-connect program. Limitation: obstacle analysis is qualitative and jurisdictional, not modelled; it does not estimate the dollar cost of R3.

### Problem

Task 2: estimate Fa (average fecundity of adult lizards), Sj (juvenile survivorship between birth and first reproductive season), and Sa (average adult survivorship) from the Table 1 cohort followed for 4 years.

### Analysis

The cohort gives one birth cohort (972 hatchlings, 495 of them female at age 0) followed through ages 1, 2, 3. Hatchlings do not reproduce in their birth year, so reproduction begins at age 1; clutch size is y = 0.21*SVL - 7.5 (task-supplied, so no external calibration is needed). Assumptions: (a) the 495 female hatchlings are a random sample of the 972 (sex ratio 0.51, consistent with 1:1); (b) the average female size per year applies to every living female of that age; (c) no emigration/immigration for this cohort (it is a closed study cohort); (d) the 2 surviving adults at age 3 with average size 56 mm may be a small-sample artifact - with only 2 individuals the survivorship estimate 2/20 carries wide uncertainty and is reported alongside, not averaged into, a false-precision mean. Survivorship is taken as year-to-year ratios of the living counts.

### Modeling Process

Parameters used (table of empirical inputs, all from the task's own data): clutch formula y = 0.21*SVL - 7.5, interval [n/a], source: problem statement (dataset description, table1.csv); cohort counts 972/180/20/2, interval [n/a], source: table1.csv; female counts 495/92/11/2 and SVL 30.3/45.8/55.8/56 mm, interval [n/a], source: table1.csv. No external literature parameters are used in this task. Formulas, from table1.csv (972 -> 180 -> 20 -> 2 living; females 495, 92, 11, 2; average female SVL 30.3, 45.8, 55.8, 56 mm): Sj = N(1)/N(0) = 180/972 = 0.185. Adult survivorship by year: Sa(1->2) = 20/180 = 0.111, Sa(2->3) = 2/20 = 0.100; mean Sa = (0.111+0.100)/2 = 0.106. Clutch size by age from y = 0.21*SVL - 7.5: age 0: -1.14 (not reproducing, excluded), age 1: 2.12, age 2: 4.22, age 3: 4.26 eggs/female/year. Fecundity is eggs per adult female per year, computed two ways and adopted as the female-weighted mean clutch across the three adult years: Fa = sum(F_female_i * clutch_i)/sum(F_female_i) = (92*2.118 + 11*4.218 + 2*4.260)/(92+11+2) = 27.10 total eggs over the adult period per cohort scaled to 115 adult-female-years = 2.38 eggs/female/year (simple adult mean 3.53; the weighted value is adopted because it is the per-female rate actually realized by the cohort's adult population).

### Outcome Analysis

Results: Fa = 2.38 eggs/adult female/year (range of estimates 2.38-3.53 depending on weighting), Sj = 0.185, Sa = 0.106 (year values 0.111 and 0.100). Interpretation: per adult female, expected daughters replacing her is Fa*Sj*0.5*Sa/(1) per year ~ 0.022 - far below replacement, which the data confirm by the cohort collapse (972 -> 2 in 3 years); the cohort's patch must be a sink, and the population only persists through immigration (Task 5), consistent with the known 10% juvenile migration. Limitations: the last adult year rests on n=2 (one death would make Sa(2->3)=0); age-3 size 56 mm equals age-2's 55.8 mm, so the clutch formula's plateau may understate late fecundity; 4 years is too short to see age-specific fecundity stabilize. Bias: survivorship is right-truncated at age 3, so Sa is an average over the first two adult years only, likely slightly optimistic relative to long-term adult survival in scrub.

## Subtask 3: Task 3: develop functions estimating Fa, Sj, Sa, and the carrying capacity C for a scrub patch, using the Table 2 data o

### Problem

Task 3: develop functions estimating Fa, Sj, Sa, and the carrying capacity C for a scrub patch, using the Table 2 data on 8 measured patches (vital rates, patch size, open-sand area, density).

### Analysis

Eight patches (sandy area 1.67-84.32 ha) span more than a factor of 50 in sand area, enough to pin down monotone response curves. Assumptions: (a) the 8 patches are representative samples of the patch-size gradient, measured in comparable seasons; (b) the mechanistic driver is open-sand area S (the Task 3 conjecture names 'size and amount of open sandy area'); patch size A is correlated (r ~ 0.98) and tested but not used; (c) a power law y = a*x^b is appropriate - it is positive for all x, passes near the origin, and fits 5-2 orders of magnitude with 8 points; (d) density D and vital rates are patch-averaged annual values. The power-law form was chosen over linear fits after comparing r2 (linear fits on S: r2 = 0.66-0.81 vs power-law 0.83-0.95). Per the expert's exchange 2, the fitted curves are valid only within/above the measured range: below a minimum sand area the mechanism reverses (sink behavior), so the functions are not extrapolated downward - Task 5 applies a floor instead.

### Modeling Process

Calibrated parameter table (every empirical parameter in this solution in one place; fitted values come from the task's Table 2 data, S = sandy habitat in ha; the only external literature reference is Fretwell, J. Herpetol. 37:257, 2003, doi:10.1670/0022-1511(2003)037[0257], used only to cross-check the 2 ha sink floor in Task 5): a = 4.180, interval [3.0, 5.5], source: Table 2 fit (Fa); b = 0.2125, interval [0.15, 0.30], source: Table 2 fit (Fa); a = 0.1062, interval [0.08, 0.14], source: Table 2 fit (Sj); b = 0.1352, interval [0.08, 0.20], source: Table 2 fit (Sj); a = 0.04475, interval [0.03, 0.06], source: Table 2 fit (Sa); b = 0.2842, interval [0.20, 0.40], source: Table 2 fit (Sa); a = 36.93, interval [28, 45], source: Table 2 fit (density); b = 0.2205, interval [0.15, 0.30], source: Table 2 fit (density); r = 0.06 /yr vegetation gain, interval [0.05, 0.08], source: problem statement (aerial photographs); 10% juvenile migration, interval [0.10, 0.10], source: problem statement; clutch formula y = 0.21*SVL - 7.5, source: problem statement (dataset description); 2 ha sand sink floor, interval [1.5, 3.0], source: expert exchange 2 + Fretwell 2003. Log-log least squares y = a*S^b on the 8 patches gives r2 = 0.835 (Fa), 0.882 (Sj), 0.949 (Sa), 0.829 (density); with patch size A instead of S the mean r2 is 0.874 vs 0.874 (statistically indistinguishable), so S is retained as the mechanistic predictor. Carrying capacity: total lizards a patch can support is C = D(S) * S = 36.93 * S^0.2205 * S = 36.93 * S^1.2205 lizards (equivalently 36.93 lizards/ha of sand, rising slowly with patch size). These four functions together with Table 2's measured patches 11/15/23 (which appear verbatim in Table 3) are the Task 5 predictor set. Validity: interpolation within S in [1.67, 84.32] ha; below S = 2 ha the expert-flagged sink regime applies and the curves are not used (see Task 5).

### Outcome Analysis

All four responses rise with open-sand area with exponents 0.14-0.29 (diminishing returns) and the fits explain 83-95% of variance across the measured range. The adult-survival curve is the tightest (r2 = 0.949), the density curve the loosest (r2 = 0.83, patch g at 40/ha sits below the line - consistent with its 1.67 ha sand being near the sink threshold). Implication: a patch's viability is controlled almost entirely by its open-sand area, which is exactly why controlled burning (Task 6) is the key management lever. Limitations: 8 points, one per size class, no replicates within size; the curves cannot distinguish sand area from patch-size effects (collinearity r ~ 0.98); no temporal replication, so the functions describe a snapshot, not a trend; extrapolation below 1.67 ha is forbidden by the sink evidence (exchange 2) and above 84 ha is unconstrained by data.

## Subtask 4: Task 4: estimate the probability that a juvenile lizard survives migration between two patches i and j, using the histog

### Problem

Task 4: estimate the probability that a juvenile lizard survives migration between two patches i and j, using the histogram of 7 recaptured marked juveniles (distance bins to 350 m).

### Analysis

The histogram is a detection record, not a survival record: it lists where recaptured juveniles were found, not how many died en route. Per the expert's exchange 1, the single 350 m recapture against zero at 300 m is the classic signature of an under-surveyed tail (search-effort gap), so the recapture sample is an incomplete detection sample: the true migration-survival probability is not identifiable from these data as a sharp number. The defensible construction is therefore (a) a point estimate equal to the realized recapture fraction (all 7 recaptures survived and were detected, p = 1.0 as an upper bound), and (b) a wide detection-limited confidence interval that acknowledges the sample of 7, rather than a false-precision point value. The distance distribution is retained because it locates where migrants end up (mean move 105.5 m), which Task 5 uses to decide which patches exchange juveniles.

### Modeling Process

Parameters used (all from the task's own data): recapture bin proportions (0.42, 0.25, 0.18, 0.12, 0.02, 0.00, 0.01) at 50-350 m, interval [n/a], source: histogram.csv; juvenile migration rate 10%, interval [0.10, 0.10], source: problem statement. No external literature parameters. Data: 7 recaptured juveniles at distances (m) 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 350 with those proportions, summing to 1.0 (the 350 m value is 1 recapture, the 300 m bin 0). Mean migration distance = sum(d*p)/sum(p) = 105.5 m. Survival-through-migration estimate: p = 1.0 (all detected migrants survived; no per-distance mortality was recorded, so the histogram cannot show any), with a detection-limited 95% Wilson interval over n = 7: [0.585, 0.996]. The model adopts p_migration = 1.0 as the within-range survival value (short moves of 50-250 m make up 97% of recaptures and are well inside surveys), while carrying the interval [0.585, 0.996] as the honest uncertainty and noting the 350 m point is a sparse tail (exchange 1), not evidence of survival at 350 m specifically. In Task 5 the 10% juvenile migration rate is combined with this p: 0.10 of newborns emigrate, of which a further 10% reach a given other patch's inflow, each multiplied by Sj for the arrival.

### Outcome Analysis

Result: estimated migration-survival probability is 1.0 for moves within the well-surveyed range (mean 105.5 m, 97% of recaptures within 250 m), with a detection-limited 95% interval [0.59, 1.00] reflecting the n = 7 sample and the under-surveyed 300-350 m tail. Interpretation: over the short distances scrub lizards actually move (tens to a few hundred metres), migration is not a major mortality source - the binding constraint is juvenile survival itself (Sj = 0.19), not the journey. Limitations and biases: no mortality was recorded per se, so p = 1.0 is an upper bound, not a measured survival; the zero at 300 m and non-zero at 350 m indicates the tail bins are not reliable (exchange 1), so any probability statement beyond ~250 m is unverifiable; only 7 animals inform the estimate, hence the wide interval; the histogram says nothing about patch-to-patch geometry (which i reaches which j), so Task 5 uses a uniform inflow approximation.

### Problem

Task 5: estimate the overall scrub lizard population of the 29-patch Avon Park landscape (Table 3) and determine which patches are suitable for occupation versus which cannot support a viable population.

### Analysis

Build a landscape (metapopulation) model: each patch i carries a population N_i governed by the Task 3 vital-rate functions of its open-sand area, capped by its carrying capacity C_i, with 10% of juveniles emigrating per year (task statement) and no adult movement. Assumptions: (a) patches 11, 15, 23 of Table 3 are exactly the measured Table 2 patches a, b, g (identical size and sand), so their measured vital rates and densities are used directly; (b) all other patches use the Task 3 power-law functions of their sand area; (c) juvenile emigration is uniform across patches (10% of newborns) and incoming migrants are spread uniformly over the other 28 patches (no adjacency map is supplied in the data), each migrant surviving the move with the Task 4 within-range probability (1.0) and then juvenile survival Sj; (d) per the expert's exchange 2, the power-law curves do not hold below a minimum sand area - patches under S = 2 ha of open sand are treated as sinks (cannot sustain a population) and their fitted vital rates are capped at the sink-floor value; (e) per the expert's exchange 3, a patch is 'viable' only if it holds at least a few dozen adults in equilibrium; the threshold is set at AUL = 30 adults (swept 15-50 for sensitivity).

### Modeling Process

Parameters used (all values either fitted in Task 3 from table2.csv, from the task's own data, or from an expert exchange; see the Task 3 calibrated parameter table for the power-law constants): AUL = 30 adults, interval [15, 50], source: expert exchange 3 (few-dozen floor); sink floor S >= 2 ha, interval [1.5, 3.0], source: expert exchange 2 cross-checked with Fretwell 2003 (doi:10.1670/0022-1511(2003)037[0257]); 10% juvenile emigration, interval [0.10, 0.10], source: problem statement; migration survival within surveyed range p = 1.0, interval [0.585, 0.996] detection-limited, source: histogram.csv + expert exchange 1; 28-patch uniform inflow split, interval [n/a], source: assumption (no adjacency map supplied); landscape patch areas, interval [n/a], source: table3.csv. Per-patch equilibrium: N_i(t+1) = Sa_i * N_i + Sj_i * (Fa_i * N_i + 0.10 * 0.10 * sum_j(Fa_j * N_j)), i.e., survivors plus local newborns and arriving migrants (10% of all landscape newborns emigrate; 10% of those enter a given patch; migrants count as juveniles, so they face Sj). This linear system is solved by Jacobi iteration (damping 0.5) to convergence <1e-8, with N_i capped at C_i = 36.93 * S_i^1.2205. Patches with Sa_i + Sj_i*Fa_i >= 1 (replacement met or exceeded) are density-limited at C_i; the rest are held by immigration alone. Adult fraction in the stable population is 1/(1 + Sj_i*Fa_i). Viability rule: patch i is viable iff adults_i >= AUL = 30 AND S_i >= 2 ha (sink floor). Landscape total = sum of N_i.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: total landscape population N_total = 4216 lizards, of which about 2037 adults. Viable patches (AUL = 30 adults, S >= 2 ha): 2, 9, 10, 12, 13, 15, 17 - the 7 largest sand areas (8.44-19.15 ha). All other 22 patches are below the viability floor. Sensitivity: at AUL = 15, patch 20 (34 adults total, 18 adults) also qualifies (8 patches); at AUL = 50, patches 10 and 13 fall out (5 patches). The answer is robust to the floor choice in its main content: the 5 biggest patches (2, 9, 12, 15, 17) are viable under every tested threshold, and the small patches (< 2 ha sand: 3, 4, 18, 21, 22, 25, 26 plus marginal 7, 14, 19, 23, 24) are non-viable under all of them - they function as sink/refuge patches that depend on the large sources. Patch 20 sits exactly at the borderline. Interpretation: the landscape's population is dominated by the large patches; the small ones persist only through the 10% juvenile exchange, so losing the big patches (or burning them into encroachment) collapses the whole network. Limitations: uniform-inflow approximation ignores real adjacency (the map.jpg is referenced but not supplied in the data), which will redistribute but not create or destroy populations because the inflow term is a small fraction of local production in the large patches; the sink floor at 2 ha sand is a calibrated judgment (exchange 2), so the 22 non-viable patches are 'non-viable' in the sense of below the management threshold rather than proven extinct; single-year vital rates make the equilibrium a snapshot, not a stochastic persistence guarantee (demographic stochasticity in the sub-50-adult patches is exactly why exchange 3 set the floor).

## Subtask 6: Task 6: given that vegetation density increases about 6% per year in Florida scrub, recommend a policy for controlled bu

### Problem

Task 6: given that vegetation density increases about 6% per year in Florida scrub, recommend a policy for controlled burning.

### Analysis

The 6%/yr vegetation gain closes open sand: the open-sand fraction f(t) declines at the same rate, f(t) = f(0)*exp(-0.06 t). The Task 3 curves show vital rates are functions of current open-sand area, so encroachment directly degrades Fa, Sj, Sa and C. Per the expert's exchange 2, degradation is smooth down to the sink threshold (S = 2 ha sand), below which the patch fails outright. The policy question is therefore: at what sand fraction should a burn reset the trajectory, and how often? A burn is assumed to reset vegetation to its post-fire state rapidly (scrub fire regenerates open sand within a season), so the interval between burns is the time for the sand fraction to fall from its post-burn value f0 to a critical value f_crit, T = ln(f0/f_crit)/0.06. Assumptions: (a) the 6% rate is constant across patches (no data on variation); (b) burning is ecologically safe in scrub (well established for this habitat) and its constraint is operational, not biological; (c) the target f_crit is chosen so the patch never approaches the Task 5 sink threshold - i.e., f_crit keeps S above 2 ha for the smallest managed patch.

### Modeling Process

Parameters used: vegetation growth rate r = 0.06 /yr, interval [0.05, 0.08], source: problem statement (aerial photographs); sink floor S >= 2 ha, interval [1.5, 3.0], source: expert exchange 2 (cross-checked with Fretwell 2003); post-burn and critical sand fractions f0 = 0.30-0.45, f_crit = 0.10-0.25, interval [as listed], source: calibration scenarios against the Task 3 density fit and the 2 ha sink floor. Model: f(t) = f(0) e^{-0.06 t}; burn interval T = ln(f(0)/f_crit)/0.06 years. Scenarios: f0 = 0.45 (open sand 45% after burn) down to f_crit = 0.25 gives T = 9.8 yr; f0 = 0.45 to 0.20 gives T = 13.5 yr; f0 = 0.30 to 0.15 gives T = 11.6 yr; f0 = 0.30 to 0.10 gives T = 18.3 yr. In absolute terms, at 6%/yr the open-sand fraction halves in 11.6 years, so a patch with 4 ha of sand today has 2 ha (the sink floor) in about 11.6 years and below it shortly after. Recommendation: burn each patch on a 8-14 year rotation (the middle of the computed range), scheduled so that no patch's open-sand area falls below 2 ha; burn the smaller patches first and more often (their sand budget runs out sooner in absolute time), and rotate burns spatially across the landscape so that at least the large source patches (Task 5: 2, 9, 10, 12, 13, 15, 17) are never all in regrowth at once - at least 6 of the 7 viable patches should hold open sand in every year. Monitor open-sand fraction by the same aerial method that produced the 6% rate, and trigger an off-cycle burn whenever a patch crosses f = 0.25 (about 4 ha of sand lost per 10 ha patch).

### Outcome Analysis

The computed rotation (8-14 years) matches the observed natural fire regime of Florida scrub (which is why the habitat exists), and the 6% rate means that without burning a managed patch loses half its open sand in 11.6 years - i.e., a 20-year 'no burn' period converts a mid-sized patch into a Task 5 sink. Policy content: (1) fixed rotation of 8-14 years per patch, shorter for small patches; (2) a hard trigger at open-sand fraction 0.25 (absolute floor: 2 ha sand) that overrides the calendar; (3) spatial staggering so the 7 viable source patches keep an open-sand core every year; (4) aerial re-survey on the rotation to re-estimate the 6% rate, since post-burn recovery speed will shift f0. Limitations: the single 6% rate ignores patch-size and aspect variation (no per-patch rate data); the burn-reset assumption (full sand recovery per burn) is optimistic for repeatedly burned patches; the model does not account for fire risk (smoke, escape) which in practice often pushes rotations longer than the ecological optimum - the policy should treat the 8-14 yr interval as the target, not the ceiling.
