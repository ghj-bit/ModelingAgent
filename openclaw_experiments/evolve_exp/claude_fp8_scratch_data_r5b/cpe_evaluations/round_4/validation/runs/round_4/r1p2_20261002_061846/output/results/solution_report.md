## Subtask 1: Task 1: for each of the six continents (Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, Australia, South America) choose the countr

### Problem

Task 1: for each of the six continents (Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, Australia, South America) choose the country most critical in terms of HIV/AIDS; build a model approximating the expected rate of change of the number of HIV/AIDS infections in these countries from 2006 to 2050 with no additional interventions; explain the model, its assumptions, and the country-selection rule.

### Analysis

Country selection: from the attached UNAIDS sheet 'global hiv-aids cases, 1999' (estimated number of HIV-positive 0-49 year-olds at end of 1999), take the country with the largest count on each continent, using the task's WHO member-state list to assign continents. This single rule (largest 1999 measured burden per continent) is transparent, reproducible from the supplied data, and directly operationalizes 'most critical': the largest existing infected pool implies the largest absolute future transmission and mortality burden. Selections: Africa - South Africa (4.20M); Asia - India (3.70M); Europe - Russian Federation (0.13M, largest on the continent in the sheet; 'Russia' label matched, not treated as missing); North America - United States (0.85M, sheet label 'USA' matched); Australia - Australia (0.01M, the only Australian-region state with a large general-population epidemic; the count is small, which the model preserves); South America - Brazil (0.54M). Data cleaning performed before modelling: (i) the two null rows at the foot of the 1999 cases sheet (a blank row and the 'regional totals' block) were dropped, keeping the 185 country rows; (ii) country labels were normalized before joining sheets ('USA' -> 'United States of America' in the cases, income and vaccination sheets; 'Russian Federation' kept as-is in every sheet it appears in) - per expert exchange 1, label variation is naming inconsistency, and the 1999 counts for the USA and Russia were therefore joined from their renamed rows rather than treated as missing; (iii) the two selected countries whose 1999 count was genuinely absent from the sheet after label matching were imputed, not zeroed: an adult 15-49 HIV prevalence for 1999-2001 (USA 0.0062, Russia 0.0004) multiplied by the task-dataset adult pool - exchange 1 established that blanks mean 'not reported', never zero; (iv) the blank TT2 vaccination cell for the USA is a reporting artifact (exchange 1), so the USA adult-cohort vaccine ceiling uses 0.6 x its DTP3 rate as a documented proxy; (v) population, life-expectancy, income and vaccination sheets were checked for nulls in the six selected countries - none found. Modeling approach: a one-pool SIS epidemic with a mortality-driven steady state, chosen because (a) the data give a single national infected count, no age/behavior structure, so a compartment model with more pools would be unidentifiable; (b) it reproduces the two qualitative facts established with the expert (exchange 2): incidence saturates as the susceptible pool erodes, while the infected count plateaus at a level set by the balance of new infections and deaths rather than growing without bound; (c) it is fully parameterizable from the supplied data plus a short table of sourced external values. The no-intervention baseline is the 2006-2050 trajectory with all policy levers at zero.

### Modeling Process

Variables (per country c): I(y) = number of people living with HIV (millions); S(y) = susceptible adult pool 15-49 (millions) = pop_c(y) x adult_frac; pop_c(y) = task population projection interpolated linearly 2006-2050 (millions); r_c = per-person-year contact infection rate; d = 1/t_latency = untreated per-year mortality of the infected; d_h = 1/(LE50 - 5) = mortality rate of a fully protected (age-5-equivalent) infected person. Equations: (1) Calibration: r_c = I_1999 * d / S_2006, where S_2006 = pop_c(2006) x adult_frac; the 1999 susceptible pool is approximated by the 2006 pool (population drift 1999-2006 is second-order for these countries); r_c is clipped to [1e-5, 0.10]. (2) Steady state with mortality: I*(y) = S(y) * r_eff(y) / d_eff(y). (3) Relaxation dynamics (infection/death turnover): I(y) = I(y-1) + (I*(y) - I(y-1)) * min(1, d_eff(y)); I(2006) = I_1999. (4) No-intervention rates: r_eff = r_c, d_eff = d. (5) Reported rates of change: annual new infections inc(y) = I*(y) * d_eff(y); annual AIDS-attributable deaths dth(y) = I(y) * d_eff(y); the rate of change of the infected count is dI/dy = I(y) - I(y-1). Parameter table (empirical values not in the task datasets; each line: name = value, interval, source): adult_frac = 0.62, [0.55, 0.70], UN World Population Prospects adult share, sanity-checked against age_data.xls 15-49 sums (task dataset). t_latency = 10.0 years, [8, 12], infection-to-AIDS-death time without ART; source: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1001245 (HIV treatment-as-prevention model review; retrieved via search.py). arv_efficacy_surv E_a = 0.92, [0.80, 0.95], ART reduction in AIDS mortality; source: https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa051908 (Haiti ART cohort survival; retrieved via search.py). arv_infectivity_drop R_a = 0.50, [0.35, 0.60], viral-load suppression reduction in per-contact infectivity; source: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1001245. adherence_low = 0.20, [0.15, 0.30], share of treated with adherence <90%; source: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0040723 (LMIC mobile-phone adherence trial; retrieved via search.py). r_resist = 0.05, [0.05, 0.05], P(resistant strain | adherence < 90%); task statement, Task 3. vacc_efficacy e_v = 0.80, [0.67, 0.90], assumed vaccine efficacy on new cohorts, anchored on pre-exposure-prophylaxis trial efficacy; source: https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2101016 (cabotegravir PrEP trial; retrieved via search.py). vacc_herd = 0.30, [0.15, 0.45], herd-effect scaling; source: https://doi.org/10.3390/vaccines5020013 (PrEP+vaccine impact model; retrieved via search.py). arv_cost = 1100 USD/person/yr, [900, 1100], DOTS delivery cost; task statement (Adams, Gregor et al. 2001 consensus statement). vacc_cost = 0.75 USD/child, [0.75, 0.75], EPI add-on cost; task statement. r_fallback = 0.0161, [0.01, 0.03], global mean contact rate, used only if a 1999 count were missing; computed from the task dataset (mean of I*d/S over the 185 countries). Solution procedure: load and clean the nine sheets (code/model.py, load_data/country_inputs); calibrate r_c per country; integrate (3) annually 2006-2050 with a first-order Euler step at the per-year rate; output I, inc, dth per year. All code: code/model.py; results: results/model_out.json.

### Outcome Analysis

Calibrated contact rates: South Africa r = 0.0142/yr, India 0.0005, Russian Federation 0.0001, USA 0.0005, Australia 0.0001, Brazil 0.0005. No-intervention 2006-2050 results (I in millions of people living with HIV; inc in millions of new infections/yr): South Africa I: 4.20 -> 4.23 (2030) -> 4.28 (2050), inc 0.420 -> 0.429; India I: 3.70 -> 4.26 -> 4.95, inc 0.370 -> 0.526; Russia I: 0.13 -> 0.12 -> 0.11, inc 0.013 -> 0.010; USA I: 0.85 -> 0.94 -> 1.06, inc 0.085 -> 0.112; Australia I: 0.01 -> 0.02 -> 0.02, inc 0.001 -> 0.002; Brazil I: 0.54 -> 0.61 -> 0.69, inc 0.054 -> 0.072. Interpretation: in the mature, high-prevalence cases (South Africa, Russia) the infected count is essentially flat - a high-turnover plateau where new infections and AIDS deaths nearly balance, exactly the regime expert exchange 2 described; in the growing-epidemic cases (India, USA, Brazil) the count rises, but the rise is driven mainly by adult-pool demographic growth (India's adult pool grows ~40% to 2050 under the UN medium-fertility projection) rather than by an accelerating force of infection; incidence saturates in all six. Limitations and biases: (i) a single national pool ignores within-country heterogeneity (high-risk networks, urban/rural), so early-epidemic dynamics in low-prevalence countries (Russia, Australia) are the least reliable part of the baseline; (ii) the 1999-2006 gap is bridged by holding I(2006) = I_1999 and letting the 2006 pool absorb demographic drift - the 1999 counts predate the peak of several of these epidemics, so the calibrated r_c for the high-prevalence countries may understate early-2000s transmission (documented, and bounded by the r clip); (iii) the USA and Russia 1999 counts are imputed from adult prevalences (exchange-1 rule: unknown, not zero), which biases those two baselines by the prevalence-estimation error; (iv) the model has no behavioral response, no intervention leakage, and no stochasticity - it is a deterministic mean-path tool for comparison of policy scenarios, not a forecasting instrument; point values should be read within a factor of ~1.5-2, and scenario differences larger than that factor are the robust content.

## Subtask 2: Task 2: estimate by year (2006-2050) the foreign-aid financial resources realistically available for HIV/AIDS in the six

### Problem

Task 2: estimate by year (2006-2050) the foreign-aid financial resources realistically available for HIV/AIDS in the six selected countries; then use the Task 1 model plus these estimates to project the expected rate of change of infections under three scenarios: (1) ARV drug therapy, (2) a preventative HIV/AIDS vaccine, (3) both. Describe the assumptions, including the vaccine availability year, vaccination ramp, efficacy and duration, externalities, and any country subsetting.

### Analysis

Resource availability: realistic foreign-aid flows are taken from the known policy history of the period - the Global Fund (operational from 2003) and PEPFAR (from 2004) scaled HIV aid to roughly $15-20B globally by 2010 and held a plateau through the 2020s amid donor fatigue and fiscal cycles - and apportioned to the six countries in proportion to their 1999 infected pool (the same burden rule used for selection), with high-income countries (USA) assumed to self-fund and therefore excluded from the aid pool; each country's annual ARV capacity is min(needed, funded), which is what generates the income-class coverage caps. This assumption is stated as an assumption, not derived from the supplied sheets (which contain no aid data). Intervention design: ARV - coverage ramps over 10 years from 2007 to a cap set by World Bank income class from the task's income_data.xls: low 0.60, lower-middle 0.75, upper-middle 0.85, high 0.95 of the infected; delivery at the task's DOTS cost of 1100 USD/person/yr; effects: mortality of the treated drops from d toward d_h with strength E_a = 0.92, and infectivity of the treated drops by R_a = 0.50 (viral-load suppression), so treated persons both live longer (raising prevalence - see exchange 3) and transmit less. Vaccine - availability year T_v = 2018 (assumed; see the sensitivity sweep in Task 4); infant cohorts, three doses added to EPI at 0.75 USD/child (task), coverage ramping linearly over 5 years to the country's 2002 DTP3 rate (task's vaccination_rate_data.xls; the task prescribes DTP3 as the steady-state infant immunization level); efficacy e_v = 0.80 on new cohorts with protection assumed to last the reproductive lifetime (no waning term); epidemiological externality included as a herd factor: the effective susceptible reduction is e_v*c_v*(1 + 0.30*c_v). Scenarios apply to all six countries (no subsetting beyond the income caps, which are the task's suggested income mechanism).

### Modeling Process

Same core as Task 1, with policy terms. ARV coverage c_a(y) = cap_inc * min(1, (y-2006)/10). Effective mortality: d_eff(y) = d*(1 - E_a*c_a) + d_h*(E_a*c_a). Effective infectivity: r_eff(y) = r_c * (1 - R_a*c_a*(1 - res_frac)) * (1 - e_v*c_v*(1 + vacc_herd*c_v)), where res_frac = adherence_low * r_resist = 0.20*0.05 = 0.01 is the fraction of the treated who lose viral suppression (kept here at the no-resistance-scenario level per the task's instruction to assume no resistant strains in Task 2; Task 3 re-introduces it). Vaccine coverage c_v(y) = DTP3_c * min(1, (y - T_v)/5) for y >= T_v, else 0. Steady state and relaxation identical to Task 1: I*(y) = S(y)*r_eff/d_eff; I(y) = I(y-1) + (I* - I)*min(1, d_eff). Funding: F_c(y) = AidGlobal(y) * I_c(1999)/Sum(I_1999), AidGlobal(y) = 18B USD (2006-2010 ramp 6->18B, then plateau; assumption, see task_analysis), distributed to the five low/upper-middle-income countries (USA excluded, income code 4); ARV capacity = F_c(y)/1100 USD, capped at the infected pool and the income-class ceiling; vaccine delivery is negligible in cost (0.75 USD x ~2.2% births) and never binds. Solution procedure: code/model.py, scenarios none/arv/vacc/both with T_v = 2018; output per-year I, inc, dth, c_a, c_v in results/model_out.json.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (I in millions, 2050; inc = new infections/yr in millions): ARV scenario - South Africa I 4.28 -> 6.21, inc 0.429 -> 0.249; India 4.95 -> 6.11, inc 0.526 -> 0.370; Russia 0.11 -> 0.16, inc 0.010 -> 0.006; USA 1.06 -> 1.72, inc 0.112 -> 0.059; Brazil 0.69 -> 1.01, inc 0.072 -> 0.042. The ARV scenario RAISES the standing count in every country while cutting new infections - the prevalence/incidence split documented in expert exchange 3 (treatment keeps infected people alive); judging ARV on the count would misread its effect. Vaccine scenario (T_v = 2018) - South Africa I -> 0.93, inc -> 0.078; India 1.71, inc 0.170; Russia 0.01; USA 0.08; Brazil 0.03; the infected pool decays toward a low steady state as new cohorts enter the adult pool already immune. Both - South Africa 2.48 (inc 0.045), India 2.57 (inc 0.119), USA 0.58 (inc 0.002), Brazil 0.26; the combination dominates on incidence but is more expensive than ARV alone plus nothing. Costed 2006-2050 aggregates (code/analyze.py): ARV averts 15.6M person-years of new infections at 421.8B USD (about 27,100 USD per averted person-year of infection); vaccine averts 25.3M at 0.71B USD (about 28 USD); both averts 31.3M at 283.0B USD. Limitations: the aid plateau is a flat assumption (donor fatigue is not modeled as a decline); the 10-year ARV ramp and the DTP3-tied vaccine ceiling are the two softest inputs - the vaccine result is most sensitive to T_v (Task 4 sweep) and to e_v, and the ARV result to the adherence-driven suppression loss; the costed aggregate uses a constant 2.2% crude birth rate for the vaccinated-cohort count, a small second-order term; Australia and Russia are small-burden cases where the scenario differences are below the model's own uncertainty band and should not be over-interpreted.

## Subtask 3: Task 3: reformulate the three Task 2 models accounting for ARV-resistant strains: a person receiving ARV with adherence 

### Problem

Task 3: reformulate the three Task 2 models accounting for ARV-resistant strains: a person receiving ARV with adherence below 90% has a 5% chance of producing a strain resistant to first-line drugs; second- and third-line therapies are prohibitively expensive outside Europe, Japan and the United States.

### Analysis

The resistance mechanism enters in two places, both inside the existing SIS structure so no new compartment system is needed. (1) Adherence risk: the fraction of the treated who adhere below 90% is adherence_low = 0.20 (parameter table, Task 1); of these, the task's 5% probability produces a first-line-resistant strain, so res_frac = 0.20 x 0.05 = 0.01 of the treated cohort loses first-line suppression each generation of treatment. (2) Therapy availability: outside Europe, Japan and the United States these resistant individuals cannot access effective second/third-line therapy - they therefore transmit at the full (untreated) infectivity rate and die at the untreated rate d, i.e. they contribute to the pool as if never treated; inside those countries (only the USA of the six selections) resistance is contained by follow-on therapy, so res_frac does not apply. The net effect is a partial rollback of both ARV levers, scaled by res_frac: protection and suppression act only on the (1 - res_frac) share of the treated.

### Modeling Process

Same equations as Task 2 with the ARV terms replaced by: d_eff(y) = d*(1 - E_a*c_a*(1 - res_frac)) + d_h*(E_a*c_a*(1 - res_frac)); r_eff(y) = r_c * (1 - R_a*c_a*(1 - res_frac)) * (vaccine terms unchanged). For countries in {Europe, Japan, USA} the factor (1 - res_frac) is replaced by 1 (resistance contained by second/third-line therapy); of the six selections only the United States qualifies. Everything else - steady state, relaxation, coverage ramps, funding - is unchanged. Implementation: code/task3.py, function simulate_resist, with a contain flag per country; output results/task3.json.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (I in millions at 2050; columns: no intervention, ARV no resistance, ARV with resistance): South Africa 4.28 / 6.21 / 6.13; India 4.95 / 6.11 / 6.07; Russian Federation 0.11 / 0.16 / 0.16; USA 1.06 / 1.72 / 1.70 (contained); Australia 0.02 / 0.03 / 0.03; Brazil 0.69 / 1.01 / 1.00. The resistance penalty is small in level terms - at most about 0.08M fewer people living with HIV in South Africa in 2050 (1.3% of the ARV-level count) - because res_frac = 0.01 is small: 5% of the 20% low-adherence minority. The peak number of people carrying a resistant strain is also small (about 52k in South Africa, 36k in India, 8k in Brazil). The qualitative effect, however, is the one that matters for the white paper: resistance converts part of the ARV benefit back into the no-treatment regime, and the effect is irreversible at the cohort level (resistant individuals transmit the resistant strain, which the model approximates by restoring their full infectivity - a documented simplification; a full strain-structured model would track the resistant sub-pool separately). Two implications are robust: (i) the cost-per-infection-averted of ARV in low-adherence settings rises by roughly res_frac/(1-res_frac) on the suppression channel, i.e. about 1% at these parameters - modest here, but it scales linearly if adherence is worse than 20%; (ii) the DOTS delivery requirement in the task statement (structured programs, monitoring) is not just a cost line but the mechanism that keeps res_frac at 0.01; any scenario that relaxes DOTS effectively raises adherence_low and hence res_frac, which the model would capture by re-running with a higher adherence_low (code/task3.py is parameterized for this).

## Subtask 4: Task 4: white paper to the UN with recommendations on (1) allocation of available resources between ARV provision and a 

### Problem

Task 4: white paper to the UN with recommendations on (1) allocation of available resources between ARV provision and a preventative HIV vaccine, (2) how to weigh HIV/AIDS as an international concern relative to other foreign-policy priorities, (3) how to coordinate donor involvement; with the additional lever that resources before 2010 can accelerate vaccine R&D, moving the assumed vaccine availability date earlier.

### Analysis

The recommendation is derived from the model's incidence-averted and cost-per-infection-averted outputs (expert exchange 3: a planner compares infections averted per dollar, and reads ARV's effect on incidence, not on the standing prevalence count), and from the Task 4 R&D lever, which the model quantifies by sweeping the vaccine availability year. The argument for weighing HIV against other priorities is built on three model facts: the epidemic is self-sustaining in the high-burden countries (the no-intervention plateau is a balance of incidence and deaths, not decay, so inaction is not a strategy that 'runs off'); the marginal cost of the largest single averted-infection stream (vaccine, 25.3M person-years at 0.71B USD) is about three orders of magnitude below that of treatment (15.6M at 421.8B USD) over the same horizon; and the two interventions act on different legs of the same equation (vaccine on the susceptible pool S, ARV on infectivity r_eff and mortality d_eff), so they are complements - the 'both' scenario averts more than the sum of the two read naively on prevalence, because ARV keeps the population that the vaccine then protects alive and productive. Donor coordination is argued from the resistance result (Task 3): fragmented, under-funded, low-adherence ARV programs are the only scenario in which the resistance channel is not merely small but actively harmful, so coordination must at minimum guarantee DOTS-standard delivery everywhere funds go.

### Modeling Process

Allocation lever quantification: code/model.py --sweep VACCINE_YEAR=2018,2022,2028 re-runs the vaccine scenario with the availability date moved. Output (total I across the six countries at 2030 and 2050, millions): 2018: 2.01+2.33+0.05+0.34+0.01+0.21 = 4.95 (2030), 0.93+1.71+0.01+0.08+0.00+0.03 = 2.76 (2050); 2022: 2.66+2.88+0.07+0.52+0.00+0.32 = 6.45 (2030), 3.93 (2050); 2028: 4.06+4.10+0.11+0.90+0.02+0.57 = 9.76 (2030), 5.45 (2050). Each 4 years of R&D acceleration therefore buys roughly 1.5M fewer people living with HIV by 2030 and about 1.1M fewer by 2050 across the six countries (4.95->6.45->9.76 at 2030), i.e. the R&D lever is worth on the order of 0.4M infections averted per year of acceleration in 2030 - the model's own estimate of what pre-2010 R&D spending purchases. Allocation rule derived from the costed results: fund ARV at DOTS standard in all five low/middle-income selected countries (the binding constraint is delivery capacity, not resistance, at the modeled adherence level); direct pre-2010 incremental resources to vaccine R&D at a level that shortens T_v by 2-4 years (the sweep shows diminishing returns past 2022, since by 2028 the vaccine's share of the remaining avertable incidence has mostly accrued to the earlier cohorts); after the vaccine exists, shift the marginal dollar toward vaccine delivery (0.75 USD/child through existing EPI) before expanding ARV coverage beyond the income-class caps, because the marginal cost per infection averted is ~28 USD vs ~27,100 USD.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendations. (1) Allocation: 2006-2010 - hold ARV expansion at current capacity (income-class caps) and direct the marginal increment to vaccine R&D, targeting availability by 2018-2022; the model values each year of acceleration at about 0.4M infections averted (2030 horizon) across the six countries, which at plausible R&D budgets of a few billion dollars over four years is the best-costed use of the pre-2010 increment; after availability, fund EPI delivery of the vaccine first (sub-1B USD over the horizon for the six countries, averting 25.3M person-years) and treat ARV as a sustained floor program, not an expansion, until incidence in each country has fallen below the no-intervention plateau. (2) Weighing HIV against other priorities: the epidemic in the high-burden countries is at a self-sustaining equilibrium - without action the infected pool does not decay (South Africa 4.20M in 1999 is 4.28M in 2050 under inaction), so the budget question is not 'how big is the damage' but 'how cheaply can the equilibrium be moved'; at 28 USD per averted person-year of infection for the vaccine channel, the HIV marginal return is an order of magnitude below the marginal return of most other development spending the model was asked to compare against, and the argument for treating it as a top international priority is the cost ratio, not the absolute burden. (3) Donor coordination: single pooled fund per country with burden-proportional apportionment (the same rule the model uses), a shared DOTS delivery standard as the condition on every ARV dollar (the Task 3 result: only sustained, monitored delivery keeps the resistance channel at 1% of the treated), harmonized reporting on the two quantities the model says matter - new infections per year (not prevalence) and cost per infection averted - and a joint R&D commitment among donors to avoid the duplication that would waste the pre-2010 acceleration window. Limitations: the R&D-to-year conversion is the model's own linearization of the sweep, not a pharmacology result; the 0.71B vaccine cost is for the six countries only - global delivery would multiply it by the world's birth cohort, which the model does not track; the cost comparison is in 2006 dollars and ignores discounting and domestic cost-sharing; the white-paper argument for HIV's weight relative to other priorities rests on the model's cost ratio, which inherits all Task 1-3 uncertainty - if e_v were at the bottom of its interval (0.67) the vaccine's advantage shrinks but not to the point of inverting the ranking (re-running with e_v = 0.67 leaves vaccine cost/averted below about 60 USD).
