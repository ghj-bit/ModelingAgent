## Subtask 1: Requirement 1. Using Names.csv (83 nodes), Messages.csv (400 links, up to 3 topic codes each) and Topics.csv (15 topics)

### Problem

Requirement 1. Using Names.csv (83 nodes), Messages.csv (400 links, up to 3 topic codes each) and Topics.csv (15 topics), build a model and algorithm that prioritizes all 83 nodes by likelihood of being part of the conspiracy, given the 7 known conspirators (Jean, Alex, Elsie, Paul, Ulf, Yao, Harvey), the 8 known non-conspirators (Darlene, Tran, Jia, Ellin, Gard, Chris, Paige, Este) and the 3 analyst-deemed suspicious topics (7, 11, 13). Explain the model and metrics. Also assess whether the senior managers Jerome, Delores and Gretchen are involved.

### Analysis

ASSUMPTIONS. (1) The 400 recorded messages are the complete set recovered by ICM; they form a non-random, non-representative slice of total office traffic (biased toward interceptable and frequent-communicator traffic), so scores are likelihoods conditional on the observed traffic only. (2) All traffic, including conspiratorial meetings, flows through the same ordinary message channel; conspiratorial content is distinguished only by topic code, not by medium (confirmed with the expert). (3) A message's evidential content for its two endpoints is exactly its topic codes; a node is more likely a conspirator if it exchanges more messages carrying suspicious topics. (4) Topic suspiciousness is additive and non-negative. (5) The 15 anchor labels are correct as given. DATA CLEANING. 135 trailing blank rows of Messages.csv were dropped; one out-of-range third-topic code (18, not in Topics.csv) on message 14->25 was filtered; 47 two-topic and 11 three-topic messages carry the extra codes as listed; no records were imputed. NAMES.csv repeats four names (Elsie, Beth, Neal, Jerome, Gretchen each appear twice); the problem's named people map to the unique node ids listed in the parameter table. MODEL AND WHY. A topic-weighted network evidence score, fit by maximum likelihood on the anchor labels, was chosen because (a) the data are a static attributed graph (no timestamps), so centrality+attribute fusion is the natural form; (b) the anchors give direct supervision that calibrates the topic weights instead of leaving them to ad hoc judgment; (c) the form is convex-concave and solvable exactly, reproducible and auditable for the DA. The suspicious topics 7, 11, 13 carry a fixed floor weight so the analysts' judgment can never be overridden by the fit; the remaining topic weights and a volume penalty are free parameters estimated from the anchors. Pure degree/centrality was rejected as the main signal because the anchors are not the highest-degree nodes (e.g. Ulf has degree 10, Harvey degree 11, while non-conspirator Paige has degree 22), so connectivity alone misleads; topic-exposure evidence dominates in this dataset.

### Modeling Process

VARIABLES. Node set V (|V|=83), message set M (|M|=400); message e=(a,b,T_e) with topic set T_e subset of {1..15}. For node u: ev(u,t) = number of messages incident on u carrying topic t; deg(u) = number of messages incident on u; susc(u) = sum of ev(u,t) over suspicious topics t in {7,11,13}. SCORE. s_u = sum_t w_t * ev(u,t) - beta * deg(u), with w_t >= 0, w_7 = w_11 = w_13 = lam_sus = 0.30 fixed (floor), and 0 <= beta <= 2. CALIBRATION (anchor fit). Let C = {18,21,7,37,43,54,67,49} (the 7 known conspirators; Elsie is two nodes, 7 and 37) and N = {48,64,65,68,74,0,2,78} (the 8 known non-conspirators). The free parameters (w_t for non-suspicious t, beta) minimize   -[ sum_{u in C} log sigma(s_u) + sum_{u in N} log(1 - sigma(s_u)) ]   (Bernoulli log-likelihood)   + gamma * sum_{t not suspicious} w_t                                (L1 sparsity)   + rho * sum_{c in C, n in N} max(0, s_n - s_c)^2                     (soft anchor-ordering hinge, rho = 10) subject to 0 <= w_t <= 4. Solved with L-BFGS-B (scipy) with an analytic gradient. gamma is swept over {0, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 3.2}; the selected gamma minimizes the total anchor violation sum max(0, s_n - s_c). The anchors are not perfectly separable by topic content alone (two known conspirators barely touch the suspicious topics), so the hinge is soft and the residual overlap is reported honestly: at the selected gamma the minimum anchor margin is min_C s - max_N s = -0.101. RANKING. Nodes are ranked by descending s_u; the 15 anchors are marked but excluded from the actionable tiers per the expert: Tier 1 (surveillance/interview) = top 5 candidates; Tier 2 (watch) = remaining candidates with score >= 0.5 * (top candidate score); Tier 3 = cleared tail. LEADERS. On the suspicious-topic subgraph (messages with any of topics 7,11,13), leader candidates are the Tier-1 nodes with the largest in-degree (communication hubs conspirators route through). PARAMETER TABLE (calibrated inputs): lam_sus = 0.30, interval [0.1, 1.0], source: task's analyst-deemed suspicious topics 7,11,13 (floor set so one exposure is a meaningful but non-decisive unit); gamma = 0.8, interval [0, 3.2], source: anchor-violation sweep in code/model.py; rho = 10.0, fixed hinge constant, source: design choice, results insensitive over one order of magnitude; W_BOX = 4.0, B_BOX = 2.0, design bounds; Tier-2 band = 0.5 * top candidate score, source: expert Exchange 3 (act on the top handful separated by a visible gap). The only data-derived inputs are the topic-exposure counts ev(u,t), degrees and anchor labels from the task's own dataset.

### Outcome Analysis

The full 83-node ranking is in results/req1.json. LEADERS. Neal(17) is the strongest leader candidate: highest suspicious-subgraph in-degree (5) among Tier 1, i.e. the hub other suspicious traffic is routed to. Gretchen(4) (in-degree 4) and Delores(10) (in-degree 3) are secondary. MANAGERS. Delores(10) ranks 7th overall (2.28, Tier 1) and Gretchen(4) ranks 8th (1.82, Tier 1) - both senior managers sit in the surveillance tier, so there is substantial model-based reason to investigate their involvement. The two Jerome records (16: 1.61, Tier 2; 34: 1.31, Tier 2) are elevated watch-list. LIMITATIONS AND BIASES. (1) Non-representative sample: low scores do not clear a node that stayed out of the recovered traffic. (2) Single-channel assumption: a node that avoids monitored topics hides. (3) Duplicated names: the two records per duplicated name are treated as distinct nodes; if they are the same person the manager signal doubles. (4) Residual anchor overlap (margin -0.10) means the ordering of mid-list nodes has real uncertainty; tiers, not ranks, are the decision-relevant output. (5) No timestamps, so recency/escalation effects cannot be modeled.

### Problem

Requirement 3. Explain how semantic network analysis and text analysis of the original message content and context (if obtainable) would let the team build better models and categorizations of the office personnel. State whether any of these capabilities were used on the topic descriptions in Topics.csv to enhance the model.

### Analysis

Scope: this task is a methodology explanation plus a statement of what was actually used. The base model only had topic codes (no transcripts), so the explanation is forward-looking, but a limited use of the semantic content of Topics.csv was available and is reported.

### Modeling Process

WHAT SEMANTIC / TEXT ANALYSIS WOULD ADD, CONCRETELY. (1) Entity-mention graphs: parse every message for person, place, account, document and amount mentions; a co-mention network (who references the same secret entity) is far sharper evidence of a shared covert project than topic codes alone - in this case the private meeting at Paul's house, the offline windows for Paige/Ellin/Chris, and the credit-card/accounting flaws are exactly the entities that semantic parsing would link. (2) Temporal and burst analysis: message timestamps allow detection of coordinated silence (no traffic before the meeting) and synchronized activity, which is invisible in the static topic model used here. (3) Style and code detection: topics 2, 7 and 12 note Spanish words 'possibly as codes'; n-gram and cross-lingual anomaly detection on the raw text would find the code words, giving a direct per-message suspiciousness feature instead of the floor weight used here. (4) Topic refinement: clustering the raw text (e.g. BERTopic/LDA) would test whether the 15 hand-coded topics are stable or whether a covert sub-thread hides inside an innocuous topic; conversely it could merge over-split topics. (5) Sentiment and politeness: the EZ case shows conspirators managing cover stories (George telling Harry he is 'just working late'); discourse analysis of such deflection patterns is a classic deception cue. (6) Semantic networks of the message content: linking concepts (credit card -> accounting flaw -> offline window -> embezzlement) gives a causal story graph whose nodes can be matched to personnel, turning the model from 'who talks about suspicious things' into 'who contributes to the plan'. SCALING to thousands of people / millions of words follows the same pipeline: streaming entity extraction, incremental graph update, and the same anchor-calibrated scoring - the topic-weight score above is exactly the low-dimensional case of that pipeline. DID WE USE IT? Partially yes: the semantic content of the topic descriptions in Topics.csv was read and used in two ways. (a) It justified the analyst floor on topics 7, 11, 13 and informed the prior that topics mentioning covert coordination (private meeting, system flaws, offline windows) are the carriers of conspiracy. (b) It revealed cross-references the codes alone do not show - Chris and Paige appear in the descriptions of topics 5, 13 and 15 (denied access, offline windows, security-training policy), and Darlene/Jean in topic 9, Alex/Elsie/Paige in topic 4 - these cross-references were used to sanity-check the fitted scores and explain, e.g., why Chris is a coherent conspirator candidate once topic 1 is added. No automated NLP (no embeddings, no parsing of transcripts) was applied, because only the coded summaries are available, not the message bodies; the description above is what would change if the original traffic were obtained.

### Outcome Analysis

Outcome: the model used here is the code-only limit of the full semantic-pipeline, and its two description-derived inputs (the suspicious-topic floor and the named-person cross-references) are exactly the features a semantic pass would produce automatically at scale. Expected benefit of the full pipeline: higher precision on mid-list nodes (the current model's weakest region), detection of covert sub-threads inside innocuous topics, and direct code-word evidence that would replace the fixed floor weight with a data-driven per-message suspiciousness score. Limitation of what was done: reading 15 summaries is not a substitute for corpus analysis; the named-person cross-references are small-sample and were used for validation, not as primary evidence, to avoid double-counting the anchors.

### Problem

Requirement 4. Explain the network modeling techniques used, why, and how they identify, prioritize and categorize similar nodes in any network database (not just crime cases), including whether the method transfers to a biological network with node-level infection probabilities and some known infected nodes.

### Analysis

Scope: generalization of the methodology. The deliverable is the transferable recipe plus a worked argument for the biological case.

### Modeling Process

NETWORK MODELING TECHNIQUES USED AND WHY. (1) Attributed-graph evidence scoring: each edge carries categorical attributes (topic codes); a node's score is an additive function of the attributes of its incident edges. This is the generic 'label propagation from attributed neighbors' principle: information flows along edges, attributes weight the flow. It was chosen over pure centrality because in this data connectivity alone is uninformative (the anchors are not the hubs), and over community detection because the task is per-node likelihood, not partitioning. (2) Anchor-calibrated weights: the attribute weights are estimated by maximum likelihood on the known labels (Bernoulli model with L1 sparsity and a soft ordering hinge). This is the same device as logistic regression / linear-chain CRF potentials, applied to graph attributes; it converts expert labels into calibrated, auditable feature weights. (3) Hinge/penalty design: the squared soft hinge on anchor mis-ordering makes the fit robust to imperfect labels (real cases always contain some), and the L1 term enforces parsimony so only genuinely informative attributes carry weight. (4) Subgraph structural cue for leadership: on the evidence subgraph, in-degree (who the traffic is routed to) identifies coordination hubs - the standard betweenness/degree heuristic for 'leader' in communication networks. (5) Tiered decision rule: converting a continuous score into act/watch/clear tiers via a data-driven gap rule, per the expert, instead of a fixed cutoff. GENERALIZATION TO ANY NETWORK DATABASE. The recipe is: (i) model the network as an attributed graph; (ii) specify which attributes are evidence for the state of interest and let the known labels calibrate their weights (max-likelihood + sparsity + soft anchor hinge); (iii) score nodes additively; (iv) tier the scores with a gap rule; (v) use a structural cue on the evidence subgraph for 'important node' (hub/leader). It applies unchanged wherever you have: a graph, categorical or numeric edge/node attributes, and a handful of verified labels - e.g. fraud rings, rumor/disinformation spreading, equipment fault propagation, or the biological case. BIOLOGICAL NETWORK CASE. Yes, the method transfers directly. Map: cells = nodes; interactions (signaling, contact) = edges; the 'attributes' are the node-level image/chemical measurements (each measurement channel is an attribute like a topic); known infected cells = positive anchors, known healthy cells = negative anchors. The same calibration then estimates which measurement channels are informatively different between infected and healthy cells (the L1 term selects them automatically - exactly like it selected topics 3,4,6,8 here), and scores every cell by its own measurements plus the infected-ness of its neighbors, which is the biologically correct form because infection status is correlated with neighbor status (spatial/interaction spread). The tier rule gives the lab a treat/watch/healthy list, and the in-degree-on-evidence-subgraph cue flags cells that look like spread sources (many neighbors showing the infection signature). Two adaptations are needed: (a) biological attributes are continuous, so the per-attribute weight becomes a coefficient on the (normalized) measurement, i.e. the model becomes a regularized logistic regression with a graph-message term - the same convex form; (b) if infection probability is already available per node (as stated in the question), it can enter as a prior feature or as the initialization of the score, and the anchor fit then calibrates the rest. Limitations carry over identically: the sample of observed interactions must be reasonably complete, labels must be reliable, and cells that avoid the measured channels hide - the same caveats as in the crime case.

### Outcome Analysis

Outcome: the submission's method is presented as a transferable, label-calibrated, attributed-graph scoring pipeline with a tiered decision rule and a subgraph leader cue; the biological-network case is confirmed as a direct instance with two stated adaptations (continuous attributes -> regularized logistic form; available infection probabilities -> prior/feature). The main risk in transfer is the same as in the crime case - non-representative sampling and label noise - which the soft-anchor hinge and the tiered (rather than point-estimate) output are specifically designed to absorb.
