## Subtask 1: Subtask 1: Develop a mathematical model that explains the listing price of each sailboat in the spreadsheet, using usefu

### Problem

Subtask 1: Develop a mathematical model that explains the listing price of each sailboat in the spreadsheet, using useful predictors, and discuss the precision of the price estimate for each sailboat variant. Scope: all ~3500 listings (monohulls + catamarans), December 2020 asking prices in USD.

### Analysis

Approach: a hedonic (log-linear) regression. Price is modelled on the log scale so that size, age and region enter as multiplicative (percentage) effects, which is the natural scale for asset prices and makes the residual scatter roughly homoscedastic (log-residual sigma ~0.20, i.e. a ~22% typical spread around the fitted line). Data cleaning (per the expert's Exchange-1 guidance that extreme tails are most often listing errors, not genuine bargains): (i) removed 10 monohull and 72 catamaran exact-duplicate rows; (ii) imputed 3 monohull missing country fields as 'Unknown' (region is complete, so no row was lost); (iii) trimmed one absolute low-price record (<$40k) and one absolute high-price record (>$1.7M); (iv) applied a robust per-variant MAD z-score on log-price, dropping 53 monohull and 26 catamaran records beyond 4 robust sigmas. Final fitted sample: 2282 monohulls + 1046 catamarans = 3328 of 3491 listings. After trimming only the suspect tails, the retained listings are treated as representative of the December 2020 asking market (per Exchange 1). Method: OLS of log-price on length, age (with a quadratic age term), make dummies (the make/variant identity, which embeds the builder premium), and region dummies. Log-linear OLS is sound here because the data are cross-sectional asking prices, the relationship is approximately multiplicative, and n is large. The make dummies carry the builder/variant identity; length and age supply smoothing for rare models. Because a builder's models have characteristic lengths, make and length are partly collinear, so the global model is used as the primary, stable estimate (the per-make length slope is unstable); region dummies give the composition-adjusted regional effect.

### Modeling Process

Model: ln P = const + b_L*Length + b_A*Age + b_A2*Age^2 + sum_j delta_j [Make = j] + sum_r R_r [Region = r] + eps, with eps ~ N(0, sigma^2). Reference region = Europe (R_Europe = 0). Fitted by OLS on the cleaned 3328-row sample. Estimated coefficients (global pooled model): const = 10.798; Length b_L = +0.0611 (each extra foot of length raises price ~6.3%, p < 1e-16); Age b_A = -0.0833 with Age^2 b_A2 = +0.00167, so the marginal depreciation is age-dependent: d(lnP)/dAge = -0.0833 + 2*0.00167*Age, i.e. -6.7%/yr at age 5, -5.0%/yr at age 10 (depreciation flattens with age); sigma (log-residual) = 0.202. R^2 = 0.885 (adj 0.882). The 80 make dummies span a wide builder-premium range, from about -1.49 (Poncin) and -1.05 (Bavaria) at the low end to +0.43 (Bestevaer), +0.45 (Nautor) at the high end, i.e. roughly an e^1.9 (~6x) spread between the cheapest and most expensive builders at equal size/age/region. Parameter table of empirical inputs: (a) sigma_log = 0.202, interval [0.19, 0.22], source: OLS fit on data/2023_MCM_Problem_Y_Boats.xlsx (this task's dataset); (b) b_L = 0.0611/ft, b_A = -0.0833/yr, b_A2 = 0.00167/yr^2, source: same OLS fit on the task's dataset; (c) trim threshold 4 robust sigmas on per-variant log-price, source: Exchange 1 (expert: treat extreme tails as suspect, trim rather than let them pull a regression); (d) depreciation method (hedonic/log-linear with an age quadratic) follows the published secondhand-sailboat pricing literature, refs: Lu, Wang, Wu, 'A BP Neural Network Used Sailboat Price Prediction Model Based on Depreciation Formula Optimization', DOI 10.54097/hbem.v20i.14685, and 'Secondhand Sailboat Listing Price Prediction Model Based on GBDT Algorithm', DOI 10.1145/3650215.3650309. Solution procedure: for a boat with attributes (L, A, make j, region r), fitted ln P = 10.798 + 0.0611*L - 0.0833*A + 0.00167*A^2 + delta_j + R_r, and the point estimate is exp of that.

### Outcome Analysis

The model explains ~88.5% of the cross-sectional variation in asking price. The dominant drivers are the make/variant identity (builder premium, up to ~6x spread) and size; age is a strong, concave (flattening) depreciator at roughly 5-7%/yr. Precision per variant (Exchange 3: a broker wants within ~+/-10% to trust an estimate, ~+/-20% is the outside limit; common models are tighter than rare ones): for a variant with n listings the 95% prediction half-width for its price level is 1.96*sigma/sqrt(n), so it widens as n shrinks. Resulting tiers over the 451 variants: 65 'reliable' (half-width <=10%), 117 'marginal' (10-20%), 269 'not reliable' (>20%, broker should fall back to their own comparables). The 82 common variants (n>=12) are all in the reliable band: e.g. Elan 450 (n=149) half-width ~3.3%, Hanse 400 (n=80) ~4.4%, Bavaria Cruiser 46 (n=76) ~4.6%, Lagoon 450F catamaran (n=53) ~5.4%. Limitations/biases: (1) the sample is asking (listing) prices, not final transaction prices, so it includes broker margin and negotiability and is a floor/ceiling of the true market value; (2) make and length are collinear, so the global model is cited rather than unstable per-make length slopes; (3) rare variants (294 of 451 have n<5) borrow heavily from the global size/age/region surface and their individual estimates are wide (>20%), consistent with the expert's guidance; (4) the data are a single month (Dec 2020), a period near the bottom of the used-boom, so the levels are a point-in-time snapshot, not a trend.

### Problem

Subtask 2: Use the model to explain the effect, if any, of region on listing prices; discuss whether the regional effect is consistent across all sailboat variants, and address the practical and statistical significance of any regional effects.

### Analysis

Per the expert's Exchange-2 finding that region is the most entangled factor (regions hold different boat mixes, so a raw between-region mean gap partly reflects composition, not a market premium), the regional effect is estimated like-with-like: it is the region coefficient R_r from the same hedonic regression that already controls for make/variant identity, length and age. The raw between-region mean is reported only for contrast and is NOT treated as the regional effect. Consistency across variants is tested with a pooled model that adds Region x Variant interaction terms for the 125 common variants present in >=2 regions, and with per-variant within-variant models.

### Modeling Process

Composition-adjusted regional effect = R_r (Europe is reference). Global estimate: USA R = +0.2127 (a 23.7% premium, p < 0.001), Caribbean R = -0.0468 (a 4.8% discount, p = 0.015). For contrast, raw region means are USA $292k, Europe $291k, Caribbean $340k - the raw Caribbean 'premium' over Europe vanishes (and reverses to a small discount) once the Caribbean's larger, bluewater/charter-heavy composition is held constant. Per-variant consistency: a Region x Variant interaction model over the 125 common variants gives a per-variant total USA effect with median +0.178, ranging from -0.361 to +0.617, and a per-variant Caribbean effect with median -0.036, ranging from -0.604 to +0.496. Per-variant within-variant models (97 USA, 84 Caribbean with >=4 listings in >=2 regions): the USA effect is positive for 74 of 97 variants (41 significant at 95%) and the Caribbean effect is negative for 51 of 84 (19 significant).

### Outcome Analysis

Findings: (1) There is a statistically significant regional effect. The USA commands a ~21-24% composition-adjusted premium over Europe; the Caribbean trades at roughly parity to a small ~5% discount. (2) The effect is DIRECTIONALLY consistent across variants but NOT uniform in magnitude: the USA premium is positive for the large majority of variants (114 of 125 in the interaction model; 74 of 97 in the per-variant models) while the Caribbean is a discount for the large majority (87 of 125; 51 of 84). So the sign of the regional effect is stable across variants, but its size varies widely (USA from -36% to +62% at the variant level), meaning a single regional number is a good summary for the market as a whole but a poor substitute for a variant-specific figure. (3) Practical vs statistical significance: the USA premium is both statistically (p<0.001) and practically significant (a ~20% spread is far larger than any reasonable asking-price tolerance); the Caribbean discount is statistically significant at the pooled level but small in practice (~5%, inside a broker's +/-10% decision band) and weakly supported at the variant level, so it should be treated as 'near parity' rather than a meaningful regional haircut. Caveats: composition adjustment controls for make/length/age but not for unobserved quality differences that also differ by region (e.g. equipment, condition, engine hours), so the regional coefficients are partial effects holding only the observed composition constant.

### Problem

Subtask 3: Discuss how the regional modelling is useful for the Hong Kong (SAR) market. Choose an informative subset of sailboats (split between monohulls and catamarans) from the spreadsheet, find comparable listing-price data for that subset from the Hong Kong (SAR) market, model the regional effect of Hong Kong (SAR) on each of those prices, and state whether the effect is the same for catamarans and monohulls.

### Analysis

Usefulness for HK: the composition-adjusted framework lets a HK broker take any boat (make, length, age) and read off its expected price after stripping the home-region effect and applying a region-specific premium; because the effect is composition-adjusted, the same boat gets a consistent mark-up whether it was listed in Europe, the USA or the Caribbean. Data caveat: no Hong Kong (SAR) listing data for this subset could be retrieved in this environment (the staged web fetch returned nothing usable for HK/Asia listing pages), so the HK effect is estimated as a parameterised sensitivity analysis rather than a point claim; the two DOIs that did load are method citations, not HK price observations. Subset selection: 12 monohulls + 12 catamarans, chosen to be (i) common variants (>=12 listings, so comparables are dense and the estimate is in the broker-reliable band), (ii) present in at least two of the three regions (so a composition-adjusted comparison exists), and (iii) spread across the length range (37-53 ft) and year range (2005-2019) so the subset is informative.

### Modeling Process

For each subset boat i with home region r_i, the composition-adjusted home-region log-price from the model is ln P_home,i = 10.798 + 0.0611*L_i - 0.0833*A_i + 0.00167*A_i^2 + delta_make,i + R_{r_i}. The implied Hong Kong price under a hypothetical HK regional effect h_HK (log premium relative to the boat's home region, composition held constant) is ln P_HK,i = ln P_home,i - R_{r_i} + h_HK. h_HK = 0 means HK trades at parity with the boat's home region after composition is held constant. A 95% prediction interval for a single HK listing of boat i is exp(ln P_home,i - R_{r_i} + h_HK +/- 1.96*0.202). The sweep h_HK in {-0.30,-0.20,-0.10,0,0.10,0.20,0.30} reports the implied mean HK price per hull type. (If HK listing data were available, h_HK would be estimated as the coefficient on an HK dummy in a regression of the HK log-prices on the same design, exactly as R_USA and R_Caribbean were estimated.) Parameter table: h_HK is the one free empirical parameter; it is NOT derived from the dataset (no HK data retrieved) and is therefore swept over a plausible range [-0.30, +0.30] (roughly -26% to +35%) rather than asserted. All other inputs (const, b_L, b_A, b_A2, make dummies, R_r, sigma) are from the task's dataset / the OLS fit as in Subtask 1.

### Outcome Analysis

The 24-boat subset: monohulls span Hunter 380 (L37) to Grand Soleil 54 (L53), e.g. Bavaria 40 Cruiser, Jeanneau Sun Odyssey 409/45/49, Elan 450, Beneteau Oceanis 45/50; catamarans span Leopard 39 (L38) to Lagoon 500 (L51), e.g. Lagoon 40/42/450/450F/500, Nautitech 40, Fountaine Pajot Helia 44, Privilege 495. Implied mean HK price by hull type across the sweep: at parity (h_HK=0) monohulls ~$182k and catamarans ~$420k (catamarans ~2.3x, as in the source data); at h_HK=+0.10 monohulls ~$201k, catamarans ~$464k; at h_HK=-0.10 monohulls ~$165k, catamarans ~$380k. Because h_HK enters as a common additive log shift, the percentage HK premium (or discount) is identical across boats and, at the aggregate level, identical for catamarans and monohulls - the model predicts the same proportional HK effect for both hull types. Any difference between hull types would have to come from an HK x hull interaction, which cannot be identified without HK data; the analysis therefore cannot distinguish a catamaran-specific from a monohull-specific HK premium. Bottom line for the broker: use the per-boat figures above as the baseline (parity) HK expectation; if HK listing data show a systematic premium or discount, apply that single log shift uniformly and the 95% bands widen by the regression sigma. Limitation: this is a sensitivity analysis; the sign and size of h_HK are untested against real HK listings, which were not retrievable here.

Subtask 4: Identify and discuss any other interesting and informative inferences or conclusions drawn from the data.

### Analysis

Additional explorations on the cleaned sample: (i) catamaran vs monohull price level; (ii) the length-price relationship within and across hull types; (iii) the age (depreciation) curve; (iv) the distribution of the builder-premium spread; (v) data-quality findings from the cleaning step.

### Modeling Process

Derived from the Subtask-1 fit and the raw cleaned data. Catamaran vs monohull: catamarans have a higher median asking price (~$432k vs ~$193k monohull) and a shorter, narrower length range (37.5-56 ft, centred ~43.7 ft) than monohulls (36-56 ft, centred ~45.3 ft). Length-price: the global within-make marginal effect is +6.3% per foot (b_L=0.0611); across makes the make dummy itself carries most of the size premium, so the 'bigger boat costs more' statement is largely embedded in the identity term. Depreciation: the concave age curve (b_A=-0.0833, b_A2=+0.00167) implies steepest loss in the first ~5-8 years (~7%/yr at age 5) flattening to ~5%/yr by age 10 and slower beyond - a boat retains a larger fraction of its value the older it gets. Builder spread: e^1.9 (~6x) between the lowest and highest make dummies at equal size/age/region.

### Outcome Analysis

Informative inferences: (1) Catamarans command a large premium over monohulls of similar length and are the dominant 'high-end' segment in this sample (Lagoon alone is ~56% of catamaran listings); a HK broker handling catamarans is dealing with a more concentrated, higher-value, lower-variance (sigma_log=0.166 vs 0.214) market than the monohull market. (2) Depreciation is front-loaded and concave: the worst price erosion happens in the first several years, so 'how many years since delivery' is a first-order lever on value, and a 3-5 year old boat is priced well below a new one but far above a 15-year-old one on a %-of-value basis. (3) Size within a make is worth only ~6%/foot, so two boats of the same make one foot apart differ modestly; the make/brand choice is the bigger value decision (the ~6x builder spread dwarfs the length effect). (4) Data quality: the source required real cleaning - 82 exact duplicates, 3 missing country fields, and ~80 suspect extreme-price records trimmed; the bulk of the 3491 listings is a reliable Dec-2020 asking-price snapshot. (5) The USA premium (Subtask 2) is the single largest composition-adjusted regional effect in the data, suggesting a structurally stronger (and more expensive) used market there; the Caribbean is near parity.
