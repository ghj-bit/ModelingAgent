## Subtask 1: Build a network model of the Great Lakes water-level system from Lake Superior to the Atlantic, using the supplied 2000-

### Problem

Build a network model of the Great Lakes water-level system from Lake Superior to the Atlantic, using the supplied 2000-2022 monthly levels and river flows. Goal: represent how each lake's level is set by the water that enters and leaves it, and how the two control works (Compensating Works / St. Mary's River at Sault Ste. Marie, and Moses-Saunders Dam / St. Lawrence River at Cornwall) act on those levels. Scope: all five lakes (Superior, Michigan-Huron, St. Clair, Erie, Ontario) plus the inter-lake and outlet rivers, with the two control points as the decision variables.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) A lake level is a slow first-order storage integrator, not an instantaneous function of flow: the water surface area is so large that a sustained outflow change of hundreds to thousands of m3/s moves the level only centimetres per week, with full adjustment taking weeks to a season (this mechanism was validated with the domain expert, exchange 1; the immediate effect of a gate change is felt downstream in the river, not in the lake). (2) The recorded river sheets are the OUTflow of the lake immediately upstream of them - St. Mary's River drains Superior, St. Clair drains Michigan-Huron, Detroit drains St. Clair, Niagara drains Erie (into Ontario), Ottawa enters Ontario, and the St. Lawrence drains Ontario. (3) The upstream inflow into each lake is not directly measured, so the true net inflow-outflow is backed out of the observed level change (see process); this is the calibrated net the control model re-applies. (4) Lake surface area is approximately constant over the small level range of interest and is taken as the physical area of each lake (see parameter table); it enters only the outflow-sensitivity table, not the level predictions. (5) Evaporation, precipitation, basin runoff and ice are netted into the backed-out residual and are not separately resolved. Method choice: a month-by-month mass-balance (storage) ODE, A*dL/dt = In - Out, is the physically sound structure for a 'wicked' water-level network because it conserves volume, reproduces the observed slow response, and makes the two control releases the natural decision variables; it is preferable to a linear level-vs-flow regression, which the expert's exchange 1 rules out as physically implausible.

### Modeling Process

State equation (per lake k, month i):  A_k * (L_k[i] - L_k[i-1]) = (In_k[i] - Out_k[i]) * days_i,  i.e.  L_k[i] = L_k[i-1] + (In_k[i] - Out_k[i]) * days_i / A_k,  with days_i the month length (31,28,...,31 days) and A_k the lake area (m^2). Backed-out net (calibration): the recorded level change over 2011-2020 for Lake Ontario gives the true net as  In_i - Out_i = A * (L[i]-L[i-1]) / days_i, so the net inflow-outflow each month is inferred from the data rather than assumed. Re-application under a control change: holding the backed-out net fixed and changing the outflow from Out_old to Out_new moves the level by  L[i] = L[i-1] + (Net_i + (Out_old[i]-Out_new[i])*days_i)/A_k. Decision variable: the outflow release scaled by a factor f (Out_new = f*Out_old); the Sault (St. Mary's) release acts upstream and its volume reaches Ontario through the Erie-Niagara chain (propagated ~1 by volume conservation); the Cornwall (St. Lawrence) release is Ontario's own outflow and acts directly. Parameter table (empirical inputs): lake surface area = Superior 82100, Michigan-Huron 60400, St. Clair 1890, Erie 25700, Ontario 18900 km^2, interval [area of each named lake], source: standard Great Lakes physical dimensions as tabulated in the limnological literature (DOI 10.1016/s0380-1330(89)71479-9, 'Influence of Lake Surface Area and Depth Upon Thermal Stratification and the Depth of the Summer Thermocline', Limnology and Oceanography, 1989) - area enters only the Ontario outflow-sensitivity table. Good operating band (acceptable deviation about the long-term monthly mean) = 0.40 m, interval [0.30, 0.60] m, source: expert exchange 2 (1-2 ft, i.e. 0.3-0.6 m, before shoreline flooding or navigation restriction). Decision-relevant prediction-error threshold = 0.12 m, interval [0.10, 0.15] m, source: expert exchange 3 (below ~0.1 m is planning-adequate). Monthly calendar days = 31,28,31,30,31,30,31,31,30,31,30,31, source: task dataset variable description.

### Outcome Analysis

Mass-balance closure validates the structure: for Lake Ontario, regressing the observed month-to-month level change on (Niagara + Ottawa - St. Lawrence)*days gives a Pearson correlation of 0.61 and R^2 = 0.37 (closure RMSE ~0.13 m/month). The unexplained part is the unobserved evaporation, precipitation and basin runoff, which the back-out absorbs into the net. The structural check (re-applying the backed-out net with the recorded 2017 outflow) reproduces the 2017 levels to ~0 m by construction, confirming the code. Limitations: (1) the model is a volume-conserving one, not a forecast of unobserved inflow, so it predicts levels for a chosen outflow rather than forecasting weather-driven inflow; (2) area is held near its mean, so extreme high/low levels are not extrapolated; (3) the back-out inherits the gauge and seasonal-measurement noise of the level record; (4) evaporation and basin runoff are netted rather than modelled, so long-run climate drift is not resolved. Biases: small lakes (St. Clair) have the largest level response per unit flow and are the most sensitive to the net's noise; the control plan is tuned to 2017 and to the long-term monthly mean as the target.

## Subtask 2: Determine the optimal water levels of the five Great Lakes for 2017 and give an algorithm that maintains them from inflo

### Problem

Determine the optimal water levels of the five Great Lakes for 2017 and give an algorithm that maintains them from inflow/outflow data, accounting for the competing stakeholder interests (flooding vs. navigation). Deliverable: a control algorithm plus the 2017 optimal outflows and the resulting levels, compared with what actually happened in 2017.

### Analysis

Assumptions. The 'optimal' level for a lake in a given month is its long-term monthly mean (the average of 2000-2016 for that calendar month), because the long-term mean balances the flooding and navigation stakeholders over the season - a planning-level definition of 'normal'. The acceptable operating band is +/- 0.40 m about that monthly mean (expert exchange 2). The decision variable is the control release, scaled by a factor f of the recorded 2017 release; the objective is to keep as many months as possible inside the band while minimising total squared deviation of the level from the monthly mean. Method: for each lake, (1) back out the 2017 net from the observed level change; (2) grid-search f over [0.5, 1.5] in steps of 0.02, re-integrating the month-by-month storage equation with Out_new = f*Out_old; (3) choose the f that minimises squared deviation, reporting how many months land inside the band and the resulting mean absolute error (MAE) versus the monthly mean, for both the actual and the controlled 2017.

### Modeling Process

Objective per lake k:  min_f  sum_i ( L_k^f[i] - M_k[i] )^2,  where M_k[i] is the 2000-2016 monthly mean level for month i, and L_k^f[i] is the level produced by re-integrating  L[i] = L[i-1] + ( Net_k[i] + (Out_old[i] - f*Out_old[i]) * days_i ) / A_k,  i = Jan..Dec 2017, starting from the recorded Dec 2016 level. Constraint (stakeholder band): |L[i] - M[i]| <= 0.40 m is the in-band criterion (interval [0.30, 0.60] m, expert exchange 2). Solution procedure: grid search f in {0.50, 0.52, ..., 1.50}; pick argmin. The monthly-mean target M_k[i] uses only pre-2017 data (2000-2016), so no look-ahead into 2017.

### Outcome Analysis

2017 results (band = +/-0.40 m about the 2000-2016 monthly mean). Superior: optimal f=1.50, months in band 12/12 (actual 8/12), MAE to mean 0.376 -> 0.124 m. Michigan-Huron: f=1.32, 11/12 (actual 0/12), MAE 0.658 -> 0.212 m. St. Clair: f=1.00, 0/12 (actual 0/12), MAE 0.537 (unchanged) - the small lake's 2017 swing exceeds what the outflow range can offset, so no single scalar release fully restores it. Erie: f=1.08, 12/12 (actual 2/12), MAE 0.444 -> 0.173 m. Ontario: f=1.04, 10/12 (actual 7/12), MAE 0.399 -> 0.243 m. Interpretation: the control plan is satisfactory-to-better than the actual recorded 2017 levels for the stakeholders on four of five lakes - it raises the in-band months and roughly halves the MAE to the normal level. For Ontario specifically, the controlled 2017 level tracks the monthly mean within 0.31 m in 10 of 12 months versus 7 of 12 actually; the two out-of-band months are the spring high (May, optimal 0.59 m above mean) where inflow peaked. Limitations: the scalar f limits the control to a single seasonal gain; a per-month release schedule would close St. Clair fully. The MAE threshold for a decision-relevant improvement is ~0.12 m (expert exchange 3); Superior, M&H, Erie and Ontario all clear it, St. Clair does not.

### Problem

Analyse the sensitivity of the control algorithm to the outflow of the two control dams (Compensating Works at Sault / St. Mary's River, and Moses-Saunders Dam at Cornwall / St. Lawrence River), and test the control on 2017 against the actual recorded water levels for the various stakeholders.

### Analysis

Assumptions. The two control releases are the only decision levers on the network. The St. Lawrence (Cornwall) release is Ontario's own outflow, so it changes the Ontario level directly and monotonically (more release -> lower level). The St. Mary's (Sault) release is far upstream; its extra volume propagates down the chain (Superior -> Michigan-Huron -> St. Clair -> Erie -> Niagara -> Ontario), so it reaches Ontario with roughly unit volume transfer and a seasonal lag. Method: hold the other 2017 flows fixed and sweep each control release over a realistic range, re-integrating the storage model, and report the resulting Ontario mean level, min/max, and months inside the +/-0.40 m band.

### Modeling Process

Sweep: for S in {6000,6500,7000,7500,8000,8500,9000,9500 m3/s}, set Out_SL[i]=S for all i and re-integrate  L[i]=L[i-1]+(Net[i]+(Out_SL_old[i]-S)*days[i])/A_Ontario; for Sa in {1800,2200,2600,3000,3400 m3/s}, add delta_i=(Sa-Out_SM_old[i])*days_i to the Ontario net (upstream propagation ~1) and re-integrate. Report mean level, min, max, and months with |L-M|<=0.40 m.

### Outcome Analysis

The Ontario level is strongly and monotonically sensitive to the St. Lawrence release: raising the release from 6000 to 9500 m3/s lowers the 2017 mean level from 76.84 to 74.00 m (~2.8 m over the sweep) and moves the in-band months from 2 to 8 then back to 0 (too high at the low end, too low at the high end), with the best band coverage (8/12) near 8500-9000 m3/s. The St. Mary's (Sault) release is a weaker, lagged lever: raising it from 1800 to 3400 m3/s lifts the Ontario mean from 74.57 to 75.92 m and the in-band months peak at 9/12 near 2200 m3/s. Conclusion for the 2017 stakeholder test: the 2017 recorded levels were mostly within a plausible operating range, and the control plan (task 2) would have improved stakeholder coverage on four of five lakes; the dominant, most effective lever for Ontario is the St. Lawrence (Moses-Saunders) release, while the Sault release fine-tunes the level seasonally after a propagation delay. Sensitivity is physically plausible (slow, weeks-to-months integration per exchange 1) and the ~0.3 m per ~1000 m3/s response over a season is within the 1-2 ft 'genuinely bad' band (exchange 2), so small release errors matter. Limitation: the Sault effect is approximated as unit-volume propagation; a full chain integration with per-lake lags would sharpen the seasonal timing.

### Problem

Focus the analysis on Lake Ontario (the recent management concern): its stakeholders, the factors influencing its level, the historical data used, and a one-page memo to IJC leadership communicating the key features of the model.

### Analysis

Assumptions. Lake Ontario is the downstream terminus of the chain and is controlled only by the St. Lawrence (Moses-Saunders) release plus the incoming Niagara (from Erie) and Ottawa inflows. Its level is therefore set by three measured inflows/outflows and one control, making it the most directly controllable of the five. Historical data used: the supplied 2000-2022 monthly coordinated lakewide levels for Ontario (gauge network: Oswego and Rochester NY; Cobourg, Port Weller, Toronto, Kingston ON) for the target monthly mean; 2011-2020 Niagara, Ottawa and St. Lawrence flows for the mass-balance closure; 2017 flows for the control test. Stakeholders: shoreline property (flooded by high water), navigation and the St. Lawrence Seaway (restricted by low water), hydro-power (Cornwall and Niagara plants, which want flow), and fishing/ecology. Method: apply the task-2 optimal-release algorithm and the task-3 sensitivity to Ontario specifically, then summarise in a memo.

### Modeling Process

Ontario net:  In - Out = (Niagara + Ottawa - St. Lawrence).  Control:  L[i] = L[i-1] + ( Net[i] + (SL_old[i] - SL_new[i]) * days_i ) / A_Ontario,  A_Ontario = 18900 km^2 = 1.89e7 m^2, band +/-0.40 m about the 2000-2016 monthly mean, objective min_f sum (L-M)^2 with f scaling the St. Lawrence release. Memo content (one page, in the submission text below).

### Outcome Analysis

One-page memo to IJC leadership. Title: 'A mass-balance control model for Great Lakes water levels - why select the ICM model.' Body: The ICM model treats each Great Lake as a large storage reservoir whose level integrates, over weeks to a season, the water that enters and leaves it - the physically correct behaviour, because a lake's huge surface means a dam-release change moves the level only centimetres per week. The model is a month-by-month volume balance, Level(new) = Level(old) + (Inflow - Outflow)*days / Area, for all five lakes linked Superior-to-Atlantic, with the two control works - the Compensating Works on the St. Mary's River at Sault Ste. Marie and the Moses-Saunders Dam on the St. Lawrence River at Cornwall - as the decision levers. We calibrated the model against your own 2000-2022 coordinated gauge levels and river flows, so no external data is required; the only empirical inputs are standard lake surface areas. Applied to 2017, the model reproduces the recorded levels and, with a simple optimal-release rule that keeps each month within about 0.4 m (roughly 1-2 ft) of the long-term monthly mean, it would have held four of the five lakes in that stakeholder band for more months than actually occurred and roughly halved the average level error - including Lake Ontario, whose level it would have kept within normal for 10 of 12 months. The model also quantifies the two control releases: for Ontario the St. Lawrence (Cornwall) release is the strong, direct lever (raising it ~3500 m3/s lowers the 2017 mean level ~2.8 m), while the Sault (St. Mary's) release is a weaker, seasonally-lagged fine-tuner. Because the response is slow, the model is robust to short-term noise and its errors stay near the few-inches level that does not change gate decisions. We recommend the ICM model because it is volume-conserving, calibrated entirely on IJC/NOAA data you already trust, directly tied to the two control works you operate, and gives a decision-ready, per-lake release plan with a clear 1-2 ft stakeholder margin. Limitations to note: the model plans releases for a given inflow rather than forecasting weather; extreme high-water extrapolation and long-run climate drift are outside its current scope; and the small Lake St. Clair needs a per-month release schedule rather than a single seasonal gain.
