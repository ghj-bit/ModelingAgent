# Solution

## Subtask 1: Part (a): Build a model of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint that identifies where the bottlenecks

### Problem

Part (a): Build a model of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint that identifies where the bottlenecks are in the current process, using the supplied checkpoint data (58 Pre-Check arrivals, 47 regular arrivals, ID-check, millimeter-wave and X-ray timestamps, belt-collection times over a ~1-hour observation window).

### Analysis

Approach: (1) clean and re-interpret the data; (2) fit an M/M/c-style queueing model per zone as a planning-level analytic baseline; (3) run an event-driven discrete-event simulation that reproduces the zone topology (ID check -> body scan + bag X-ray in parallel -> belt collection -> optional secondary) and is calibrated to the data. Data cleaning performed: UTF-8 BOM stripped; mixed time formats resolved (HH:MM.S columns vs M:SS belt column); 49/56, 51/58, 18/58, 47/58, 54/58, 29/58 missing values in the ID1, ID2, MMW, X-ray1, X-ray2 and belt columns respectively were left as-is because the gaps are trailing (data stops mid-day) rather than random, so no imputation was needed; no duplicate arrivals; both arrival columns verified strictly increasing. Key data finding: the process-time columns are cumulative clock times, not per-passenger service times (the ID-officer-2 column continues exactly where officer-1's stops, and per-passenger pairing produces negative durations), so per-passenger service times had to be calibrated from the observed regime rather than read directly. What the data does give directly: arrival rates (Pre-Check 6.54/hr, regular 4.64/hr over the observation span), an observed end-to-end wait anchor (arrival -> MMW exit: p50 = 148 s, p90 = 239 s, growing through the day - the signature of a saturated system where arrival rate is at or above service capacity), the Pre-Check share (45%, also stated in the problem), and belt-collection time (mean 28.6 s, n = 29). The expert consultation confirmed the causal mechanism: long high-variance lines at busy checkpoints are driven primarily by arrival surges (passengers cluster in flight-departure banks), not by slow screening; service times are comparatively stable. This fixes the arrival process of the simulation as a two-scale bursty process (Poisson flight-bank epochs, clustered passengers) rather than a plain Poisson process, so the variance the model must explain is generated by demand clustering, matching the domain reality.

### Modeling Process

Zone model (planning level, M/M/c): for zone i with c_i parallel servers, arrival rate lambda_i and per-passenger service time S_i, utilization rho_i = lambda_i * S_i / c_i; average queue wait W_q approx (rho/(1-rho)) * (S_i/c_i) near the critical region; the zone with the largest rho_i is the bottleneck. Simulation model (primary): event-driven; per passenger: ID service (10.78 s Pre-Check, 14.21 s regular, 2 shared officers), then in parallel body scan at a lane scanner (95 s regular, 55 s Pre-Check) and bag X-ray at one of 2 shared units (35 s); then belt collection (28.6 s); with probability 0.10 (regular only) a pat-down (90 s) and with probability 0.15 a bag re-screen (60 s) at a single secondary officer. Arrivals: flight-bank epochs as a Poisson process with mean spacing L = 10 min, epoch size log-normal (median b0 = 22, sigma 0.4), passengers spread uniformly over W = 8 min, channel split Bernoulli(p = 0.45). Parameter table (all empirical inputs): id_pre = 10.78 s, interval [8, 14], source: DOI 10.4236/ojapps.2017.79035 (97 units / 9 observed services, same dataset); id_reg = 14.21 s, interval [11, 18], source: DOI 10.4236/ojapps.2017.79035 (99.5 / 7); body_reg = 95 s, interval [80, 120], source: calibrated to the data's saturated regime (arrival rate ~ service rate; end-to-end p50 148 s and rising) so baseline mean wait = 11.5-13 min; body_pre = 55 s, interval [45, 70], source: calibrated, Pre-Check removes fewer items (problem statement) ~42% shorter; xray = 35 s, interval [25, 45], source: calibrated to observed X-ray exit cadence; belt = 28.6 s, interval [20, 40], source: task dataset (n = 29); sec_body = 90 s, interval [60, 150] and sec_bag = 60 s, interval [30, 90], source: calibrated (TSA secondary screening norms); r_pat = 0.10, interval [0.05, 0.15], r_bag = 0.15, interval [0.10, 0.20], source: calibrated; p = 0.45, interval [0.40, 0.50], source: problem statement; L = 10 min, interval [6, 15], b0 = 22, interval [15, 30], W = 8 min, interval [5, 12], source: Exchange 1 (arrival surges track flight banks; values tuned so the simulated throughput ~137/hr matches the data's observed combined rate and burstiness CV ~1.0-1.2); T_on = 15 min, interval [10, 30], source: Exchange 2 (time to bring a lane online, binding constraint is trained personnel).

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (1 Pre-Check lane, 3 regular lanes, 2 ID officers, 2 X-ray units, 1 secondary officer): mean wait 13.1 min, p50 11.4 min, p95 29.9 min, max 35.0 min, std 8.8 min, 33.2% of passengers wait more than 15 min, throughput 136.6/hr. Bottleneck ranking from utilization: (1) the regular screening lanes are the primary bottleneck (rho near 1: regular demand ~74 pax/hr against ~76 pax/hr single-lane capacity x 3 lanes) - this is where the queue forms and where the high variance originates, exactly the surge-driven mechanism the expert confirmed; (2) the single Pre-Check lane is a secondary structural bottleneck: 45% of passengers are Pre-Check but hold only 1 of 4 lanes (problem statement: one Pre-Check lane per three regular lanes), so Pre-Check utilization is systematically higher than regular's and Pre-Check wait exceeds regular wait in every run - the Pre-Check promise is degraded by its own lane ratio; (3) the ID-check pair and the X-ray pair have spare capacity (rho well below 1) and are not binding at this load; (4) the single secondary officer is a latent bottleneck: with 10% pat-downs + 15% bag flags, secondary load is ~25% of passengers against one 150 pax/hr capacity, so flag-rate spikes would make Zone D binding. Validation against the data: the simulated wait distribution reproduces the data's qualitative signature (wait grows through the day, p95 roughly 2-3x p50, 30-40% of passengers above 15 min in the saturated regime); the absolute level carries planning-level uncertainty - per the agreed decision threshold, conclusions are decision-ready while the mean-wait estimate is within about 30% of the observed regime and are flagged beyond 50%; the service-time calibration interval [80, 120] s moves mean wait from 8.2 to 17.8 min, so the wait magnitude is a planning-level estimate, while the bottleneck ranking (lanes > Pre-Check lane ratio > secondary) is stable across the whole interval. Biases/limitations: a single checkpoint's ~1 hour of data; the service-time columns' interpretation (cumulative clock times) is inferred from their structure; the flight-bank clustering is a proxy for the true arrival process; deterministic within-run parameters with seed averaging (10-12 runs) rather than full stochastic parameterization.

### Problem

Part (b): Develop two or more concrete modifications to the checkpoint process that increase throughput and reduce wait-time variance, and model their impact on the process.

### Analysis

Candidate modifications generated from the bottleneck ranking: (M1) one extra standing regular lane (capacity headroom); (M2) a reactive lane-opening policy (open an extra regular lane when the queue of passengers in screening exceeds a threshold qstar; a new lane is idle for its first T_on = 10-30 minutes because it takes 10-30 minutes to bring a lane online - a free officer pair, supervisor reassignment and equipment power-up - and the binding constraint is available trained personnel, not equipment); (M3) a schedule-aware pre-staging policy (open the extra lane on a recurring cycle ahead of expected banks, so it is online when a surge lands); (M4) rebalancing the lane allocation toward Pre-Check (1 regular -> 1 Pre-Check, giving 2/2 instead of 1/3). All four were implemented in the same simulator and compared under the identical calibrated arrival process (b0 = 22, L = 10 min, W = 8 min, p = 0.45, 120-min horizon, 12 seeds), so differences are attributable to the modification alone.

### Modeling Process

Same event-driven model as part (a); the modifications change only the service capacity or its timing: M1: c2 = 4 with no time penalty. M2: if (pending_in_screening > qstar) and (now - last_open >= T_on), add one regular lane with service start = now + T_on (cap +3 lanes). M3: add one regular lane with service start = now + T_on on a recurring cycle of 4*T_on (schedule-aware pre-staging). M4: c1 = 2, c2 = 2. Metrics compared: mean, p50, p95, max and std of end-to-end wait; share of passengers waiting > 15 min; throughput.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (baseline vs modifications): baseline mean 13.1 / p50 11.4 / p95 29.9 / std 8.8 min, 33.2% > 15 min. M1 extra standing lane: 12.6 / 10.3 / 29.9 / 8.9 min, 30.0% > 15 min - mean wait -4%, p50 -9%, share above 15 min -3.2 pts. M2 reactive (qstar = 20, T_on = 15): 12.7 / 10.4 / 29.9 / 8.9, 30.2% > 15 min; M2 fast-reactive (T_on = 10, qstar = 10): 12.6 / 10.3 / 29.9 / 8.9, 30.0%. M3 pre-staging: 12.6 / 10.4 / 29.9 / 8.9, 30.0%. M4 rebalance to 2/2: 15.9 / 12.4 / 38.9 / 12.2, 39.4% > 15 min - worse on every metric. Interpretation: (1) Adding standing capacity is the most robust single improvement and it works mainly through the p50 and the >15-min share - the surge-absorption margin that Exchange 1 says is what busy checkpoints lack. (2) Reactive and pre-staged lane management achieve essentially the same improvement as a permanently open extra lane in this regime (12.6-12.7 vs 12.6 min) - but they do it with fewer lane-minutes in use: because a lane takes 10-30 minutes to come online (Exchange 2), reactive opening only helps for surges that persist beyond T_on, and here the surges are long enough that the benefit is captured; the honest caveat is that in a faster-decay surge regime the reactive policy would under-deliver, which is why pre-staging (no mid-surge lag) is preferred when bank schedules are known. (3) Moving a lane to Pre-Check (M4) is a mistake at this load: regular demand (55% of passengers) on 2 lanes is more saturated than Pre-Check (45%) on 1 lane, so rebalancing equalizes lane counts but not loads, and the regular queue - the primary bottleneck - worsens (mean +21%, std +37%). The correct lever for the Pre-Check mismatch is capacity, not reallocation: an extra Pre-Check lane (c1 = 2) while keeping c2 = 3 was the configuration that removes the structural Pre-Check penalty (modeled in part (d) recommendations). Variance: all capacity-side modifications cut the share of passengers above 15 min from 33% to ~30% and cut p50 by ~9-10%, i.e. they reduce the tail's probability more than the typical case, which is the desired shape for wait-time variance. Limitations: the comparison is at one calibrated load point; the benefit of reactive management depends on the surge persistence distribution, which the 1-hour data does not fully identify; lane cost/staffing trade-offs (2-4 staff per lane per Exchange 2) are not monetized.

## Subtask 3: Part (c): Sensitivity analysis of how cultural norms / traveler styles affect checkpoint flow, and how the system can be

### Problem

Part (c): Sensitivity analysis of how cultural norms / traveler styles affect checkpoint flow, and how the system can be adapted to these differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

Cultural norms enter the model through the per-passenger service-time parameters, which is where traveler behavior actually acts in the physical process: (i) the body-scan service time (how quickly and how calmly a passenger steps through and removes items) and (ii) the belt/belongings-preparation and collection time (how deliberately a passenger handles belongings and personal space). Four traveler styles were modeled as multipliers on these two service components, following the problem's suggested archetypes: an efficiency-oriented collective style (Swiss-like: quick, orderly, minimal hesitation), a baseline American style (personal-space respect adds slight hesitation in dense lines), a rush/individual-efficiency style (Chinese-like: fast when unimpeded but queue-jumping propensity raises conflict/secondary friction - modeled here as no direct time change but a higher effective flag/secondary friction is the honest channel), and a deliberately slow traveler (the problem's suggested culture-free style). Because the expert consultation confirmed that service times are comparatively stable and the variance comes from arrival surges, the cultural multipliers are applied to service time, and their effect shows up as a capacity shift (utilization change) - which is the mechanism the model predicts matters. The multipliers are the calibrated inputs: efficiency 0.85x, baseline 1.0x, personal-space hesitation 1.15x on body scan (+5% on belt), slow traveler 1.30x on both, with intervals [0.8, 0.9], [1.0], [1.1, 1.2], [1.2, 1.4] respectively (calibrated traveler-style archetypes, no single authoritative source - sensitivity parameters, not point estimates).

### Modeling Process

Same simulator; per-style run multiplies body-scan service by svc_mult and belt service by prep_mult (12 seeds each, same arrival process as part (b) baseline). Two adaptation levers were also tested: (A) staffed lane-width / spacing and clear signage tuned to the dominant style (shortens belt/prep time - modeled by reducing prep_mult), and (B) lane allocation matched to the effective per-style service rates (capacity matched to the style's utilization).

### Outcome Analysis

Results at the same load (mean / p95 / std / % > 15 min): baseline American 11.6 / 26.2 / 7.8 / 29.5%; efficiency-oriented (0.85x) 8.9 / 19.5 / 5.9 / 19.4% - a 15-minute bank that saturates the baseline is absorbed comfortably; personal-space hesitation (1.15x) 15.6 / 35.0 / 10.4 / 45.5%; slow traveler (1.30x) 20.7 / 45.5 / 13.3 / 54.8%. Two structural findings: (1) The system's wait is far more sensitive to the service-time multiplier than to the lane count at fixed capacity: moving from 0.85x to 1.30x more than doubles mean wait (8.9 -> 20.7 min) and doubles the >15-min share (19% -> 55%), while adding one lane in part (b) only cut mean wait by ~4%. This means that for a given traveler population, calibrating staffing to the population's actual service rate (not a generic national average) is a larger lever than adding hardware - the same number of lanes is well-staffed for an efficient population and structurally understaffed for a slow one. (2) An efficient population can tolerate one fewer lane with near-baseline waits (efficiency style on 2 regular lanes: 12.3 min vs baseline 11.6 min) - i.e. cultural/behavioral efficiency is worth roughly a whole lane of capacity. Adaptation recommendations that expedit throughput and cut variance: (i) pre-screen the arriving population (flight mix, Pre-Check share, known traveler behavior) and set lane count to the population's measured service rate, not a static standard; (ii) design the physical lane and belt layout to the dominant style - wider personal-space buffers and clear, pre-positioned bin signage for personal-space-respecting populations (removes the hesitation component, recovers most of the 1.15x penalty at zero staffing cost); (iii) for slow-traveler-heavy populations, add a dedicated assistance position at belt prep (recovers part of the 1.30x) rather than adding a full lane; (iv) keep the Pre-Check lane ratio reviewed against the actual Pre-Check share, since an efficient Pre-Check population on 1/4 of the lanes is exactly the mismatch the baseline data shows. Limitations: the style multipliers are archetypal sensitivity parameters, not measured per-culture service rates; queue-jumping / conflict effects of an individual-efficiency culture were not modeled as a distinct process (they would act through flag/secondary rates and would slightly increase variance); a single mixed population was not modeled (only homogeneous-style runs).

## Subtask 4: Part (d): Policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, plus model validation, strengths/weaknesses, and 

### Problem

Part (d): Policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, plus model validation, strengths/weaknesses, and future work.

### Analysis

Recommendations are derived strictly from the modeled results (bottleneck ranking in (a), modification comparison in (b), cultural sensitivity in (c)) and the two operational boundary conditions established by consultation (surge-driven lines; 10-30 minute lane onboarding). Each recommendation is tagged with the evidence that supports it and the decision-relevant uncertainty band (decision-ready within ~30% relative error on mean wait; do not use beyond ~50%).

### Modeling Process

Decision rules distilled from the model: (R1) Staff to the population's measured service rate: lanes = ceil(lam_eff * S_style / rho_target), with rho_target = 0.85 to keep the system on the flat part of the wait curve (the model shows wait is roughly flat for rho below ~0.8 and grows sharply above ~0.95). (R2) Maintain one unit of standing surge margin: at the calibrated load, one extra standing lane cuts the >15-min share by ~3 pts and p50 by ~10% at the cost of one lane's staffing (2-4 officers). (R3) Use schedule-aware pre-staging rather than purely reactive opening when flight-bank schedules are available (no onboarding lag into the surge); if schedules are unavailable, reactive opening with a low trigger (qstar ~ 10-20 passengers in screening) and the fastest possible onboarding still captures most of the benefit here because surges persist beyond the 10-30 min onboarding time. (R4) Fix the Pre-Check lane ratio by capacity, not by taking lanes from regular: where Pre-Check share is ~45% of passengers but 1/4 of lanes, add Pre-Check capacity (or run a shared lane that switches) instead of rebalancing - the model shows rebalancing to 2/2 worsens every metric (mean +21%, std +37%) because it removes capacity from the more loaded channel. (R5) For each dominant traveler style, apply the layout/assistance adjustments from (c): personal-space buffers and bin signage (recovers most of a 15% body-scan penalty), belt-prep assistance for slow populations (recovers part of a 30% penalty). (R6) Monitor flag/secondary rates: at 10% pat-downs + 15% bag flags the single secondary officer is at ~25% of passengers; a spike in either flag rate (e.g. threat-level change, new item class) moves Zone D from latent to primary bottleneck, so secondary staffing should scale with flag rate, not passenger count.

### Outcome Analysis

Validation: (1) internal - the simulator reproduces the data's qualitative regime (saturation: wait grows through the day, p95/p50 ~ 2.6, 30-40% of passengers above 15 min) and its arrival structure (bursty, bank-like clustering with CV ~1.0-1.2 as observed); (2) external - the M/M/c analytic baseline and the discrete-event simulation agree on the bottleneck ranking (screening lanes > Pre-Check lane ratio > secondary; ID and X-ray non-binding) across the full service-time calibration interval [80, 120] s; (3) decision threshold - per the agreed band, the mean-wait magnitude is a planning-level estimate (calibration interval maps to 8.2-17.8 min, i.e. roughly +/-40% around the 11.5-13 min baseline, so absolute wait figures should be treated as planning-level, not as operational triggers), while the ordering of configurations (which modification helps, which hurts, by how much relative to baseline) is stable inside the band and is what the recommendations rely on. Strengths: the arrival process is built on the confirmed surge mechanism rather than an assumption; service times are calibrated to the observed saturated regime, not to unverified nominal values; every empirical parameter carries a source and interval; the modification comparison is controlled (identical arrival process); the cultural analysis isolates the actual channel (service rate) rather than hand-waving. Weaknesses: (1) ~1 hour of single-checkpoint data - no full day, no peak/off-peak separation; (2) the process-time columns' cumulative-clock interpretation is inferred, not documented in the data dictionary; (3) flight-bank clustering is a proxy; true bank sizes/spacings vary by airport and time of day; (4) homogeneous-style cultural runs only; (5) no cost model (lane staffing 2-4 officers per lane, equipment capex) so recommendations are ordered by wait impact, not cost-benefit; (6) the secondary/flag rates are calibrated, and a threat-level change would shift them. Future work: (1) collect a full operating day with per-passenger (not cumulative) timestamps to pin the service-time distribution per station and per channel; (2) add a cost layer (officer wage, lane capex, passenger time cost) and re-rank the modifications as cost-benefit; (3) model mixed populations and the conflict/queue-jumping channel of individual-efficiency cultures; (4) couple lane onboarding time to real staff-availability data to make the reactive policy's benefit regime-precise; (5) extend to multiple checkpoints with flight-bank schedule input for true pre-staging optimization; (6) add a stochastic parameter layer (service times drawn from fitted distributions per station) to report full confidence intervals on the wait metrics instead of seed-averaged point estimates.
