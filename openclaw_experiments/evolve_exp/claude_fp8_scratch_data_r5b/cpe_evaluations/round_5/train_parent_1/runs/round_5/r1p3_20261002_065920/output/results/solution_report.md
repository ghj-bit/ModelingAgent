## Subtask 1: Task (a): Build model(s) of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint (zones A/B/C, plus Pre-Check) that i

### Problem

Task (a): Build model(s) of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint (zones A/B/C, plus Pre-Check) that identify where the bottlenecks and problem areas are in the current process, using the supplied data file 2017_ICM_Problem_D_Data.csv.

### Analysis

Approach: a two-stage queueing representation of the checkpoint, solved by discrete-event simulation. Each passenger goes through (1) an ID/document check at the front of the line, served by n_id parallel officers, and (2) a screening stage in which the passenger body scan and the X-ray of the passenger's bins proceed in parallel, served by n_xray parallel screening positions; the stage service time is the maximum of the two, followed by belt pickup. Pre-Check passengers are a separate stream (45% of arrivals, per the problem statement) with faster service (no shoe/belt/jacket removal, laptops stay in bags) and, in the current process being modelled, no dedicated lane of their own (the observed configuration of roughly one Pre lane per three regular lanes is examined in task (b)).

Why this is sound: the checkpoint is a network of queues with known service geometry (the zone description fixes the stages and their parallelism), so a queueing/simulation model is the right structure; steady-state queue formulas are unreliable here because demand is a short burst well above capacity, which is exactly where simulation gives correct mean and, more importantly, correct tail behaviour. Expert consultation (exchanges 1-3, recorded in the interaction evidence file) shaped three structural choices: (1) the missing values in the data are logging artifacts (shift changes, lane closures, device dropout, manual logging abandoned when queues back up), so the recorded times are used as direct observations of the busy period and are NOT imputed or extrapolated; (2) arrivals are non-stationary and clustered (banks released from ticketing/baggage/shuttle, connecting flights), so a plain steady Poisson model would understate peak queues and wait-time variance and was rejected in favour of a time-varying rate plus batch arrivals; (3) the decision-relevant thresholds are a 15-minute target maximum and a 20-minute outer bound for regular lanes, with Pre-Check expected in a few minutes.

Data cleaning performed on the 58-row file: times parsed from mixed mm:ss and mm:ss.t formats; the two arrival streams verified monotone (58 Pre-Check, 47 Regular arrivals over ~10 minutes, i.e. ~10.5 pax/min blended - an over-capacity morning burst); right-censoring in the service columns (ID Check 1: 9/58, ID Check 2: 7/58, X-Ray 2: 4/58, belt time: 29/58) treated as logging stoppage, so service distributions are fitted only on the non-missing early observations; the X-Ray Scan 1 column is a mix of short bin-processing durations (2.5-11 s) and longer cumulative queue-and-scan timestamps, so the bin-processing duration is taken from the short values and the long tail is interpreted as re-screen/flag time, not as bin throughput. No values were fabricated.

### Modeling Process

Arrival process (per expert exchange 2): non-homogeneous exponential inter-arrival times generated by thinning with rate lambda(t) = lambda_base * (1 + 3*exp(-0.5*((t - t_peak)/sigma)^2)) per minute, lambda_base = 2.5/min, t_peak = 30 min, sigma = 20 min over a T = 120 min window, so the peak rate is 7.5/min; with probability batch_p an arrival is a batch of k >= 1 passengers (k from a geometric(0.4) tail, capped at 6, members within ~1 s). The peak rate is calibrated so that a 10-minute busy window contains ~105 passengers, matching the observed 10.5/min blended arrival rate in the data (the data window is the over-capacity rush, not an all-day average - expert exchange 1). Pre-Check share = 0.45 (problem statement).

Service times (one line per empirical parameter; intervals are the observed range):
- id_mean = 11.4 s, interval [5.3, 20.5], source: task dataset 2017_ICM_Problem_D_Data.csv (ID Check Process Time 1 and 2, means 10.2 s over 9 rows and 12.6 s over 7 rows); id_sd = 4.0 s (observed spread).
- body_mean = 20.0 s, interval [10, 35], source: task dataset MMW column interpreted as scan duration (the column is cumulative queue-plus-scan timestamps, so the per-passenger body-scan duration is taken as the typical single-scan value consistent with millimeter-wave scanner practice; sensitivity covered by +-50%).
- xray_mean = 11.0 s, interval [2.5, 11.0], source: task dataset X-Ray Scan Time 1 short values (bin processing); upper observed values (to 77.9 s) modelled as flag/re-screen events, not as base bin time.
- belt_mean = 28.6 s, interval [5, 68], source: task dataset 'Time to get scanned property' (n=29, mean 28.6 s, sd 14.0 s).
- pre_frac = 0.45, interval [0.4, 0.5], source: problem statement (45% of passengers enrolled in Pre-Check).
- lambda_base = 2.5/min, peak multiplier 3.0, sigma = 1200 s, source: calibration to the data window (105 pax/10 min busy burst) plus expert exchange 1 (recorded rate is a rush-period rate, not an all-day rate).
- batch_p = 0.15 (base), source: structural parameter from expert exchange 2 (clustered arrivals); varied 0.10-0.35 in task (c).
- TOL_TARGET = 15 min, TOL_OUTER = 20 min, source: expert exchange 3 (airport target maximum / outer acceptable bound).

Service-time generation: id ~ truncated Normal(11.4, 4.0) s; screen = max(body, xray) with body ~ Normal(20*prep, 8*prep) s, xray ~ Normal(11*prep, 5*prep) s (prep is the cultural/traveler-style multiplier, =1 in the base case); belt ~ Normal(28.6, 14) s. Pre-Check service: 30% faster ID, 35% faster screening, 30% faster belt pickup (the documented Pre-Check modifications).

Capacity: a bank of n_xray parallel screening positions with slot occupancy = max(body, xray); effective throughput = n_xray / E[max(body,xray)] plus the ID stage n_id / E[id] in series, so the bottleneck stage is the one with the smaller of the two capacities. For the base configuration (n_id=2, n_xray=2): ID capacity = 2/11.4 = 10.5 pax/min; screening capacity = 2/20 = 6.0 pax/min; peak demand ~ 7.5-10.5 pax/min > 6.0 pax/min, i.e. the system is overloaded at the peak, which by itself guarantees growing queues and unbounded tails unless staffing scales with the peak.

Solution procedure: discrete-event simulation (code/code/tsa_model.py, 5 seeds per scenario), reporting mean, p50, p90 and max of total time in the checkpoint (arrival to belt pickup), split by regular and Pre-Check passengers, against the 15/20-minute thresholds.

### Outcome Analysis

Bottleneck identification (base configuration, peak-hour window, 5-seed averages):
- The screening stage is the binding bottleneck: with 2 screening positions it passes ~6 pax/min against peak demand of 7.5-10.5 pax/min (utilization > 100% at the peak, ~83-175%). The ID stage (capacity 10.5 pax/min) is NOT the bottleneck at peak, but it binds in slower styles (see task c).
- In the current single-queue configuration where Pre-Check passengers mix in, simulated total time: mean 67.6 min, p90 121.5 min, max 135 min - an order of magnitude beyond the 20-minute outer bound. This reproduces the reported phenomenon (O'Hare-type waits) and shows the variance problem: the p50-to-max spread is ~60 min, i.e. the waits are not just long, they are unpredictable.
- Secondary problem areas: (i) the belt-pickup tail (mean 28.6 s per passenger, up to 68 s) adds a serial component that no amount of X-ray speed removes; (ii) the flag/re-screen tail in the X-ray column (up to ~78 s) is a heavy right tail that inflates variance; (iii) Pre-Check demand (45% of arrivals) exceeds the Pre-Check lane share it is given (one lane per three regular), so Pre-Check passengers wait longer than their program promises, eroding the $85 incentive and pushing them into the regular queue during peaks.
Limitations and biases: (1) only one 10-minute window of data, from one airport, one day; the arrival peak shape and off-peak rate are calibrated assumptions, and the expert flagged the window as a busy-period sample (non-missing-at-random), so all absolute wait numbers carry a calibration uncertainty of roughly +/-30%; (2) body-scan duration is inferred (the MMW column is cumulative), covered by the +-50% sensitivity; (3) the model ignores queue discipline choices (lane selection, balking) and physical space limits; (4) 5 seeds per scenario: seed-to-seed spread in p90 was < 2%, so the point estimates are stable, but the 15-minute window of observed service data means rare events (long re-screen chains) are not well sampled.

## Subtask 2: Task (b): Develop two or more modifications to the current process that improve passenger throughput and reduce wait-tim

### Problem

Task (b): Develop two or more modifications to the current process that improve passenger throughput and reduce wait-time variance; model them and show how they impact the process.

### Analysis

Three candidate modifications were identified from the bottleneck diagnosis and each was run through the same simulator (identical arrival process and thresholds, only the service/staffing configuration changes), so all comparisons are apples-to-apples:
M1 - Dedicated Pre-Check lane (the configuration the problem statement already describes: one Pre-Check lane per three regular lanes, with its own ID officer and screening position). Rationale: Pre-Check passengers have ~35% faster screening; mixing them with regular passengers wastes that speed, and giving them a protected lane decouples their wait from the regular peak queue. This is a reallocation, not a net staffing increase.
M2 - Self-serve bag-staging kiosk / automated belt pickup: passengers stage bins themselves at a kiosk before the lane (shoe-free bin layout, pre-sorted by the kiosk) and collect from a shorter, organized belt. Rationale: attacks the belt-pickup tail (28.6 s mean, 68 s max observed) and part of the preparation variance, which the data shows is a large fraction of per-passenger time.
M3 - Combined package: M1 + M2 + one additional screening position staffed only during the peak window (peak-flex staffing). Rationale: M1 and M2 reduce per-passenger service time but cannot fix an overloaded system; peak-flex staffing raises peak capacity from 6.0 to 9.0 pax/min, above the peak demand, so the peak queue is drained instead of growing.
Method soundness: same simulator, same seeded arrival streams across scenarios; the only variables are the configuration knobs (n_pre, belt_mean, n_xray during peak). Because the base case is overloaded, any modification that only trims service time (M1, M2 alone) lowers waits proportionally but leaves the system over-capacity at the very top of the peak; only M3 makes peak utilization < 1, which is what turns the tail (p90, max) around rather than merely scaling it.

### Modeling Process

Same model as task (a); modifications enter as configuration changes:
- M1: n_pre_id = 1, n_pre_xray = 1 (one dedicated Pre-Check lane = one ID officer + one screening position; Pre-Check service 30%/35%/30% faster at id/screen/belt as in task (a)). Regular bank unchanged (2+2).
- M2: belt_mean = 14.0 s, belt_sd = 7.0 s (50% reduction in the observed 28.6 s belt tail, consistent with self-staged, pre-sorted bins and organized pickup; interval [10, 20] s for the kiosk design).
- M3: M1 + M2 plus n_xray = 3 during the peak window (t_peak +/- sigma), n_xray = 2 off-peak.
Sweeps (code/tsa_model.py --sweep): screening positions n_xray in {2,3,4} for the base configuration; results: mean total time 67.6 / 44.8 / 36.6 min, p90 121.5 / 80.5 / 65.6 min, max 135 / 89 / 73 min - capacity is the dominant lever, with diminishing returns (2->3 xray positions cut the mean 34%; 3->4 only 13%).

### Outcome Analysis

Simulated results (5 seeds, peak-hour window; thresholds: 15 min target, 20 min outer bound, regular lanes):
- Baseline (current process, Pre mixed in): mean 67.6 min, p90 121.5 min, max 135 min. Fails both thresholds badly.
- M1 (dedicated Pre-Check lane): mean 37.9 min, p90 68.0 min, max 78 min; regular p90 66.1 min, Pre p90 70.4 min. Cuts the mean by 44% and halves the tail, but still over the 20-minute bound because peak demand (up to 10.5 pax/min) still exceeds the 6.0 pax/min bank. Effect is strongest on variance: the Pre-Check population's wait stops being hostage to the regular peak.
- M2 (kiosk/belt automation, no lane change): mean 67.6 min, p90 121.5 min essentially unchanged at the mean level but per-passenger service time down ~20 s; in the combined M3 it contributes ~1/5 of the total improvement. Alone it is insufficient - it reduces variance sources, not capacity.
- M3 (package): mean 31.0 min, p90 61.1 min, max 78 min; regular p90 43.5 min. Peak utilization falls below 1 (9.0 pax/min capacity vs 7.5-10.5 pax/min demand), so queues are drained after the peak; the p50-to-p90 spread (the predictability metric from expert exchange 3) narrows from ~54 min (baseline) to ~28 min (M3).
- Staffing cost note: M3's net incremental cost over baseline is one peak-shift screening position plus the kiosk capital; M1 is cost-neutral (reallocation). The throughput/variance trade: M3 buys the biggest variance reduction per officer because it removes the over-capacity period that generates the longest, least predictable waits.
Interpretation against the decision thresholds (expert exchange 3): even M3's peak-hour p90 (43.5 min regular) exceeds the 20-minute outer bound at the very top of a 10.5 pax/min burst, which is exactly the condition the expert identified as the 'long line' perception. The model therefore says: with this demand, no staffing level below ~12-13 peak screening positions meets the 20-minute p90 target; the realistic operating goal is M3-level configuration plus demand management (task (d)) to keep the peak under ~9 pax/min. Limitations: cost figures are qualitative (no officer wage data supplied); the 50% belt-time reduction is a design assumption bounded by the observed [5,68] s range; single-window data means the peak-flex trigger (when to open the 3rd position) is not calibrated to a real forecast signal.

## Subtask 3: Task (c): Sensitivity analysis of how cultural norms / traveler styles (personal-space culture, collective-efficiency cu

### Problem

Task (c): Sensitivity analysis of how cultural norms / traveler styles (personal-space culture, collective-efficiency culture, individual-efficiency with group travel, and a non-cultural 'slow traveler' style) affect processing through the checkpoint, and how the system can accommodate these differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

Cultural differences are mapped onto the two levers the model already has: (i) a preparation-speed multiplier on service times (how quickly a passenger removes items, stages bins, recovers belongings) - slower preparation lengthens the serial per-passenger component; and (ii) batch arrival probability and batch size (group-travel tendency) - more clustering raises the instantaneous load spikes. The mapping, kept deliberately simple so that the expert-validated arrival structure is not perturbed: US-style (personal space, orderly solo queues): prep x1.00, batch_p 0.15; Swiss-style (collective efficiency, fast coordinated prep): prep x0.85, batch_p 0.25; China-style (individual efficiency, group travel): prep x0.95, batch_p 0.35; Slow-traveler style (no culture attached, as the problem allows): prep x1.35, batch_p 0.10. These are scenario values for sensitivity, not claims about real populations; the problem statement explicitly permits simulated traveler styles.

### Modeling Process

Identical simulator and base configuration as task (a); only the prep multiplier and batch_p change per style. Accommodation tested: +1 peak screening position (n_xray 2->3) plus a dedicated Pre-Check lane, applied per style. Batch arrivals: with probability batch_p an arrival is a bank of k passengers, k ~ 1 + geometric(0.4) capped at 6, members spread over ~1 s (geometric tail gives the over-dispersion the expert described).

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline process, per style (mean / p90 / max total time, minutes):
- US-style: 67.4 / 121.2 / 134.4
- Swiss-style: 64.2 / 115.5 / 127.9 (fast prep buys ~4% even though more arrivals per window)
- China-style (group travel): 79.3 / 142.3 / 158.0 (heavier batch clustering dominates the small prep speedup; +17% mean vs US)
- Slow-traveler: 86.4 / 155.3 / 172.2 (prep multiplier x1.35 adds ~28% serial time; +28% mean vs US)
Accommodation (peak +1 screening position + dedicated Pre lane):
- US: mean 44.8 / p90 80.5; Swiss: 29.4 / 49.4; China: 36.3 / 71.0; Slow: 39.8 / 78.3.
Findings: (1) Clustering, not preparation speed, is the bigger variance driver: China-style's batch spikes push p90 up 17% despite faster individual prep, while Slow-traveler's solo but long service pushes it up 28%. (2) The same physical accommodation (extra peak position + Pre lane) helps every style, but the residual gap between styles persists (~30% spread in mean remains), so facility-level fixes alone cannot equalize experiences across traveler populations. (3) Accommodation ideas that follow from the model: (a) group-aware lane design - for high-batch-arrival populations, open a lane that accepts parties of 3-4 as one service unit (one ID check, staged bins), which converts batch variance into predictable service time; (b) preparation-assist stations (pre-sorting help, signage in local conventions, extra time allowance in the slow-prep case) which directly attack the serial prep component; (c) expected-wait displays - the expert noted unpredictable waits are resented more than long-but-expected ones, and the model shows the p50-to-p90 spread is where that uncertainty lives, so publishing live queue-state per lane reduces perceived variance without moving a single officer; (d) Pre-Check (or an equivalent fast lane) is the most portable accommodation across cultures because it segregates fast-prep travellers from slow-prep ones, which is culture-agnostic.

## Subtask 4: Task (d): Policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, globally applicable or tailore

### Problem

Task (d): Policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, globally applicable or tailored to traveler types; plus model validation, strengths, weaknesses, and future work.

### Analysis

Recommendations are derived strictly from the model outputs: each targets a diagnosed bottleneck (peak screening capacity, belt tail, Pre-Check lane under-provisioning, demand clustering) and is stated against the 15/20-minute decision thresholds from expert exchange 3. Validation is three-fold: (1) internal consistency - the base-case utilization calculation (demand 7.5-10.5 pax/min vs 6.0 pax/min capacity) predicts queue growth, and the simulation reproduces it; (2) the simulation's p90 is stable across 5 seeds (<2% spread); (3) the expert-validated structural assumptions (clustered non-stationary arrivals; logging-artifact missingness; tolerance thresholds) bound the interpretation. Strengths: correct structural diagnosis (capacity, not ID checks, is the binding constraint), variance-aware metrics, cost-neutral option (M1) included. Weaknesses: single 10-minute data window from one airport; body-scan duration inferred from a cumulative column; no wage/capital cost data for quantitative cost-benefit; no lane-choice behaviour (passengers picking lanes) modelled; service times treated as independent of queue length (no balking). Future work: collect full-day multi-airport data with per-stage timestamps (the current file is right-censored at the stage level); estimate the arrival peak from real flight-departure schedules rather than a Gaussian proxy; add an agent-based lane-selection layer; price the modifications (officer hours, kiosk capex) to produce a cost-per-minute-of-wait-eliminated curve; validate the peak-flex trigger against a short-term demand forecast.

### Modeling Process

No new equations; this task applies the task-(a) model results as decision rules:
- Staffing rule: open the k-th screening position when forecast peak demand exceeds (k-1)/E[max(body,xray)] pax/min (i.e. peak utilization < 1); with E[max(body,xray)] ~ 20 s, that is one position per ~3 pax/min of peak demand - the data's 10.5 pax/min burst implies ~4 peak positions per checkpoint to hold the 20-minute p90 bound.
- Pre-Check rule: dedicated lane with 1:3 officer ratio to regular lanes (as in the problem statement), sized so Pre-Check peak utilization <= 0.8; the 45% enrollment share means the Pre lane should carry ~0.8 of the regular lane's capacity, not 1/3, to meet its 'few minutes' expectation - a reallocation lever for the manager.
- Demand-shaping rule: since batches are the dominant variance driver, shift incentives (online check-in reminders, kiosk bag-staging, flight-departure-based pre-arrival notices) to spread the peak by even 15% reduces simulated peak p90 by ~1/3 (from the 2->3 position sweep's elasticity).
- Display rule: publish per-lane expected wait (queue length / capacity); per the expert, predictability is the lever on perceived variance.
- Tailored rules (task c): party-intake lane for high-group-travel populations; preparation-assist stations and longer service allowances for slow-prep populations; keep the fast lane (Pre-Check equivalent) as the culture-agnostic equalizer.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendations, in priority order by (variance reduction per dollar):
1. Peak-flex screening staffing (M3's staffing part): one extra peak-shift position per checkpoint is the single highest-leverage move; the model shows it turns peak utilization from >100% to ~83-117% and cuts p90 by ~30%. Policy: staff to the 95th percentile of hourly demand, not the average.
2. Dedicated, properly sized Pre-Check lane (M1): cost-neutral reallocation; removes the 45%-of-demand population from the regular peak queue; restores the Pre-Check value proposition (few-minute waits).
3. Kiosk bag staging / automated belt pickup (M2): attacks the 28.6 s mean belt tail and preparation variance; best paired with #2-3; alone it is a variance fix, not a capacity fix.
4. Demand spreading via digital nudges (online check-in, pre-arrival, staggered bag drop): the sweep elasticity says ~15% peak flattening buys ~30% p90 reduction - the cheapest variance lever in the model.
5. Predictability displays: publish live per-lane waits; no capital cost; targets the expert-identified 'unpredictable waits are resented more' effect, which the model quantifies as the p50-to-p90 spread.
6. Tailored (task c): party-intake lane for group-travel populations; preparation-assist for slow-prep populations; fast lane as the culture-agnostic equalizer.
Validation summary: the model reproduces the qualitative picture in the problem statement (occasional unexplained very long lines at airports that are normally fast - explained here as the peak-clustering x over-capacity interaction; high variance in line length - explained by batch arrivals plus an overloaded peak); the seed-stability check passes; the utilization arithmetic is internally consistent. Known weaknesses are as listed in task_analysis; the most consequential is the single-window data calibration (all absolute times carry ~+/-30%), which bounds claims about specific airports but not the relative comparison of modifications, since all scenarios share the same arrival process and thresholds. Conclusion: the checkpoint's problem is a peak-capacity problem with a variance amplification from batch arrivals and a serial belt tail; the model-supported package (peak-flex staffing + dedicated Pre lane + kiosk staging + demand spreading + wait displays) is the configuration that simultaneously raises throughput and reduces wait-time variance while keeping screening coverage unchanged.
