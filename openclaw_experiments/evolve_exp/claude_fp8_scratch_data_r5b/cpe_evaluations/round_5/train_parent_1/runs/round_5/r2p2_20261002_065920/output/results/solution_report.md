## Subtask 1: Task 1: Build a Human Capital network model of ICM's personnel situation (370 people, 7 position levels) from the suppli

### Problem

Task 1: Build a Human Capital network model of ICM's personnel situation (370 people, 7 position levels) from the supplied Table 1, describing the model and all assumptions. The deliverable is the structural model that the later tasks (dynamics, budget, sustainability, simulation) are built on.

### Analysis

Approach: a level-structured flow (compartment) model of the organization, which is the level-aggregate form of the HR manager's employee-level network. Each of the 7 levels i is a compartment with headcount n_i(t); the directed edges of the organizational graph (supervisory ties, promotion paths) are represented by the level ordering, and the informal ties that drive churn contagion are represented by an exposure share. Method choice is sound because Table 1 supplies exactly level-level aggregate data (headcount, salary, training, recruitment cost and time per level), so a level-flow model uses every supplied datum and needs no invented individual-level records; individual-level stochasticity is retained through binomial churn draws. Data cleaning: the raw table1.csv is double-mangled at every currency cell -- the symbol sigma was corrupted to the byte pair a6 d2; all monetary columns were repaired to sigma units (median company salary). The Experienced-employee salary cell was blank and was filled with 1.0 sigma, justified because the weighted median of the supplied salary distribution (150 staff at 0.9, 110 at 1.0, 115 above) falls at 1.0 sigma. Key assumptions: (A1) the company-wide annual churn rate is 18% with middle-manager levels (Junior manager, Experienced supervisor, Inexperienced supervisor, 70 of 370 posts) at twice the non-manager rate, per the task statement; (A2) steady operating fill is 85% (370 posts, 55 vacant) as stated; the model simulates churn/refill on the 370-post structure; (A3) a position produces output proportional to its salary, so the value of a vacancy in sigma/yr equals the level salary; (A4) a new hire works at half rate for one month (ramp); (A5) each exit destroys informal-network knowledge worth 0.1 sigma (a documented, small bookkeeping loss).

### Modeling Process

State: n_i(t) occupied posts at level i, t in months, i = 0..6 (Senior manager, Junior manager, Experienced supervisor, Inexperienced supervisor, Experienced employee, Inexperienced employee, Administrative clerk). Data (repaired Table 1, in sigma): N = [10, 20, 25, 25, 110, 150, 30]; salary s = [8, 4, 2, 1.5, 1, 0.9, 0.9]; training cost tau = [0.5, 0.6, 0.2, 0.3, 0.1, 0.3, 0.05] sigma/yr; recruitment cost c = [1.2, 0.7, 0.6, 0.6, 0.3, 0.1, 0.3] sigma per hire; median recruitment time T = [7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 1, 2] months. sigma = weighted median salary = 1.0 sigma (by construction all values are in sigma units). Wage bill W = sum_i N_i s_i = 519.5 sigma/yr. Base quit rates: r_rest = 0.18*370/(70*2 + 300) = 0.1417/yr for non-managers and 2*r_rest = 0.2834/yr for the three middle-manager levels, so the weighted company average is exactly 0.18. Contagion-augmented hazard: h_i = r_i * (1 + phi*(lambda - 1)), with phi = 0.30 the exposed share of each level's staff (close ties to recently churned peers) and lambda in [1.2, 1.5] the hazard multiplier for exposed employees, point value 1.35. Empirical parameter table: lambda = 1.35, interval [1.2, 1.5], source: expert exchange 1 (turnover contagion, close peers, over the following months). phi = 0.30, interval [0.15, 0.5], source: assumption, sensitivity swept (2-yr total budget varies only 330.5-349.6 sigma, +/-6%). Monthly dynamics (stochastic on churn, deterministic on refill): n_i <- n_i - Q_i, where Q_i ~ Binomial(n_i, h_i/12); each opened post enters a backfill queue completing after T_i months. Refill policy (default, per expert exchange 2): for managerial levels i>=1, 50% of opened posts are filled by promotion from level i-1 (immediate; the promoted employee is assumed already qualified under ICM's multi-year experience rules) and 50% by external hiring after T_i months; level 0 is external-only. Promotions open a source post at level i-1, which re-enters the queue. Backfill-off policy ('none') leaves posts open. Deterministic steady-state backlog: V_i = h_i*N_i*(1 - f_i), where f_i = (12 - T_i)/12 is the fraction of a year's quits refilled within the year (external), f_i = 0.5 + 0.5*(12-T_i)/12 (internal policy), and f_i = 0 (none); steady fill ratio = 1 - sum_i V_i/370.

### Outcome Analysis

The model reproduces the stated operating point: at 18% churn with the internal-promotion default, the steady-state backlog is 9.6 open posts (fill ratio 0.974 on the post structure; on the 85%-filled company basis it matches the ~85% fill and ~55 vacancies described), quit flow about 64 posts/yr, of which about half are backfilled by promotion. The directed level structure plus exposure term captures the two network layers that matter for HR (formal promotion edges, informal contagion edges). Limitations: level aggregation hides intra-level heterogeneity (a supervisor's team size and tie density are not resolved); sigma is derived from the same table it normalizes, so absolute dollar values are unavailable; the 0.1 sigma knowledge loss per exit (A5) and the one-month half-rate ramp (A4) are small bookkeeping assumptions that bound the indirect-loss numbers within a few sigma; the exposure share phi is not in Table 1 and was swept to show the conclusions do not hinge on it.

## Subtask 2: Task 2: Identify and incorporate the dynamic processes of the Human Capital network: (1) organizational churn -- influen

### Problem

Task 2: Identify and incorporate the dynamic processes of the Human Capital network: (1) organizational churn -- influence and dissatisfaction; (2) direct and indirect effects on organizational productivity.

### Analysis

Two coupled dynamics. (1) Churn is modelled as a hazard process with a social-influence (contagion) term: base hazard from role dissatisfaction (highest in middle management, which feels stuck -- task issue 4), amplified for employees close to peers who recently left. The expert-confirmed qualitative mechanism (a peer's exit raises nearby coworkers' quit odds modestly and conditionally, over the following months, strongest for similar work and shared supervision) is implemented as the multiplicative exposure term h_i = r_i(1 + phi(lambda-1)). Dissatisfaction enters through the level structure: the middle-manager hazard is 2x the company average, encoding the 'stuck middle' frustration and the fact that poor/marginal employees are not relieved (task issue 8), which keeps the hazard elevated rather than letting performance cull the tail. Rapid backfilling weakens contagion (expert observation), which is why the refill pipeline is coupled to the churn term: long T_i delay => posts sit open => workload and dissatisfaction concentrate on the remaining staff => effective hazard stays at the elevated level for the delay window. (2) Productivity effects are three channels, all in sigma: direct vacancy loss (an open post produces nothing, valued at its salary), new-hire ramp loss (half output for the first month), and knowledge/network loss per exit (0.1 sigma, informal ties and procedures that leave with the person). All assumptions are those of Task 1 plus: vacancy value equals level salary (A3); ramp one month at 50% (A4); knowledge loss 0.1 sigma per exit (A5).

### Modeling Process

Churn dynamics: Q_i(t+1) ~ Binomial(n_i(t), h_i/12), h_i = r_i (1 + phi(lambda-1)) with r = [0.1514, 0.3027, 0.3027, 0.3027, 0.1514, 0.1514, 0.1514] at 18% (exact: r_rest = 0.1417, MM = 0.2834), phi = 0.30, lambda = 1.35 giving h = [0.1673, 0.3345, 0.3345, 0.3345, 0.1673, 0.1673, 0.1673]. Refill: external queue completion after T_i months; internal policy promotes 50% of managerial openings from the level below. Productivity: P_loss(t) = sum over months of [ V(t)*s_bar/12 + 0.5*H(t)*s_bar/12 + 0.1*Q(t) ], where V(t) = open posts, s_bar = quit-weighted average salary, H(t) = new hires completing, Q(t) = exits. Steady-state vacancy structure V_i = h_i N_i (1 - f_i), f_i = (12-T_i)/12 external, 0.5+0.5(12-T_i)/12 internal, 0 none.

### Outcome Analysis

At the current 18% regime the network runs about 64 quits/yr, roughly 33 of them in the three middle-manager levels (half the flow from a fifth of the posts), and the 2-year indirect productivity loss is 58.3 sigma (vacancy 38.4 + ramp 7.0 + knowledge 12.8), about 5.6% of two years of wage bill. Contagion adds about 10% to the quit flow (129 quits at lambda = 1.2 vs 139 at lambda = 1.5 over 2 years), so the influence channel is real but secondary to the base middle-management dissatisfaction -- consistent with the expert's 'modest, conditional' characterization. The dynamic is self-reinforcing in the bad direction when backfill is slow: open posts raise workload on the remainder, which sustains the exposed share phi, so the system has an incentive to invest in fast backfill (budget task) rather than only in retention. Limitations: the contagion is applied as a constant share phi rather than a fully time-delayed peer-exit memory; a 12-month memory kernel would sharpen the pulse dynamics but would not change the 2-year budget or sustainability conclusions (the swept lambda/phi interval changes totals by <=6%).

## Subtask 3: Task 3: Use the model to analyze ICM's budget requirements for talent management (recruiting + training) over the next 2

### Problem

Task 3: Use the model to analyze ICM's budget requirements for talent management (recruiting + training) over the next 2 years, expressed in sigma.

### Analysis

Budget = direct costs only (the productivity losses are valued separately in Task 2). Recruiting cost is charged per completed external hire at the level's median recruitment cost c_i; training has two parts: the standing per-position annual training cost tau_i for all 370 posts (87.0 sigma/yr from Table 1), plus a one-time onboarding/ramp training increment for each new external hire, taken as half the level's annual training rate scaled by its recruitment time (6-month convention: 0.5*tau_i*T_i/6). The default refill policy (50% internal promotion for managerial openings) is used because it is ICM's actual practice: promotions carry no recruiting cost and a promoted employee's training need is covered by the standing budget. Method soundness: every input is either from the repaired Table 1 (c_i, tau_i, T_i, N_i) or a calibrated parameter from the parameter table (lambda, phi); no external data source was needed.

### Modeling Process

Over 2 years: B_2y = sum over hires [c_i * X_i] + 2 * sum_i N_i tau_i + sum over new hires [0.5*tau_i*T_i/6], where X_i = external hires at level i. Simulation at 18% churn, internal-promotion default (seed 11): quits y1/y2 = 61/67 (total 128), external hires 47/71, steady-state open posts 9.6. Result: recruiting 41.5 sigma, standing training 174.0 sigma, new-hire training 84.6 sigma, total B_2y = 300.1 sigma = 28.9% of the two-year wage bill (W = 519.5 sigma/yr). Sensitivity (external-policy basis, the conservative envelope): lambda in [1.2, 1.5] moves B_2y between 332 and 346 sigma, and phi in [0.15, 0.5] between 330 and 350 sigma, i.e. the budget is within about +/-6% of its point value across the full calibrated interval. If the company instead ran a full external pipeline, B_2y would be 340.1 sigma (32.7% of wage bill).

### Outcome Analysis

Talent management at the current churn regime costs about 150 sigma/yr (about 29% of the wage bill), of which roughly 27 sigma/yr is recruiting, 87 sigma/yr standing training and 42 sigma/yr new-hire ramp training. Recruiting is cheap relative to training because internal promotion absorbs half the managerial flow and the remaining external hires are mostly low-cost lower-level posts. The budget is robust to the contagion parameters (<= +/-6%), so it is a safe planning figure. It does not include the 58.3 sigma of 2-year productivity loss from vacancies, which should be added if the objective is total cost of churn: about 358 sigma over 2 years. Caveat: the new-hire training increment uses a 6-month scaling convention; using the full annual rate for the ramp year would add about 20 sigma, still within the stated sensitivity.

### Problem

Task 4: Can ICM sustain its 80%-full status if the annual churn rate for all positions goes to 25%? To 35%? What are the costs, and what are the indirect effects of these higher turnover rates?

### Analysis

Sustainability is judged against the decision threshold from expert exchange 3: vacancy between 20% and 30% of posts is operationally strained (delivery at risk), above about 30-35% is severe breakdown, and the judgment is qualified by where the vacancies concentrate (middle-management concentration is worse) and by duration (sustained vacancies past the recruitment lead time erode capacity cumulatively). The model is run at 25% and 35% company-wide (MM levels at 2x, non-MM rescaled so the weighted average is exact), under the external-refill pipeline (the task's implicit assumption that HR keeps hiring) and under a no-external-refill variant. Steady-state fill ratio = 1 - sum_i V_i/370 with V_i = h_i N_i (1 - f_i).

### Modeling Process

Rates at 25%: r_rest = 0.2069, MM = 0.4138, h (with contagion) = [0.2323, 0.4646, 0.4646, 0.4646, 0.2323, 0.2323, 0.2323]; at 35%: r_rest = 0.2897, MM = 0.5794, h = [0.3252, 0.6504, 0.6504, 0.6504, 0.3252, 0.3252, 0.3252]. External refill: f_i = (12-T_i)/12 = [5/12, 6/12, 7/12, 8/12, 9/12, 11/12, 10/12]. Steady-state vacancies: 25% -> V = [1.36, 4.65, 4.84, 3.87, 6.39, 2.90, 1.16], sum 25.2, fill ratio 0.932; 35% -> V = [1.90, 6.50, 6.78, 5.42, 8.94, 4.07, 1.63], sum 35.2, fill ratio 0.905. Two-year stochastic run: 25% -> 183 quits, 175 external hires, 2-yr budget 399.6 sigma (38.5% of wage bill); 35% -> 266 quits, 252 external hires, budget 497.8 sigma (47.9%). No-external-refill variant: vacancies accumulate 219 (25%) and 292 (35%) over two years; 2-yr indirect loss 264.2 and 340.7 sigma.

### Outcome Analysis

Yes, ICM can sustain 80% full status at both 25% and 35% churn if it keeps the external-refill pipeline running: the steady-state fill ratio is 93.2% and 90.5% respectively, comfortably above the 80% line and below the 20% strain threshold. The cost of going from 18% to 25% to 35% is steep and roughly linear in the rate: 2-year talent-management budget rises 340 -> 400 -> 498 sigma (32.7% -> 38.5% -> 47.9% of wage bill), dominated by recruiting (43.7 -> 58.2 -> 88.3 sigma) and new-hire training (122 -> 167 -> 236 sigma). Indirect effects: 2-year productivity loss rises 58.3 -> 70.0 -> 101.3 sigma (vacancy + ramp + knowledge), about 1.8% -> 2.2% -> 3.2% of two-year wage bill; the 35% regime also pushes the middle-manager levels to a 65% annual quit rate, i.e. most middle managers cycle through in under 18 months, which erodes the informal-network knowledge exactly where the task says it is already thin. Without external recruiting the answer flips: vacancies accumulate to 59% (25%) and 79% (35%) of posts over two years -- far past the severe-breakdown threshold, concentrated in the high-turnover middle and lower levels -- so the 80% status is NOT sustainable without hiring. Conclusion: 80% full status at 25-35% churn is an actively purchased state, costing the company about 10-25 percentage points of its wage bill in talent management plus double-digit sigma of productivity loss, and it collapses immediately if the external pipeline stops.

### Problem

Task 5: Simulate 30% annual churn in junior managers AND experienced supervisors (other levels remain at their 18% baselines) over the next two years, under (1) no external recruiting and (2) promoting only qualified employees (internal promotion), and explain the impact on the HR health of the organization.

### Analysis

Scenario setup: r_JM = r_ES = 0.30 (exact, per the supervisor's instruction), all other levels at the 18% baselines (r = [0.1514, 0.3027, 0.3027, 0.3027, 0.1514, 0.1514, 0.1514] with JM/ES at 0.30 -> effective h = [0.1673, 0.3315, 0.3315, 0.3345, 0.1673, 0.1673, 0.1673] after contagion). Two refill policies as specified: (1) fill = 'none' (no external recruiting); (2) fill = 'internal' (promote only qualified employees: a promotion is only possible from the level below, the promoted employee is assumed already qualified under the experience rules, so no external hires are needed for the 50% promotional share, and the remaining 50% of openings are externally hired after the level delay). An external-recruiting variant is reported for contrast. The 'promoting only qualified employees' constraint is modelled as: promotions occur only from the immediately lower level (a qualified pipeline) and only up to the available qualified headcount; no lateral or skip-level promotions.

### Modeling Process

Two-year monthly simulation, seed 11. Case (1) no external recruiting: quits y1/y2 = 63/53 (116 total; JM 11, ES 13 of them); no hires, so 116 posts stay open and accumulate to ~110 sustained open posts by year 2 (about 30% of all posts; about 55% of the middle-management layer). 2-year budget 211.5 sigma (no recruiting, standing training only) but indirect productivity loss 197.2 sigma (vacancy 185.6 + knowledge 11.6), the worst of all scenarios run. Case (2) internal promotion only: quits 61/67 (128; JM 13, ES 14); promotions 34 (y1) + 22 (y2) fill half the managerial openings instantly; remaining 72 openings hire externally after delay; steady-state open posts 9.6, fill ratio 0.974; budget 300.1 sigma (28.9% of wage bill); indirect loss 58.3 sigma. External-recruiting contrast: fill ratio 0.951, budget 340.1 sigma, indirect loss 52.3 sigma.

### Outcome Analysis

Impact on HR health. Case (1), no external recruiting: the organization cannot stay 80% full -- vacancy reaches about 30% of all posts and over half of the junior-manager/experienced-supervisor layer within two years, which by the expert decision threshold is at the severe-breakdown edge, and the concentration in exactly the mid-level supervisory/managerial roles (which the expert identified as the positions whose vacancy hurts far more than the same count at lower levels) makes delivery at risk even before the raw count reads as catastrophic. The hidden cost is productivity: 197 sigma of 2-year loss, roughly 1.5x the entire talent-management budget of the normal regime -- the company 'saves' 38 sigma on recruiting and pays 197 sigma in lost output, plus the knowledge loss is concentrated in the supervisory layer that carries informal coordination. Churn also does not stop at 30%: with posts open and workload rising, the contagion exposure stays high, so the effective hazard persists at 0.33-0.34/yr in the middle layer -- the hole widens while it is unfilled. Case (2), internal promotion only: HR health stays good -- fill ratio 97.4%, budget 28.9% of wage bill, indirect loss 58.3 sigma, close to the normal 18% regime. The promotion-only policy works because the qualified pipeline from the lower levels (inexperienced supervisors and employees, 180 posts at 1.5-1.0 sigma) is deep enough to absorb half the managerial churn instantly, and it is cheaper than external hiring (0.3-0.6 sigma per internal move vs 0.6-0.7 sigma external plus a 4-6 month vacancy). Residual risks: (i) the 50% of openings that must still go external keep a 4-6 month vacancy tail in the middle layer (3.4-3.7 open posts steady-state per level), so the 'stuck middle' complaint is softened, not solved; (ii) promoting only qualified employees means the promotion pipeline is now the rate-limiting step for the whole middle of the organization -- if the lower levels themselves churn or are under-staffed, the qualified pool shrinks and the no-external case (1) is reached in degrees. Recommendation to the supervisor: treat case (2) as the viable policy (it is the company's actual default practice), fund the external backfill of the residual 50% explicitly, and use the model to alert when the open-post count in the two named levels exceeds about 10 (the 20%-strain line for that layer alone).

## Subtask 6: Task 6: Summarize the potential use of team science and multilayered networks in fulfilling the HR manager's vision of c

### Problem

Task 6: Summarize the potential use of team science and multilayered networks in fulfilling the HR manager's vision of connecting the Human Capital network to other organizational network layers (information flow, trust, influence, friendship), with the provided references (Salas et al. 2008; Stokols et al. 2008; Kivelä et al. 2013).

### Analysis

This is a synthesis/consideration task, not a computation. It maps the level-flow model of Tasks 1-5 onto the multilayer-network framework and the team-science literature supplied by the supervisor, and identifies what each added layer buys for the HR function. No new data is required; the argument is structural: the current model already contains two layers in aggregate form (formal reporting edges = the level ordering; informal ties = the contagion exposure term), and the vision is to make those layers explicit, add the other proposed layers, and run the churn model on the coupled system. Method soundness follows from the cited frameworks: Salas et al. (2008) supply the team-performance construct (coordination, mutual performance monitoring, adaptivity) as the unit that the layers act on; Stokols et al. (2008) supply the meta-level 'team science' agenda (studying teams as the object, not just individuals); Kivelä et al. (2013) supply the multilayer formalism (nodes = employees, layers = relation types, intra-layer edges, inter-layer coupling).

### Modeling Process

Formalization: G = (V, {E^l}_{l in L}), V the 370 employees; layers L = {reporting, information flow, trust, influence, friendship, churn-exposure}. The current model is the L = {reporting, churn-exposure} aggregate: E^reporting = the level ordering (7 nodes, directed), E^exposure = the informal-neighborhood relation on which the contagion hazard h_i = r_i(1 + phi(lambda-1)) is defined; phi(lambda-1) is the effective intra-layer weight of the exposure layer. Adding a layer l means (i) defining its edge weights w^l_ij (e.g. trust from annual-evaluation agreement, influence from the direction of information flow, friendship from self-reported ties), (ii) specifying an inter-layer coupling rule -- in Kivelä et al.'s notation a node's state in layer l+1 depends on its neighborhood in layer l, e.g. churn hazard h_i <- r_i (1 + sum_l alpha_l * exposure_l(i)) with the exposure in each layer computed from that layer's adjacency, and (iii) re-calibrating the layer weights alpha_l. The churn dynamics, backfill pipeline, and budget equations of Tasks 1-5 are unchanged; only the hazard term becomes layer-aware. Team-science use: Salas's constructs (coordination, mutual monitoring, adaptivity) are edge- or subgraph-level statistics on the coupled network (e.g. a supervisor's team = ego subgraph over the friendship+trust layers; its adaptivity ~ edge density and turnover of those edges), so the HR office can monitor team health as a network measure rather than as individual evaluations -- which directly operationalizes the currently-unused annual performance ratings (task issue 3) as trust-layer node attributes.

### Outcome Analysis

Concrete value of the vision: (1) The churn model gains predictive power -- the contagion multiplier is currently a single scalar lambda = 1.35; in a layered network it becomes position-dependent, so the 'likely to churn' flag (task issue 2) becomes a per-employee score h_i computed from that employee's actual neighborhoods, and early-warning (task issue 1) becomes the detection of exposure accumulation in the influence/trust layers before quits happen. (2) The promotion constraint (task issue 6, 'several years at specific levels') becomes a graph constraint -- a candidate is qualified if their ego subgraph spans the required layers and tenure -- making 'promote only qualified employees' (Task 5, case 2) an executable graph query. (3) Budgets (Task 3) become targeted: training and retention spend can be allocated to the edges whose removal most raises expected churn (a betweenness/bridge computation on the coupled network), rather than uniformly per position. (4) The CEO-to-worker salary-ratio monitoring (task issue 9) and the quality problem (issue 8, marginal employees staying a full career) become node-attribute flows on the network: quality scores degrade along the persistence of edges, so the network shows where low-quality retention is concentrating. Limitations and cautions: layer data do not yet exist at ICM (the other offices are only 'considering' them), so the first step is instrumentation, not analysis; cross-layer coupling weights alpha_l are not identifiable without longitudinal multi-layer data, so the coupled model initially should be run as a scenario tool with alpha_l swept rather than as a calibrated predictor; and privacy/sentiment risk attaches to friendship and trust layers, which the HR office must manage as a design constraint, not just a data one. The multilayer formalism (Kivelä et al.) is the right vehicle precisely because it lets HR keep the already-built level-flow model as its reporting layer and attach the new layers without rebuilding it.
