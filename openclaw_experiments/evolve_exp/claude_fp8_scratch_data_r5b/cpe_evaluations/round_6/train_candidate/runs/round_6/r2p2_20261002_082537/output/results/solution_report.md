## Subtask 1: Build a model that captures the flow of play as points occur, applied to the Alcaraz-Djokovic 2023 Wimbledon final (matc

### Problem

Build a model that captures the flow of play as points occur, applied to the Alcaraz-Djokovic 2023 Wimbledon final (match_id 2023-wimbledon-1701) and the other 30 featured matches. The model must identify which player is performing better at any point in the match and how much better, and produce a visualization of the match flow. The serve advantage (server wins most points) must be factored in.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) A point-by-point log is a complete record of who won each point, the server, serve number, and game/set outcomes; the four enrichment fields (speed_mph, serve_width, serve_depth, return_depth) are shot-level annotations that are only logged for a subset of points, so they are excluded from the momentum computation and used descriptively only. (2) The probability that the server wins a point is high and differs by serve number; this serve advantage is a structural effect, not 'momentum', and must be removed before measuring who is playing better. (3) A player's true performance advantage is the deviation of point outcomes from this serve-normalized expectation, accumulated over the match with memory. Method: a serve-normalized residual at each point, combined with game-level scoreline events (breaks of serve and holds under pressure), passed through an exponential weighted moving average to give a smooth, signed momentum trace. This is sound because it isolates deviation-from-expectation (the only thing that can mean 'playing better') and anchors momentum to the scoreline rather than to raw point streaks, which the expert interaction showed are mostly chance.

### Modeling Process

Let the match be points i=1..N. For point i let s_i in {1,2} be the server, n_i in {1,2} the serve number, and y_i in {1,2} the point victor. Baseline server-win probabilities estimated from the full dataset: p1=0.754 (first serve), p2=0.530 (second serve). Player-1 win probability for the point: Pr(y_i=1) = p_n if s_i=1 else 1-p_n. Serve-normalized point residual for player 1: r_i = 1[y_i=1] - Pr(y_i=1) (in [-1,1], mean ~0, positive favors player 1). Game-level events (only at game-ending points): a break of serve B_i = +1 if player 1 wins a game on player 2's serve, -1 if player 1's serve is broken, 0 otherwise; a break-point save S_i = +1 if player 1 saves a break point on his own serve, -1 if he concedes one, 0 otherwise. Combined per-point signal: z_i = r_i + 0.5*B_i + 0.5*S_i. Momentum trace: M_t = sum_k lam^(t-k) z_k with decay lam=0.15 (equivalently M_t = M_{t-1} + lam*(z_t - M_{t-1}), M_0=0). Interpretation: the player with the larger signed momentum is performing better; M_t>0 means player 1 is better, M_t<0 means player 2; |M_t| is the magnitude of the advantage in 'points-ahead' units (one unit ~ the serve-normalized value of a sustained run). The visualization is the time series M_t vs point number, split by set, with the better player labelled by the sign; a CSV of (match, set, game, point, M_t, better_player, |M_t|) is written for all 31 matches. Apply to 1701: M_t starts negative in set 1 (Djokovic dominates 6-1, two breaks), turns positive and peaks in set 3 (Alcaraz 6-1, three breaks), dips negative in set 4 (Djokovic 6-3, two breaks), and is positive through most of set 5 (Alcaraz 6-4). The sign of M_t at each set end matches the set winner in 4 of 5 sets, and across all 31 matches the sign of M_t at set end predicts the set winner in 105 of 117 sets = 89.7% (chance 50%).

### Outcome Analysis

Results: The momentum trace reproduces the narrative of the final — Djokovic's set-1 edge, Alcaraz's tiebreak-earned set 2 and routine set 3, Djokovic's set-4 comeback, and Alcaraz's set-5 close. The metric's set-level accuracy of 89.7% (105/117) shows it reliably identifies the better performer, far above the 50% chance baseline; the correlation between |M_t| at set end and the set score margin is 0.24 (weak, expected because M_t is serve-normalized and a 6-4 and a 7-6 set both end near zero momentum). Limitations and biases: (1) The enrichment fields are availability-biased (logged in stretches by match, coverage from 0 to ~0.99), so any serve-speed or shot-placement signal computed from them would be biased toward the logged stretches and was therefore excluded. (2) The decay lam=0.15 is a chosen memory constant; a shorter lam makes the trace noisier and a longer lam over-smooths the swings, so the 'how much better' magnitude depends on this tuning. (3) Because M_t is anchored to breaks and holds, it under-weights a player who is dominating within his own service games (holding easily) and over-weights breaks, which is the deliberate design choice to separate real scoreline shifts from chance point streaks. (4) The final point of a match has M_t near 0 (the winner just closed it), so the trace is a match-flow instrument, not a match-winner predictor.

## Subtask 2: A tennis coach postulates that swings in play and runs of success are random, not the effect of a real 'momentum' force.

### Problem

A tennis coach postulates that swings in play and runs of success are random, not the effect of a real 'momentum' force. Assess this claim using the model/metric.

### Analysis

Assumption: If momentum were a real persistent force, the observed point and game structure would deviate from what a model of independent points-with-the-observed-per-point win probabilities produces. Method: a permutation/resampling null that preserves the per-point win probability (i.e. the serve advantage and the overall skill gap) but shuffles which specific points are won, then compares the observed run structure and momentum persistence against that null at both the point level and the game (break) level. This isolates 'clustering beyond the per-point probabilities' as the signature of a real momentum effect.

### Modeling Process

Point-level test: for each match, form the player-1 point-win sequence. Compute (a) the distribution of consecutive same-outcome run lengths, (b) the longest run, (c) the autocorrelation of the momentum trace M_t at lag 5. Null: resample each point's outcome independently from its observed win probability Pr(y_i=1) (which already encodes serve and skill), 200 times per match, and recompute the same statistics. Game-level test: count breaks of serve per set; compare the observed fraction of sets with >=2 and >=3 breaks against a binomial null where each game is a break with the empirical break probability (0.159) independently.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: Point level — observed mean run length 2.13, longest run 7.9, fraction of runs >=6 of 0.029, momentum autocorrelation at lag 5 of 0.433. The shuffled null gives mean run 2.15, longest run 8.7, fraction of runs >=6 of 0.046, autocorrelation 0.387. The observed runs are no longer, no more frequent, and no more persistent than the null (the run fraction is actually lower than chance). Game level — observed fraction of sets with >=2 breaks 0.43 and >=3 breaks 0.19, versus the binomial null 0.49 and 0.21; breaks do not cluster beyond the independent-game model. Conclusion: the coach's randomness claim is SUPPORTED at both the point and the game level. There is no statistical evidence of a momentum force that makes runs of points or breaks of serve more likely than the per-point/per-game probabilities already predict. The 'swings' perceived in a match are consistent with the natural variation of a high-variance process (points are close to 50/50 after removing the serve advantage, so streaks appear often by chance). Bias caveat: the null preserves the per-point win probabilities, so it cannot rule out a momentum effect that would change those probabilities themselves (e.g. fatigue or pressure altering a player's true point win rate); it only rules out a momentum effect that clusters outcomes at fixed per-point probabilities. The game-level test is the stronger evidence because it is where the expert said a real shift would show.

## Subtask 3: Develop a model that predicts when the flow of play is about to shift from one player to the other, using data from at l

### Problem

Develop a model that predicts when the flow of play is about to shift from one player to the other, using data from at least one match. Identify the factors most related (if any). Advise a player going into a new match against a different player, and test the model on other matches. Assess generalizability to other matches, tournaments, court surfaces, and other sports such as table tennis.

### Analysis

Assumption: If flow reversals are predictable, then the state of the match just before a reversal (a break of serve, or a player losing a serve game under pressure) should carry information beyond the current per-game break probability. Method: a logistic-regression classifier predicting whether the next game is a break of serve (the concrete, observable flow shift), from recent-match-state features: breaks in the last 3 games, unforced errors in the last 3 games, whether the previous game was a break, games elapsed in the match (a fatigue/late-match proxy), average rally length, and whether the game currently faces a break point. Trained on the early-round matches (13xx/14xx) and tested on the later rounds (15xx/16xx/17xx) to check generalization.

### Modeling Process

Features per game g (computed over the match to that point, using only past games to avoid leakage): x1 = number of breaks in the previous 3 games, x2 = sum of server unforced errors in the previous 3 games, x3 = 1 if the previous game was a break, x4 = games elapsed in the match, x5 = average rally length of the current game's points so far, x6 = 1 if a break point is currently faced. Target y_g = 1 if game g is a break of serve. Model: y_g ~ logistic(W0 + W1 x1 + ... + W6 x6), fit by regularized gradient descent. Decision rule for a 'reversal warning': flag when predicted P(break) is elevated relative to the base rate 0.159, consistent with the expert threshold that a single break is normal but the model should look for the state in which breaks become more likely. In-sample fit on the full set: AUC 0.497 (chance 0.5), accuracy 0.839 (the base rate is 0.159 so always-predicting-no-break gives 0.841). Cross-match (train early / test late): AUC 0.465.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: The in-sample AUC of 0.497 and the out-of-sample AUC of 0.465 are both at or below chance (0.5). The model cannot predict when the flow of play will shift; no combination of the tested recent-state features carries predictive power for the next break. The largest-magnitude coefficients were on 'break point currently faced' and 'games elapsed' (a late-match effect), but these do not lift AUC above 0.5. This is consistent with the randomness finding in the previous subtask: because breaks are already near-chance given the per-game probability, there is no reliable leading indicator among these features. Factors that seem most related (descriptively, not predictively): being down on a break point, and later in the match (fatigue) — but neither is actionable as a predictor. Advice to a player going into a new match against a different player: (1) do not expect to 'read' a momentum swing from point streaks or even single breaks — the data says these are not reliable reversal signals; (2) the only durable lever the data supports is the serve advantage (first-serve win rate 0.754 vs 0.530 on the second), so preparation should focus on holding serve under break-point pressure (break-point save) and converting the breaks you create, since those are the scoreline events that actually move the match; (3) treat any apparent swing as likely chance and avoid reactive over-correction. Generalizability: within this Wimbledon 2023 men's dataset the model does not generalize (test AUC below chance). It is not expected to generalize to women's matches, other tournaments, or other court surfaces without re-estimation, because the per-point and per-game win probabilities (and hence the base break rate) differ by surface and by player pool; the *method* (serve-normalized residual + game-level events + a prediction baseline at the base rate) would transfer, but the specific coefficients would not. For other sports such as table tennis, the structure differs (a point changes the server or the score in a different way, and rallies are far shorter), so the serve-advantage normalization and the break concept would need to be re-derived; the general lesson — that apparent 'streaks' in a near-50/50 per-unit process are mostly chance — should carry over, but each sport needs its own baseline estimated from its own data.

## Subtask 4: Produce a memo (summarized in this container) with advice for coaches on the role of 'momentum' and how to prepare playe

### Problem

Produce a memo (summarized in this container) with advice for coaches on the role of 'momentum' and how to prepare players to respond to events that impact the flow of play during a tennis match.

### Analysis

Goal: translate the quantitative findings into coaching advice. The memo is grounded in the three results above: (1) a serve-normalized momentum metric that tracks who is better (89.7% set-level accuracy), (2) the demonstration that point and game streaks are consistent with randomness, and (3) the failure of any tested feature to predict flow reversals.

### Modeling Process

No new model; this consolidates the decision rules. The operating advice is derived from the model's validated components: use the momentum trace (serve-normalized, anchored to breaks and holds under pressure) to read the current balance of play; treat the threshold for a 'real' shift as two breaks in a set or a 3-4 game run that includes a break (a single break or any point streak is classified as noise); and recognize that no observed indicator reliably forecasts the next shift.

### Outcome Analysis

Memo for coaches: (1) On the role of momentum — the data does not support a self-reinforcing 'momentum force'. Streaks of points and even runs of games are, in aggregate, no more frequent than a model of independent points at the observed win rates would produce. A player who 'feels the momentum' is usually experiencing the natural clustering that any near-coin-flip process generates. Coaches should not plan around the idea that a hot player will keep winning points; the realistic expectation is that any run is temporary. (2) What is real — the serve advantage is large and is the one structural asymmetry to manage: holding first serve (winning 75% of those points) and not losing the second serve (only 53%) are where matches are actually won and lost. Break points are converted only about a third of the time, so the side under pressure (the server facing a break point) is favored to hold. (3) How to prepare players to respond to flow events — because reversals are not predictable from point streaks or single breaks, preparation should be about consistency under the specific high-leverage events the data shows matter: (a) rehearse break-point saves and holds under pressure, since a real swing is anchored to these; (b) when down in a set or facing a break point, treat it as a near-fifty-fifty situation (the data shows the receiver rarely wins the point), not as a sign the opponent is 'on a roll'; (c) avoid over-reacting to a short run by either player — the model says the run is most likely chance, so the player should reset to a fixed pre-point routine rather than changing strategy in response to a streak. (4) Limitations of this advice — it is drawn from men's singles at Wimbledon 2023 on grass; the base rates (serve advantage, break conversion) will shift on clay, hard court, or in women's matches, and the coaching emphasis should be re-estimated on the relevant surface. The qualitative lesson — streaks are mostly noise, serve is the real asset, and reversals are not forecastable — is the robust part.
