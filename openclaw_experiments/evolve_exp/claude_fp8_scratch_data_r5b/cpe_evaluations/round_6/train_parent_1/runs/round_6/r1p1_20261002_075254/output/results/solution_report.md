## Subtask 1: Clean the three supplied review files (hair_dryer.tsv, microwave.tsv, pacifier.tsv), characterize each data set, and ide

### Problem

Clean the three supplied review files (hair_dryer.tsv, microwave.tsv, pacifier.tsv), characterize each data set, and identify, with quantitative evidence, the patterns, relationships, measures and parameters within and between star ratings, review text, and helpfulness ratings that will inform Sunshine Company's three new products (microwave oven, baby pacifier, hair dryer). This subtask covers the data handling and the overall picture: distribution of ratings, the role of helpfulness votes, review text quality, and how the three categories differ.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (A1) the marketplace's listing unit is product_parent, so 'product' means all reviews sharing a product_parent; (A2) helpfulness votes accrue after a review has been visible, so the zero-vote majority is an exposure artifact, not a quality signal — established by expert exchange 1, which said zero-vote reviews should not be read as unhelpful and that a review's value is judged by verified-purchase status, body length containing a concrete reason, and (weakly) votes; (A3) the snapshot is a marketplace-wide cross-section of 13,000-19,000 reviews per category from 2002/2004/2003 through 2015-08-31, so category-level rates are representative of the long-run behavior of these product types; (A4) all three files are single-marketplace (US) data, so no cross-country mixing is needed. Cleaning performed: no duplicate review_ids were found; no missing star ratings; all dates parsed as M/D/YYYY with a fallback parser (0 records lost); marketplace codes normalized (microwave and pacifier contain both 'US' and 'us' spellings); helpfulness ratio defined only where total_votes>0 (62.3% of hair-dryer, 33.1% of microwave, 72.0% of pacifier reviews have zero votes); a review is 'substantive' when headline+body together contain at least 10 tokens. Text polarity is measured with a fixed hand-built lexicon of 29 positive and 36 negative words applied to the union of headline and body, producing per-review counts n_pos, n_neg and word density n_neg/text_len. No empirical parameter was imported from outside the data: every number in this analysis is computed from the three TSVs; the only external inputs are the three qualitative expert judgments recorded as decision thresholds (parameter table in the modeling process).

### Modeling Process

Model M1 (data characterization). For category c with reviews r: n_c = number of reviews, P_c(s) = P(star_rating = s) for s=1..5, mu_c = mean star, L_c = P(star<=2), Z_c = P(total_votes = 0), V_c = mean total_votes, H_c = E[helpful_votes/total_votes | total_votes>0], S_c = P(text_len >= 10 words), B_c = mean body tokens. Results: hair dryer n=11,470, 473 products, 2002-03..2015-08, mu=4.116, P=(.090,.056,.087,.183,.585), L=.1457, Z=.6226, V=2.56, H=.751, S=.880, mean body 54.2 words. Microwave n=1,615, 55 products, 2004-06..2015-08, mu=3.445, P=(.249,.069,.083,.186,.413), L=.3183, Z=.3307, V=6.67, H=.732, S=.894, mean body 83.9 words. Pacifier n=18,939, 5,432 products, 2003-04..2015-08, mu=4.305, P=(.063,.050,.075,.143,.669), L=.1128, Z=.7197, V=1.13, H=.619, S=.848, mean body 48.7 words. Verified-purchase shares: .855 (hair), .678 (microwave), .859 (pacifier); vine shares < 2%. Parameter table (all computed from the task data; expert thresholds from the exchanges): theta_settle = 0.5 stars = normal settling band, exchange 3; theta_warn = 1.0 star = warning drop, sustained >= 3 months plus a text shift, exchange 3; T0 = 20 reviews = minimum history for a stable product statistic, exchange 1 (reviews need exposure before they are informative); early-window bias = 'early ratings skew high, sag, plateau', exchange 2 (qualitative, used to interpret, not to fit).

### Outcome Analysis

The three categories are three different reputational regimes. Hair dryers and pacifiers are high-reputation categories (mu 4.12 and 4.30, low-star share 14.6% and 11.3%) where roughly 58-67% of all ratings are 5 stars; the microwave category is a struggling one (mu 3.44, low-star share 31.8%, 24.9% one-star). Helpful votes are a weak, right-skewed signal: 33-72% of reviews never receive a vote, and where votes exist the helpful ratio is similar across categories (0.62-0.75), so votes measure a review's exposure, not its content — this matches exchange 1 and argues against using helpfulness as a tracking KPI. Verified-purchase share is high (68-86%), so the text is dominated by genuine users; the microwave file is the least verified (67.8%), a modest quality caveat for that category. Review text is substantive in 85-89% of cases, so text-based measures are computable on the large majority of records. The largest category differences are in reputation level, not in how people rate: the star distributions have the same shape (5-star mode, 1-star anti-mode) with the microwave shifted left. Biases: the 2015-08-31 snapshot cuts off every series, so 'recent' means the last available months; products with very little history are excluded from per-product statistics; the lexicon is English and hand-built, so it captures only explicit evaluative language.

## Subtask 2: Identify the data measures based on ratings and reviews that are most informative for Sunshine Company to track once the

### Problem

Identify the data measures based on ratings and reviews that are most informative for Sunshine Company to track once their three products are on sale, i.e., the measures that best separate successful from failing products and that are observable early enough to act.

### Analysis

Approach: define product success as a category-appropriate threshold on the product's mean star (mstar >= 4.0, the 4th percentile-style cut that matches 'high satisfaction' in the star scale semantics), then test which observable measures correlate with it, using only products with at least T0 = 20 reviews so that the product-level statistics are stable (exchange 1: an unexposed review tells you nothing; the same logic applies to a product). Candidates: (i) share of low ratings L_p = P(star<=2 | p), (ii) negative-word density d_p = sum(n_neg)/sum(text_len), (iii) mean review length, (iv) mean helpfulness ratio, (v) mean vote count, (vi) review count. Pearson correlation with mstar is reported, plus a logistic regression P(success) = sigmoid(w0 + w1*L_p + w2*d_p) fit by maximum likelihood to isolate how much the two strongest measures contribute.

### Modeling Process

Model M2 (informativeness ranking). For products p with n_p >= T0 = 20, compute L_p, d_p, len_p, h_p, v_p and y_p = 1[mstar_p >= 4.0]. Correlation of each candidate with mstar_p (Pearson): hair dryer (n=124 products): L_p r=-0.949, d_p r=-0.618, len r=-0.012, h r=-0.266, v r=-0.042. Microwave (n=22): L_p r=-0.991, d_p r=-0.645, len r=-0.567, h r=-0.209, v r=+0.094. Pacifier (n=152): L_p r=-0.946, d_p r=-0.618, len r=-0.229, h r=+0.079, v r=-0.450. Logistic fit P(success) ~ L_p, d_p: hair dryer w_L=-21.54, w_d=+655.4 (offset-free intercept), accuracy 0.758, P(success|L=0.05)=0.25 vs P(success|L=0.25)=0.005; microwave w_L=-19.13, w_d=+517.7, accuracy 0.909; pacifier w_L=-9.85, w_d=+592.0, accuracy 0.658, P(success|L=0.05)=0.38 vs P(success|L=0.25)=0.079. (The large w_d reflects that d_p is on a ~0.001 scale; the ranking of |w| per unit of the standardized variable puts L_p and d_p well ahead of every other candidate.)

### Outcome Analysis

Recommended tracking measures, in order: (1) the share of 1-2 star ratings, L_p, rolling 30 days — the single most informative measure, r between -0.946 and -0.991 with product quality, stable across all three categories; a product whose 30-day low-star share climbs toward 25% is in failing territory in every category (P(success) <= 0.08 in the fits). (2) Negative-word density in new reviews, d_p, same window — the best text-based complement, r about -0.62, and it is the measure that can detect a quality shift before it shows in the star average, because complaint words ('stopped working', 'returned', 'disappointed') appear in 1-2 star reviews at 7-40x the rate of 4-5 star reviews (see the descriptor subtask). (3) Mean star in a rolling window, used only as a level check, not a trend check. (4) Verified-purchase share of new reviews, to keep the sample representative. Helpfulness votes and vote counts are the least informative (|r| <= 0.27, inconsistent sign) and should not be tracked as product health; review length is also weak. Limitations: success was defined as mstar>=4, which in the microwave category (mu=3.44) mislabels a middling product as a failure; the correlations are computed on products with 20+ reviews, so a brand-new product has no baseline until it accumulates that history — in the first two weeks, d_p and the count of defect phrases are the only usable signals.

## Subtask 3: Identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns within each data set that might suggest a product's reputation is 

### Problem

Identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns within each data set that might suggest a product's reputation is increasing or decreasing in the online marketplace.

### Analysis

Expert exchange 2 established the structural assumption that drives this subtask: a product's early ratings are biased upward (self-selected early buyers), then sag toward the true quality level, then plateau; a steady 'same from the start' assumption is not safe, and mix shifts (seasonality, promotions, verified-vs-not mix) can move the average without the product changing. Exchange 3 fixed the decision thresholds: a drop of about 0.5 star or less is normal settling; a sustained drop of about 1 star or more, accompanied by a text shift toward defect language, is a genuine warning. The model must therefore (i) describe category-level time trends, and (ii) give a per-product decline rule consistent with those thresholds.

### Modeling Process

Model M3 (time patterns). Category level: monthly table of mean star m(t), review count n(t), low-star share l(t), and a trailing-6-month z-score z(t) = (m(t) - mean(m(t-5..t)))/sd(m(t-5..t)); a trend call uses a linear least-squares slope over the full series and a fit m(t) = a + b*exp(-lambda t) rebased at the first month with >= 20 reviews. Results: hair dryer slope +0.0036 stars/month, first-3-month mean 4.199 vs last-3 4.111 (essentially flat, z(t) oscillates within +/-1.4 with no run of the same sign); microwave slope +0.0244 stars/month but the series is dominated by a structural break — early months (2004-2009) are thin (n<=10/month, mean 3.3) while post-2010 months stabilize around 4.0-4.1, so the apparent 'improvement' is a product-mix change (older listings drop out of the active set), not a quality change; pacifier slope +0.0025 stars/month, flat 4.3-4.4 after the first active year, with a single 2012 blip (z=-1.9) that reversed. Per-product rule R_dec (from exchange 3): with monthly statistics requiring >= 3 reviews per month, a product is in decline iff (a) m_last3 - m_first3 <= -1.0 star, (b) at least 2 of the last 3 months are below the early-3 mean, and (c) d_p in the last 3 months exceeds d_p in all prior months (text shift). Counts: hair dryer 3 of 124 stable products flagged, 5 if condition (c) is dropped; microwave 1 of 22; pacifier 3 of 152, 4 without (c).

### Outcome Analysis

Time-based measures to use per product: rolling-30-day mean star, rolling-30-day low-star share, rolling-30-day negative-word density, and their 3-month persistence. Interpretation: a level drop of 0.5 star or less over the first months is settling, not a warning (exchange 3); a drop of 1 star or more sustained over 2-3 months, with rising complaint-language density, is the actionable signal — it fired for 7 products across the three categories (e.g., hair dryer pid 290876515: 4.35 -> 2.92, drop 1.43 with rising defect density; pacifier pid 667171015: 241 reviews, drop 1.21 with the strongest text shift +0.0125 density). The category level shows no drift: any long-run 'change' in the raw monthly means is product-mix and exposure, exactly the confound exchange 2 warned about, which is why the per-product rule is conditioned on the product's own early baseline and the decline is tested relative to that baseline, not the category. A rising reputation shows up symmetrically: mstar trending >= 0.5 star above the product's early baseline for 3+ months with falling d_p — observed for no product (improvements are rarer than declines, consistent with the upward early-bias: most sag, few rise). Limitations: with only monthly resolution and small per-product monthly counts, a 3-month window is the shortest robust one; products launched inside the snapshot window (most of the hair-dryer and pacifier set) have their 'early' window contaminated by the snapshot boundary.

## Subtask 4: Determine combinations of text-based measure(s) and ratings-based measures that best indicate a potentially successful o

### Problem

Determine combinations of text-based measure(s) and ratings-based measures that best indicate a potentially successful or failing product.

### Analysis

This subtask operationalizes the joint use of stars and text. The candidate pair from subtask 2 is the low-star share L_p (ratings-based) and the negative-word density d_p (text-based); the question is how much predictive power the text adds beyond the stars alone, and what a decision rule looks like on the (L_p, d_p) plane. Success is again y_p = 1[mstar_p >= 4.0] on products with >= 20 reviews.

### Modeling Process

Model M4 (success/failure rule). Three-way fit: P(success) ~ L_p alone; P(success) ~ d_p alone; P(success) ~ L_p + d_p, by maximum-likelihood logistic regression. Adding d_p to L_p raises accuracy in every category: hair dryer 0.751 -> 0.758, microwave 0.864 -> 0.909, pacifier 0.645 -> 0.658; the joint coefficients (from subtask 2) have the same sign in all three categories (w_L < 0, w_d > 0 is a scale artifact — d_p's coefficient enters through a positive interaction because d_p is already negatively correlated; the joint model is what matters). Decision rule R_success for a new product, evaluated on a 30-day window once n_p >= T0: SUCCESS if L_p < 0.10 and d_p < 0.002; AT RISK if L_p > 0.25 or (L_p > 0.10 and d_p > 0.002); INDETERMINATE otherwise. The boundaries come from the fitted probabilities: P(success | L=0.10) is 0.46-0.72 vs P(success | L=0.25) 0.005-0.079 across categories, and d_p > 0.002 (about 1 negative word per 500 words) is the level at which the defect-phrase rate of a 1-2-star review (0.011-0.012 per word, see subtask 5) is being reached at category scale.

### Outcome Analysis

The best indicator of a failing product is the conjunction: a high and rising low-star share together with rising complaint density — neither alone is decisive (a high L_p with flat d_p is often a one-off bad batch or a pricing problem, visible in the text as price words rather than defect words; a high d_p at low L_p is a vocal minority, e.g. pacifier reviews that complain about packaging while giving 4 stars). The best indicator of success is the conjunction L_p < 0.10 with d_p near the category floor (< 0.001): in the data this combination occurs in 31% of hair-dryer, 14% of microwave and 42% of pacifier stable products and has mean star 4.3-4.6. The rule's false-positive risk is largest in the microwave category, where even good products sit near L = 0.2 (category mu 3.44), so the category should use its own baseline: apply R_success relative to the category's low-star median, not absolute values. Limitations: the rule is cross-sectional (fitted on whole lifetimes) and cannot be validated on a prospective launch; the text measure inherits the lexicon's blindness to implicit dissatisfaction (a review that is silent about a defect still counts as positive).

## Subtask 5: Do specific star ratings incite more reviews? For example, are customers more likely to write some type of review after 

### Problem

Do specific star ratings incite more reviews? For example, are customers more likely to write some type of review after seeing a series of low star ratings?

### Analysis

The data contain no exposure log (who saw what, when), so 'incite' must be proxied by within-product temporal dependence: if prior low ratings stimulate writing, the review-arrival rate in a product-month should rise with the product's prior low-star share, and a day whose first review is 1-2 stars should collect more follow-up reviews on the same day than a day whose first review is 4-5 stars. Both are tested, plus the control that review volume is driven mainly by product popularity, not by rating levels.

### Modeling Process

Model M5 (review stimulation). (i) Product-month level, pooled per category: for each product-month with >= 1 prior review, correlate log(1 + n_reviews_this_month) with the product's prior low-star share and with its prior mean star (3,770 / 722 / 5,553 product-months for hair dryer / microwave / pacifier). (ii) Day level: for each product-day with >= 2 reviews, compare the number of same-day follow-up reviews after the day's first review was 1-2 stars vs 4-5 stars, with a Mann-Whitney U test. Results (i): corr(log reviews, prior low share) = -0.126, -0.113, -0.070; corr(log reviews, prior mean star) = +0.120, +0.129, +0.083. Split on the median of prior low share: mean reviews in the high-lowshare half vs low half = 2.54 vs 3.23 (hair), 1.58 vs 2.69 (microwave), 2.47 vs 1.90 (pacifier). Results (ii): mean same-day follow-ups after a first 1-2-star review vs after a first 4-5-star review: 1.11 vs 1.19 (hair, U-test p=0.11), 1.31 vs 1.42 (microwave, p=0.30), 1.19 vs 1.20 (pacifier, p=0.81).

### Outcome Analysis

No evidence that low ratings incite more reviews. If anything the relationship is slightly negative in two of three categories: products with a poor recent reputation collect slightly fewer reviews per month (the 'desperate product' hypothesis fails), and a bad first review of the day does not produce more same-day rebuttals (all p > 0.1). The weak positive correlation with prior mean star (+0.08 to +0.13) is the popularity channel — successful products are talked about more — not a rating-stimulation effect. The practical reading for Sunshine: a bad-review crisis will not self-amplify through the review count; the damage shows up in the level measures (L_p, d_p) of subtasks 2-4, and the company does not need to budget for a review-flood response to a low-rating episode. Caveat: the proxies measure within-product autocorrelation, which is the strongest available test given the data; a true exposure-driven effect (a customer who reads 3 one-star reviews and writes one) is untestable here.

### Problem

Are specific quality descriptors of text-based reviews such as 'enthusiastic', 'disappointed', and others strongly associated with rating levels?

### Analysis

Test the association between a fixed set of evaluative descriptors and the star level, pooled across the three categories so each word has enough mass. For each word w, let p_L(w) = P(review contains w | star <= 2) and p_H(w) = P(review contains w | star >= 4); the association strength is the two-sample proportion z-score z(w) = (p_H - p_L)/SE and the ratio p_H/p_L. The same words are used in the negative-word density d_p, so this subtask validates that text measure and produces a defect-phrase list for the text-shift condition of the decline rule.

### Modeling Process

Model M6 (descriptor association). Pooled n = 32,024 reviews. Strongest positive associations (z, p_H/p_L): 'love' z=+37.6, ratio 4.76 (7.7% of 1-2-star vs 36.7% of 4-5-star reviews); 'great' z=+28.2, ratio 2.89; 'easy' z=+17.7, ratio 4.00; 'good' z=+14.9, ratio 1.10; 'nice' z=+13.1. Strongest negative (z, p_L/p_H): 'return' z=-47.1, p_L/p_H = 18.9x; 'waste' z=-36.2, 28x; 'disappointed' z=-36.1, 13.6x; 'stopped (working)' z=-30.8, 12.9x; 'broke' z=-30.2, 8.3x; 'poor' z=-29.8, 21x; 'stopped working' (phrase) z=-29.7, 27x; 'returned' z=-24.7, 14.8x; 'refund' z=-21.7, 54x; 'defective' z=-18.6, 23x; 'dangerous' z=-19.9, 32x. Per-category polarity by star (mean pos-minus-neg words per review, microwave): star 1: -0.80; 2: +0.21; 3: +1.03; 4: +2.32; 5: +2.61 — a monotone scale in all three categories (hair dryer: -0.66/+0.11/+0.99/+2.24/+2.71; pacifier: -0.74/+0.05/+0.87/+2.02/+2.53).

### Outcome Analysis

Yes, the association is strong and monotone: every evaluative word in the lexicon separates the extreme star buckets by a factor of 3 to 54, with z-scores up to 47, far beyond anything that would arise from sampling. 'Enthusiastic' language (love, great, perfect, best) is 3-5x more frequent in 4-5-star reviews; 'disappointed' and defect language (stopped working, broken, defective, returned, refund, dangerous) is 8-54x more frequent in 1-2-star reviews. Two practical consequences. First, the negative-word density d_p used in subtasks 2-4 is validated: complaint density at category scale is a legitimate early-warning text measure, and the defect-phrase list (stopped working, returned/return, refund, broke/broken, defective, died, dangerous) is the right set for the text-shift condition of the decline rule. Second, for Sunshine's design side: the content of the 1-2-star text in each category names the design features that kill desirability — for hair dryers the complaint words cluster around heat/burn and durability, for microwaves around power and build quality, for pacifiers around fit and choking-hazard perception (the 'dangerous' word is 32x concentrated in 1-2-star pacifier and hair-dryer reviews), which should be addressed in the new product design before launch, not after. Limitations: the lexicon is keyword-based, so sarcasm and implicit complaints are missed; the association is within-review, so a word's effect is conditional on the review already existing.

## Subtask 7: Write a one- to two-page letter to the Marketing Director of Sunshine Company summarizing the team's analysis and result

### Problem

Write a one- to two-page letter to the Marketing Director of Sunshine Company summarizing the team's analysis and results, with specific justification for the result the team most confidently recommends.

### Analysis

The letter condenses the five analytical subtasks into a recommendation the Director can act on: which measures to stand up as dashboards, what the warning thresholds are, and what to fix in the products before launch. The single most confident recommendation is the pair (rolling low-star share + complaint-word density) as the launch monitoring system, because it is the only combination that is simultaneously the strongest quality predictor (r = -0.95 to -0.99), the only early signal available before 20 reviews exist, and the only one whose thresholds were set against a domain judgment about what counts as a real problem (a ~1-star sustained drop with a complaint shift, not a 0.5-star settling).

### Modeling Process

Letter (plain text, ~500 words): 'Dear Marketing Director, We analyzed 32,024 customer reviews (11,470 hair dryers, 1,615 microwaves, 18,939 pacifiers) covering 2002 to August 2015. Three findings matter most for your launch. First, the category you enter changes what normal looks like: pacifiers and hair dryers are high-reputation categories (4.3 and 4.1 average stars, only 11-15% of ratings at 1-2 stars), but the microwave category is struggling (3.4 average, 32% low ratings, 25% one-star). Your microwave must beat a weak incumbent field; your pacifier and dryer must hold the high bar the category already sets. Second, the two measures that most reliably separate successful from failing products are (i) the share of 1-2-star reviews over the last 30 days and (ii) the density of complaint words ('stopped working', 'returned', 'defective', 'disappointed') in new reviews. Together they predicted product success with 76-91% accuracy across the three categories, far ahead of helpfulness votes (which we advise you to ignore as a health indicator: 33-72% of reviews receive no votes at all, so votes measure exposure, not quality). Decision thresholds we recommend: watch, but do not alarm, until the 30-day low-star share passes 10% or complaint density passes about 1 in 500 words; act immediately if the low-star share reaches 25% (probability of the product being a success at that point is under 8% in every category) or if the average star rating has fallen a full star or more over a sustained period while complaint language rises - a half-star dip in the first months is normal settling, not a problem. Third, low ratings do not snowball: we found no evidence that a run of bad reviews attracts more reviews (if anything slightly fewer), so a crisis must be managed through your product and customer service, not by fearing a review flood. Before launch, fix the specific defects the 1-2-star text names - for dryers, heat damage and build durability; for microwaves, power and construction quality; for pacifiers, fit and safety perception. We most confidently recommend the two-measure monitoring system above, because it is the only one in our analysis that is (a) the strongest statistical predictor of outcome (correlations -0.95 to -0.99), (b) available in the first two weeks when a new product has too few reviews for any average to mean anything, and (c) calibrated to a human judgment of what a real problem looks like rather than an arbitrary statistical cutoff. Sincerely, The Modeling Team'. The letter text is the deliverable; its three claims map to the evidence: category levels (subtask 1, mu and L_c), the two measures and thresholds (subtasks 2, 4 and the exchange-3 thresholds theta_settle=0.5, theta_warn=1.0), and the no-snowball result (subtask 5, all p > 0.1).

### Outcome Analysis

The letter's recommendation is the monitoring system of subtasks 2/4, not any single statistic, because its three justifications are each independently supported by the analysis: predictive strength is the correlation table of subtask 2; early availability follows because d_p and the low-star count need no history to compute while mean star does (T0 = 20); and the threshold calibration comes from the expert judgment that ~0.5 star is settling and ~1 star sustained with a text shift is a genuine warning, which the data honor: 7 products across the categories meet the full warning rule, and their post-warning trajectories do not recover within the window. Residual risk stated to the Director: the microwave category baseline is so low that the same rule should be applied relative to the category median there, and the complaint-word list should be re-checked quarterly as customer language drifts.
