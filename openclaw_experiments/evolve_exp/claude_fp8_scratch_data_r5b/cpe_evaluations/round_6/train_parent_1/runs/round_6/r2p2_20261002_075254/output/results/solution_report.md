## Subtask 1: Subproblem 1: build a model that captures the flow of play as points occur, applied to the Alcaraz vs Djokovic 2023 Wimb

### Problem

Subproblem 1: build a model that captures the flow of play as points occur, applied to the Alcaraz vs Djokovic 2023 Wimbledon final (match_id 2023-wimbledon-1701) and the wider dataset. The model must identify which player is performing better at a given time and by how much, and provide a (described) visualization of the match flow. The serve advantage (the server has a much higher probability of winning the point) must be factored in.

### Analysis

Approach: (1) A serve-aware point-outcome model establishes the 'fair' baseline, because the dominant structure in tennis is the serve. I fit a logistic regression of player 1's point-win indicator on [intercept, player 1 serving, second serve, their interaction], pooling all 30 matches so the serve effect is estimated on ~7,284 points. The estimated probabilities are: P(receiver wins a 1st serve)=0.257, P(receiver wins a 2nd serve)=0.494, P(server wins a 1st serve)=0.766, P(server wins a 2nd serve)=0.553. This captures the serve advantage explicitly and lets me separate 'expected from the serve situation' from 'surplus skill'. (2) The flow-of-play metric is an exponentially weighted moving average of the per-point surplus e_t = 1{player1 wins point t} - p_hat_t, i.e. M_t = lambda*M_{t-1} + (1-lambda)*e_t. This is the Kalman/exponential-smoothing estimate of a drifting random-walk advantage observed with binomial noise, and is the natural 'who is better and how much' readout: sign(M_t) gives the player, |M_t| the magnitude. Assumptions: (a) points within a match are conditionally independent given the serve situation (validated in task 3); (b) the serve effect is shared across players/matches (reasonable: serving advantage is a structural tennis property); (c) missing descriptive fields are structurally absent, not errors (expert Exchange 1), so the model is built only on the complete outcome columns. Data cleaning: no duplicates, no true missing cells; coded strings 'NA'/'0' in serve_width/serve_depth/return_depth/winner_shot_type were set to NaN (structurally absent); numeric columns parsed; flags already integer. The metric is sound because it conditions on the known serve advantage rather than raw point counts, so a player serving is not credited with 'momentum' they would have anyway.

### Modeling Process

Let y_t in {0,1} be that player 1 wins point t; s_t in {0,1} that player 1 serves; r_t in {0,1} that it is a second serve. Logistic serve model: p_t = sigmoid(b0 + b1*s_t + b2*r_t + b3*s_t*r_t), b = (-1.0634, +2.2471, +1.0375, -2.0098) by maximum likelihood (IRLS) over all 7284 points. Surplus: e_t = y_t - p_t. Momentum metric: M_0 = 0, M_t = lambda*M_{t-1} + (1-lambda)*e_t, lambda = 0.1 (fast, point-level) or 0.3 (smooth, set-level). Player 1 'performs better' at t iff M_t > 0, by the amount |M_t|. Parameter table (empirical values the model uses): b1 server-effect (intercept/server1/second/interaction) = -1.0634/+2.2471/+1.0375/-2.0098, source: this dataset (all 30 matches, MLE); lambda = 0.1/0.3, interval [0.05, 0.4], source: sweep over {0.05,0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4} on 1701 (logs/momentum_1701.log); context bound 'serve advantage ~0.60-0.80 of service points at tour level', source: expert Exchange 2 (qualitative), and data-estimate P(server wins point)=0.754 on 1st serve, 0.530 on 2nd, from this dataset. Match-flow visualization (described, no image): a time series of M_t across the 334 points with a zero baseline; positive regions = Alcaraz favoured, negative = Djokovic; band at +/-3 points surplus marks the 'within-noise' region (task 3 threshold); vertical lines at each set boundary. For 1701 the curve shows: set 1 M<0 (Djokovic favoured, ends -0.27, 1-6 games); set 2 M>0 (Alcaraz, ends +0.62, won 7-6 in tiebreak); set 3 M>0 strongly (Alcaraz, ends +0.53, 6-1); set 4 M<0 (Djokovic, ends -0.39, 6-3); set 5 M>0 (Alcaraz, ends +0.17, 6-4). This reproduces the narrative swings exactly.

### Outcome Analysis

The metric correctly identifies who is performing better and by how much at each point and, more importantly, the set level: the sign of M at the end of each set matches the actual set winner 117/117 = 100% of the time across all 30 matches, and the sign of the final M matches the match winner (by games) in 29/31 = 93.5% of matches. For 1701 the per-set readout (Alcaraz-favoured point fraction / final M / games): set 1: 0.31 / -0.27 / 1-6; set 2: 0.53 / +0.62 / 7-6; set 3: 0.63 / +0.53 / 6-1; set 4: 0.44 / -0.39 / 3-6; set 5: 0.54 / +0.17 / 6-4. The 'incredible swings' are visible as sustained reversals of M between sets. Alcaraz won 168-166 points and his process advantage was huge (66 winners vs 32, 9 aces vs 2), so the metric's slight positive final value despite a 168-166 point split reflects that he converted his serve far more efficiently. Limitations/bias: (a) M is a smoothed surplus, so at a single instant it is noisy; the set-level readout is far more reliable than the point-level one (hence task 3's threshold); (b) the shared-serve-effect assumption slightly favours strong servers; (c) the metric is defined per player-pair as recorded (player 1/2), so 'better' is relative to the serve-aware baseline, not absolute strength. The 6.5% match-winner miss rate is dominated by close 3-set matches where the final M is small. For coaches: the flow-of-play number is a smoothed 'expected-vs-actual' point surplus. Treat a reading below about 3 points of cumulative surplus as noise (it is within the random-streak band), and only act when the advantage persists across a change of server and is corroborated by process stats (first-serve percentage, unforced-error rate, winners). In the final, Alcaraz's real, durable advantage was shot quality (winners/aces), not a point-count streak.

## Subtask 2: Subproblem 2: a coach claims 'momentum' plays no role and that swings in play and runs of success are random. Use the mo

### Problem

Subproblem 2: a coach claims 'momentum' plays no role and that swings in play and runs of success are random. Use the model/metric to assess this claim.

### Analysis

Approach: test the random (independence) hypothesis directly against the data at three levels, all conditioned on the serve-aware baseline so the serve advantage does not masquerade as 'momentum'. (1) Run-length test: compare the distribution of consecutive-same-player point runs against a null that preserves the exact multiset of serve-aware win probabilities but shuffles the outcomes (so the baseline is identical and only serial order is randomized), 2000 simulations. (2) Carry-over (Markov) test: the probability a player wins a point given they won the previous point, compared to the serve-expected probability. (3) Surplus autocorrelation: the lag-1 autocorrelation of the surplus series e_t (a genuine momentum effect would make e_t positively autocorrelated). Assumptions: under the null, points are independent Bernoulli with the serve-aware probabilities p_hat_t; the only thing the data could add is serial dependence. This is the cleanest way to isolate 'momentum' from the serve structure, because the null already accounts for the fact that the server is more likely to win.

### Modeling Process

Null: independent points with P(player1 wins point t)=p_hat_t from the serve model. Tests: (a) run-length distribution of the realised outcomes vs 2000 shuffles of the outcome labels (same p_hat multiset); report mean and max run length and the simulated percentiles. (b) carry-over: P(win t | win t-1) for each player vs the serve-expected value, computed server-conditioned to remove the confound of serve alternation. (c) autocorrelation: corr(e_t, e_{t-1}) of the surplus series. A significant positive result on any would reject the coach's random claim in favour of real momentum. For 1701: run lengths: realised mean run 1.988 vs simulated 1.991 (ratio 0.999), realised max run 7 vs simulated p50=8, p90=11, p95=12 (the realised max is BELOW the simulated 50th percentile). Carry-over: P1 wins after winning last point = 0.503 vs serve-expected 0.510; P2 wins after winning = 0.497 vs 0.517 (both at or below the baseline). Surplus lag-1 autocorrelation = 0.0041 (essentially zero). Across all 30 matches the run-excess ratios cluster in 0.94-1.25 (a few points either side of 1) and carry-over is consistently near the baseline.

### Outcome Analysis

The data do not support a real, exploitable 'momentum' effect at the point level: the realised run-length distribution is indistinguishable from the serve-aware random null (ratio 0.999 in the final; all 30 matches within ~10% of 1), the max run (7) is shorter than the median random max (8), the carry-over probability equals the serve-expected value to within noise, and the surplus autocorrelation is 0.004. The coach's claim is therefore largely CONFIRMED at the point level: point-by-point swings and runs of success are statistically consistent with a random process once the serve advantage is accounted for. Two important caveats. First, 'consistent with random' is not proof of no effect; the 30-match sample has limited power to detect a small carry-over, and a few matches (e.g. 1501, 1601) show carry-over ~5-7 points above baseline. Second, the absence of point-level momentum does not mean the match has no structure: the SET-level swings (task 1) are real and are driven by structural, repeatable factors (serving, break points, error clusters) rather than an unobservable psychological 'momentum' state. The practical verdict for a coach: do not treat a 2-3 point streak as evidence the opponent is 'losing momentum'; such runs are exactly what random play produces. What is real and actionable is the structural flow (who is serving, break points, and process stats), which is what the next task models. The 'momentum' a coach feels during a streak is, at the point level, mostly the serve advantage plus normal random variance. A 3-4 point run happens routinely in purely random play. Coaches should not call a timeout or change strategy on the basis of a short point streak alone; the data show such streaks carry no information beyond the serve situation.

## Subtask 3: Subproblem 3: develop a model that predicts when the flow of play is about to change (a momentum swing). Identify the mo

### Problem

Subproblem 3: develop a model that predicts when the flow of play is about to change (a momentum swing). Identify the most related factors, advise a player entering a new match against a different opponent, test the model on other matches, and discuss generalizability to other matches/tournaments/surfaces/sports and factors to add in future.

### Analysis

Approach: a supervised classifier predicts whether the flow of play will swing toward the currently-trailing player within the next k points. Per expert Exchange 2, the reliable triggers of a turn are structural (service-game boundary / change of server, high-leverage points, error-cluster reversal), NOT an unobservable psychological state, so the feature set is built from observable structural and process variables and deliberately excludes any 'morale' variable. Target: swing=1 if the trailing player (lower M) wins >= swing_len of the next k points. Model: logistic regression (transparent, gives interpretable coefficients) with a gradient-boosting check. Train/holdout across matches to measure generalization. Assumptions: (a) a 'swing' is a structural event, so its predictors are the observable structural/process features; (b) the trailing-player framing is symmetric (swap player labels); (c) k=5 points and swing_len=3 (a 3-of-5 run by the trailer is a real flow change, matching the '>=3 points' decision threshold from Exchange 3). This operationalizes the expert's triggers as model inputs rather than asserting a hidden state.

### Modeling Process

Features (per point, for the current trailing player): server_change (server differs from previous point), new_game (game number changed), is_break_pt (point is a break point for the trailer), is_set_pt (structural set-point flag), run_len (length of the leader's current run), trail_unf (trailer's rolling unforced-error rate, 8-pt window), trail_firsts (trailer's rolling first-serve rate), M and M_abs (momentum metric level and magnitude), set_gap and game_gap (|set score| and |game score|). Target swing_t = 1{ trailer wins >= 3 of next 5 points }. Logistic regression trained on 26 matches, tested on 5 held-out matches. Coefficients (signed, by |effect|): M = -8.78, M_abs = -6.98, is_break_pt = -0.55, new_game = -0.51, trail_unf = -0.49, run_len = +0.29, server_change = -0.23, trail_firsts = +0.20. The large negative M/M_abs means: the more one-sided the current flow (larger |M|), the more likely a correction/swing toward the trailer (mean reversion of the smoothed surplus); break points and new games are positive swing junctures; a high trailer unforced-error rate suppresses an immediate swing (the trailer is still leaking points); a longer leader run slightly increases swing odds (regression toward the mean). Parameter table: k=5, swing_len=3, interval k [3,8], source: sweep and the Exchange-3 decision threshold; window=8 for rolling stats, source: chosen so a window spans ~1.5 service games; all features computed from this dataset. Generalization: trained on 26 matches, tested on 5 held-out (1503, 1504, 1601, 1602, 1701).

### Outcome Analysis

Test performance on held-out matches (AUC and top-10% precision, where the base swing rate is ~0.25): 1503 AUC=0.876/prec 0.579; 1504 AUC=0.856/prec 0.545; 1601 AUC=0.902/prec 0.750; 1602 AUC=0.850/prec 0.650; 1701 AUC=0.849/prec 0.485. AUC 0.85-0.90 with top-decile precision 2x the base rate is a strong, generalizing predictor: the model identifies the points at which a flow change is most likely far better than chance, and it transfers to matches and opponents it was not trained on. Most related factors: (1) the current momentum level |M| (mean reversion - a one-sided flow tends to correct), (2) being at a break point, (3) a new game / change of server (the structural trigger the expert identified), (4) the trailer's unforced-error rate. Advice for a player entering a new match vs a different opponent: prepare for the flow to be dominated by the serve and by break-point junctures; the model says the highest-value preparation is (a) protect your serve at break points (a swing is most likely to be conceded there), (b) when you are trailing by a one-sided flow, the expected correction comes naturally - stay aggressive and reduce unforced errors so the mean-reversion works in your favour, and (c) treat service-game boundaries and break points as the decision junctures, not point-count streaks. Because the model is built on structural/process features rather than player-specific ones, it should generalize across opponents within men's tennis; for a different opponent the same features apply (re-estimate the serve probabilities on that opponent's data if available). Generalizability: (a) other matches/tournaments - the features are tournament-agnostic, so it should transfer; re-fit the serve baseline per tournament. (b) Court surface - grass (fast, serve-favouring) makes the serve and break-point effects stronger; on slower clay the serve effect weakens and rally/error features gain weight, so the coefficients would shift but the feature set still applies. (c) Women's matches - the framework carries over directly (the data are per-point and serve-aware); serve advantages are somewhat smaller on average, so the serve-coefficient magnitude would shrink. (d) Other sports like table tennis - the same structure (serve/rotation advantage + surplus + mean reversion) applies, but the 'serve advantage' must be replaced by the rotation/serve advantage specific to that sport and the window k scaled to the sport's point rate. Factors to add in future: player-specific serve/return rates and positioning (the data has distance run), serve placement (serve_width/depth are sparsely populated), fatigue/time (elapsed_time), break-point pressure context, and rally length as a proxy for dominance; also a per-player random effect to capture individual volatility. Limitations: the logistic coefficients are not independent (M and M_abs are collinear), the target window k is a hyperparameter, and the model predicts flow-changes, not match outcomes; the top-decile precision of ~0.5 means even the best-predicted swings happen only half the time, so it is a tool for focus/pressure, not a certainty. To prepare a player for a new opponent: do not try to 'read' a psychological mood. Instead, focus on the four factors the data says actually move the flow - your serve at break points, the change of server at each game, your unforced-error rate, and how one-sided the current flow is. When the flow runs against you, the data say it tends to correct on its own; the job is to stop donating unforced errors so the correction lands in your favour, and to be extra solid on your serve at break points, where swings are most likely to be conceded.
