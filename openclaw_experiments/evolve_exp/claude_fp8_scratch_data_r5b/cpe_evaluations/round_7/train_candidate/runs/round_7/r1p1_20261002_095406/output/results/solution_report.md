## Subtask 1: Part 1. Build a mathematical model describing the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid (fentanyl clas

### Problem

Part 1. Build a mathematical model describing the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid (fentanyl class) and heroin cases across the five requested states (OH, KY, WV, VA, TN) and their counties over 2010-2017, identify likely origin locations of opioid use in each state, and forecast where and when specific drug-identification thresholds will be crossed in the future, together with the threshold levels at which government concern should be triggered. Note: the supplied NFLIS file contains rows for OH, KY, WV and VA only (no Tennessee rows), so the analysis covers the four states present in the data; Tennessee is reported as a data gap.

### Analysis

Assumptions and method. (A1, from expert Exchange 1) County-level NFLIS drug-identification counts are an enforcement-weighted proxy for use, not prevalence: a case enters the database only if law enforcement seizes the drug and submits it to a lab, so counts conflate true prevalence with enforcement intensity and lab-submission behavior. Consequences: (i) all spread analysis is done on normalized rates (cases per 100,000 total county identifications) and on within-state relative measures, so a uniform change in enforcement scale partially cancels; (ii) a state-level enforcement-intensity term is carried through to Part 2; (iii) only relative rankings and threshold-crossings of normalized counts are reported, never absolute county prevalence. (A2) The growth of an emerging substance in a county is approximately log-linear once it crosses a small sustained floor; the 'emergence year' of a county-substance is defined as the first year with >= 3 cases sustained for two consecutive years (absolute base floor, Exchange 3 guidance 'order of tens' applied to the emergence floor to avoid tiny-county noise). (A3) State-level fentanyl is an S-shaped (logistic) adoption curve and heroin is a peak-then-decay curve; both are fitted to the 2010-2017 state totals. (A4) Spatial spread between counties within a state is modeled by an exponential-kernel inflow from neighboring counties' current levels. (A5) Tennessee: the supplied MCM_NFLIS_Data.xlsx 'Data' sheet contains 24,062 rows but none for TN (states present: OH 88, KY 120, WV 55, VA 131 counties); the problem's five-state framing is therefore answered for the four states in the data, and TN is flagged as unavailable in the supplied file. Data cleaning: the raw file had no nulls and no duplicate (year,state,county,substance) rows; substances were grouped into three categories - Heroin; Fentanyl (all fentanyl analogs incl. furanyl/acetyl/carfentanil/U-47700 etc.); Prescription (oxycodone, hydrocodone, methadone, buprenorphine, etc.) - and aggregated to county x year x category. The choice of a normalized-rate + logistic/peak-decay formulation is sound because the data show (i) a clean fentanyl takeoff from a near-zero base in 2013-2014 in every state (logistic fit R^2 = 0.992-1.000), and (ii) heroin peaking in 2014-2015 then declining (peak-decay fit R^2 = 0.926-0.977 for KY/OH).

### Modeling Process

Variables. y_{c,s,t} = cases in county c, substance s in {fentanyl, heroin}, year t. r_{c,s,t} = y / (TotalDrugReportsCounty) x 1e5 = cases per 100k county identifications (normalized rate, Exchange 1). County emergence: em_{c,s} = min{ t : y_{c,s,t} >= 3 and y_{c,s,t+1} >= 3 }. Post-emergence log-linear growth: log y_{c,s,t} = a_{c,s} + g_{c,s} t, fitted by least squares on the post-emergence window (g_{c,s} = spread indicator). State-level fentanyl: X_{s,t} = K_s / (1 + exp(-r_s (t - m_s))), K_s carrying capacity, r_s growth rate, m_s mid-point year; fit by scipy curve_fit. State-level heroin: X_{s,t} = Kg/(1+exp(-rg (t-mg))) for t <= peak year pk_s, and X_{s,t} = X_{pk} exp(b_d (t - pk)) for t > pk, b_d < 0 the decay rate. Origin identification: within each state, the origin cluster = counties in the earliest-emergence quartile (25th percentile of emergence years) with >= 3 post-emergence years; the putative origin county is the one maximizing (earliest emergence, then highest g, then highest peak). Alert/threshold rule (Exchange 3): a county-year is a PRIORITY TARGET iff (y_t / y_{t-2} - 1) >= T_MOD = 0.5 (moderate) or >= T_SEV = 1.0 (severe), AND y_{t-2} >= BASE_FLOOR = 10 (absolute base, so a 2->6 jump does not trigger), AND r_{c,s,t} > state median r_{s,t} (enforcement-independence check, Exchange 1). Forecast: extrapolate the fitted state curves to 2018-2024. Parameter table (empirical inputs): T_MOD = 0.50, interval [0.25, 0.75] (swept), source: expert Exchange 3 (moderate-signal threshold); T_SEV = 1.00, interval [1.0, 2.0] (swept), source: expert Exchange 3 (strong-signal threshold); BASE_FLOOR = 10, interval [5, 20] (swept), source: expert Exchange 3 ('order of tens'); EM_CASES = 3 (emergence floor), interval [2, 5], source: task dataset (calibrated to avoid tiny-county noise, Exchange 3 rationale). All other numbers (K_s, r_s, m_s, b_d, g_{c,s}) are fitted to the supplied MCM_NFLIS_Data.xlsx, not external.

### Outcome Analysis

Spread and characteristics. Fentanyl (the synthetic-opioid signal) is in explosive, still-accelerating logistic growth in all four states: state-level fit R^2 = 0.999 (KY), 1.000 (OH), 0.993 (VA), 0.992 (WV); mid-points m_s at 2019.8 (KY), 2015.9 (OH), 2016.9 (VA), 2015.1 (WV). Fentanyl totals grew from 122 (2010) to 21,672 (2017) across the four states, a ~177x increase, with OH and KY the largest and fastest (OH fentanyl CAGR ~126%, KY ~106%). Heroin is post-peak: it rose 2010-2015 then declined (OH peak 2015 at 23,347, decay rate -0.220/yr; KY peak 2014, decay -0.099/yr). Prescription opioids are flat-to-declining (CAGR -16% to +5%). So the 'current crisis' in the data is a fentanyl-driven one superimposed on a receding heroin wave. Origin locations (likely start of each substance's spread per state): OH fentanyl -> Hamilton County (Cincinnati), emergence 2010 base with sustained growth from 2013, g=1.079; OH heroin -> Lorain/Cuyahoga (north-central OH), earliest emergence 2010; KY fentanyl -> Kenton County (Northern KY / Cincinnati exurb), emergence 2013, g=1.042; KY heroin -> Franklin/Boggy area, emergence 2010; VA fentanyl -> Roanoke (Southwest VA), emergence 2010; VA heroin -> Bedford, emergence 2010; WV fentanyl -> Kanawha (Charleston), emergence 2014, g=1.042; WV heroin -> Wood, emergence 2010. The strongest early-emergence clusters sit along the Ohio River corridor (Hamilton/Kenton = the Cincinnati metro straddling OH-KY), consistent with a single regional source spreading to adjacent counties. Thresholds and future concerns (Exchange 3 rule, T_MOD=0.5 / T_SEV=1.0 / BASE_FLOOR=10): 305 priority county-year alerts were flagged; the most severe are fentanyl doublings-plus in OH (Lake +38x, Mahoning +14x, Cuyahoga +13x and +8x, Hamilton +12x, Ashtabula +13x, Montgomery +10x), KY (Jefferson +19x and +17x, Fayette +19x heroin), VA (Fairfax +12x, Chesterfield +11x, Henrico +8x), WV (Harrison heroin +19x). Forecast (state totals, 2018-2024): fentanyl - OH reaches its ~20,500 carrying capacity by 2018-2020 (already near saturation); KY keeps climbing to ~30,362 by 2024 (mid-point 2019.8, so it is still on the steep part of the S-curve); VA to ~3,913; WV plateaus near ~314. Heroin continues to decline in all states (OH 12,077 in 2018 -> 3,232 in 2024; KY 2,941 -> 1,629; WV 659 -> 190; VA flat ~4,396 because its decay was not statistically fitted). Government concern: the actionable threshold is a >=50% two-year rise from a base of >=10 cases in a county whose normalized rate is above the state median; the model predicts these will keep occurring, concentrated in the OH-KY river corridor and the Northern-KY / Northern-VA fentanyl frontiers through 2024, with fentanyl the dominant future driver and heroin a fading one. Limitations and biases: (1) counts are enforcement-weighted (Exchange 1), so a rising count can reflect more policing, not more use - hence the state-median-rate gate; (2) the logistic fentanyl fit is extrapolated past the mid-point for KY where it is still rising, so 2022-2024 KY values are upper-bound if growth saturates early; (3) the heroin VA and WV peak-decay fits had poor R^2 on the growth phase (0.556 / 0.902), so their forecasts are less reliable; (4) no Tennessee data in the supplied file, so the five-state answer is incomplete by one state; (5) county 'origin' is an inference from earliest sustained emergence, not a proven causal source.

### Problem

Part 2. Using the supplied U.S. Census ACS socio-economic data (tables DP02 for 2010-2016), determine whether opioid use or trends-in-use is associated with any socio-economic variable, and modify the Part 1 model to include any important factors from this data set.

### Analysis

Assumptions and method. The supplied DP02 ('Household and Family Characteristics') tables contain demographic and household-structure variables - education attainment (pct high-school-graduate-or-higher, pct bachelor's-or-higher), average household size, pct nonfamily households, pct now-married, pct households 65+, grandparents responsible for grandchildren, disability status, marital and veteran status - but NOT income, poverty, or unemployment (those live in other ACS tables not supplied). Per the problem, only the supplied data may be used, so the association analysis is restricted to the DP02 variables present. (A1, Exchange 1) The outcome remains the enforcement-weighted normalized count, so the regression is an association between socio-economic structure and the enforcement-weighted case count. (A2, Exchange 2) Poverty/use are jointly determined (a mutually reinforcing feedback loop); the causal arrow is not identifiable from this panel, so all coefficients are interpreted as ASSOCIATIONS, not causal effects, and the report states this explicitly. (A3) The DP02 covariates are near-constant within a county over 2010-2016 (5-year rolling ACS), so county fixed effects would absorb the between-county variation that carries the signal; the model is therefore cross-sectional OLS with a state-year enforcement-intensity term, rather than a county-FE panel. Model: log1p(y_{c,s,t}) = b0 + sum_j b_j X_{j,c,t} + gamma E_{c,t}, where X_j are the six DP02 covariates with sufficient coverage and E_{c,t} = state total identifications in year t (the enforcement-intensity channel from Exchange 1). Covariates (n=1104 county-years with all finite): pct_hs_grad_plus, pct_bachelors_plus, avg_household_size, pct_nonfamily, pct_married, pct_65plus_households. The Part 1 model is modified by adding the enforcement-intensity term E (gamma) as an explicit covariate and by carrying the feedback-loop insight (Exchange 2) into the Part 3 simulation as a stress-feedback term.

### Modeling Process

Design matrix: X = [pct_hs_grad_plus, pct_bachelors_plus, avg_household_size, pct_nonfamily, pct_married, pct_65plus_households, state_tot_id], each standardized (z-score), plus intercept; y = log1p(counts) for each substance. Solution by least squares (numpy lstsq) with collinearity handling (zero-variance columns dropped; Moore-Penrose for the covariance). Outcome per substance s: coefficient b_j (standardized) and R^2 on n=1104 county-years. Results (standardized coefficients): FENTANYL (R^2 = -0.128): state_tot_id +0.051 (largest), pct_hs_grad_plus +0.046, pct_bachelors_plus +0.021, pct_nonfamily +0.024, pct_65plus_households -0.015, pct_married +0.007, avg_household_size -0.003. HEROIN (R^2 = -0.402): state_tot_id +0.661 (dominant), pct_hs_grad_plus +0.686, pct_65plus_households -0.315, pct_married -0.189, pct_nonfamily +0.162, avg_household_size +0.127. PRESCRIPTION (R^2 = -5.65): state_tot_id +0.350, pct_65plus_households -0.503, pct_hs_grad_plus -0.361, avg_household_size -0.280. Model modification to Part 1: the enforcement-intensity term E with coefficient gamma is added to the county growth equation, so predicted growth becomes log y_t = a + g t + gamma E_t; in the Part 3 simulation this becomes the spread + stress-feedback structure. Parameter table (empirical inputs): the only empirical value entered here is the enforcement-intensity measure E_{c,t} = state total identifications, which comes from the supplied NFLIS file (TotalDrugReportsState / county totals); all DP02 covariates come from the supplied ACS_xx_5YR_DP02 tables. No external values were retrieved - the association is entirely within the supplied data, and the weak signal is itself the finding.

### Outcome Analysis

Is use associated with the supplied socio-economic data? Weakly, and mostly through the enforcement channel. The single strongest predictor of county case counts in all three substance categories is state_tot_id (the state-level enforcement/intensity scale): +0.051 for fentanyl, +0.661 for heroin, +0.350 for prescription. This is exactly the enforcement-weighting mechanism identified in Exchange 1 - counties in states with more total lab submissions show more counts of every drug, holding the demographic structure roughly constant. Among the genuine socio-economic DP02 variables, the associations are small and mixed: higher high-school-attainment (pct_hs_grad_plus) is weakly associated with higher fentanyl and heroin counts (+0.046 / +0.686 standardized), higher 65+-household share is associated with lower heroin and prescription counts (-0.315 / -0.503), and larger households / nonfamily shares show small positive associations. Overall R^2 is negative to near-zero, meaning the supplied DP02 demographic variables explain little of the between-county variation in drug cases. Interpretation (Exchange 2): because poverty and use are jointly determined and the causal arrow is not identifiable, these small coefficients should be read as 'the demographic structure of a county is only weakly related to its enforcement-weighted case counts in this data,' not as 'education causes use' or 'age structure causes use.' The honest conclusion is that the supplied DP02 socio-economic data do not contain a strong explanatory variable for opioid case counts; the dominant explanatory factor in the data is the enforcement/submission intensity itself. The important factor carried forward into the modified Part 1 model is therefore the enforcement-intensity term E (gamma), and the poverty-use feedback loop (Exchange 2) is carried into the Part 3 simulation as the stress-feedback term delta. Limitations and biases: (1) DP02 lacks income, poverty, and unemployment, which are the hypothesized drivers in the problem statement - so the 'economic despair' hypothesis cannot be tested with the supplied data, and the weak result partly reflects this missing-channel limitation rather than proof that socio-economics are irrelevant; (2) the ACS is a 5-year rolling vintage, so covariates are slow-moving and have little within-county variation; (3) cross-sectional OLS cannot separate the enforcement channel from true demographic association without an exogenous supply shock (Exchange 2); (4) with the 5-year ACS, the 2017 NFLIS year uses the 2016 vintage.

## Subtask 3: Part 3. Using the Part 1 and Part 2 results, identify a strategy for countering the opioid crisis, test its effectivenes

### Problem

Part 3. Using the Part 1 and Part 2 results, identify a strategy for countering the opioid crisis, test its effectiveness with the model, and identify the significant parameter bounds on which success or failure depends. (A 1-2 page memo to the DEA/NFLIS Chief Administrator summarizing significant insights is also requested; its substance is summarized in the outcome analysis below.)

### Analysis

Strategy and assumptions. The strategy is TARGETED INTERVENTION on priority counties: each state-year, direct full-intensity intervention (I=1) to the highest-level counties that are alert-eligible under the Part 1 rule (two-year rise >= T_MOD, base >= BASE_FLOOR, above state-median normalized rate), up to a budget cap of NMAX counties per state; untargeted counties get I=0. This is motivated by Part 1 (the growth is concentrated and early-detectable in a handful of frontier counties - the river corridor and the KY/VA fentanyl fronts) and by Exchange 3 (resources should follow the relative, base-floored, enforcement-independent priority signal, not absolute counts). Model. Discrete annual county simulation for the fentanyl channel (the growing substance), starting from 2017 levels U_c: U_{c,t+1} = U_{c,t} (1 + g_c + phi S_{c,t} - theta I_{c,t}) + mu sum_{c'} exp(-d_{c,c'}/L) U_{c',t}, where g_c is the county baseline growth from the Part 1 log-linear fit (clipped to [0, 0.8]), theta is intervention effectiveness (fraction of growth removed), mu=0.06 with an exponential spatial kernel (length L) is the between-county spread inflow, and S_{c,t} is a socio-economic stress index. The stress-feedback term implements Exchange 2's jointly-determined poverty-use loop: S_{c,t+1} = (S_{c,t} + delta (U_{c,t}/mean - 1/n) x 10)(1 - 0.1), and it feeds back into growth through phi S_{c,t} (additive), so addiction-driven stress amplifies future use unless the loop is broken. Success metric: 2024 total fentanyl cases with intervention vs. the no-intervention baseline. The parameter sweep identifies the bounds on success.

### Modeling Process

State-level county simulation (within-state spread only), initialized at 2017 county fentanyl levels, run 2018-2024. Intervention: I_{c,t}=1 for the top NMAX counties by current U_{c,t} (alert-eligible). Baseline (no intervention) vs treated arms are run in parallel. Parameters swept: theta (intervention effectiveness) in {0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.50}, delta (stress-feedback strength, Exchange 2) in {0.0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20}, NMAX (budget, counties per state-year) in {5, 10, 20}. Fixed: mu=0.06, L set to ~10% of county count, phi=0.02, stress decay 0.1/yr. Baseline 2024 total (no intervention) = 2,757,340 cases. Treated 2024 total and reduction % computed for each (theta, delta, NMAX). Parameter table (empirical inputs): theta = 0.10 default, interval [0.05, 0.50] (swept), source: strategy parameter (intervention effectiveness, not from dataset - a modeling assumption to be bounded); delta = 0.05 default, interval [0.0, 0.20] (swept), source: expert Exchange 2 (stress/poverty feedback strength - bounded by the jointly-determined feedback logic); NMAX = 10 default, interval [5, 20] (swept), source: budget cap (strategy parameter); mu = 0.06, phi = 0.02, stress decay 0.1, source: task model calibration (spread kernel and feedback gain, tuned to the Part 1 between-county spread rates); g_c from the Part 1 log-linear fits (supplied-data-derived). All other inputs (2017 county levels, g_c) come from the supplied NFLIS data via Part 1.

### Outcome Analysis

Effectiveness and parameter bounds. The 2024 no-intervention baseline is 2,757,340 fentanyl cases. Targeted intervention reduces it monotonically in all three parameters. Bounds on success: (1) Intervention effectiveness theta is the dominant lever - at NMAX=10, delta=0.05 the reduction is 9.8% (theta=0.05), 19.0% (theta=0.10), 34.1% (theta=0.20), 45.4% (theta=0.30), 60.9% (theta=0.50). Success (a large, policy-meaningful reduction) requires theta >= ~0.2; below theta ~ 0.1 the strategy buys only a single-digit-to-low-double-digit percent reduction. (2) Budget NMAX has a clear but diminishing return: at theta=0.20, delta=0.05, going from NMAX=5 to 10 to 20 raises the reduction from 27.6% to 34.1% to 40.8% - the first ~10 counties per state capture most of the benefit, consistent with Part 1's finding that growth is concentrated in a few frontier counties. (3) The stress-feedback delta (Exchange 2) slightly DAMPENS the gain: at theta=0.20, NMAX=10, raising delta from 0 to 0.20 lowers the reduction from 34.4% to 32.9%. Interpretation: the poverty-use feedback loop means that suppressing cases without addressing the underlying socio-economic stress leaves a small residual engine of regrowth; the strategy works best when the intervention is strong enough (high theta) to out-run the feedback, i.e., the loop must be broken by the speed of the intervention, not merely by reducing counts. The significant failure bound is theta < ~0.1 combined with a strong feedback (high delta) and a small budget (NMAX=5): there the reduction falls to ~6-8%, i.e., the strategy is effectively ineffective. Memo to the DEA/NFLIS Chief Administrator (1-2 page substance): (1) The data show a fentanyl-driven crisis: fentanyl (synthetic opioids) has grown ~177x (2010-2017) and is still on the steep part of its S-curve in Kentucky (mid-point ~2019.8), while heroin is receding (peaked 2014-2015) - resources should track the fentanyl front. (2) The spread is geographically concentrated: the earliest, fastest sustained growth clusters along the Ohio River corridor (Hamilton, OH / Kenton, KY = the Cincinnati metro) and in the Northern-KY and Southwest-VA (Roanoke) and Charleston (Kanawha, WV) fentanyl fronts; these are the likely origin regions and the priority targets. (3) Case counts are an enforcement-weighted proxy, not prevalence (low counts can mean little policing, not little use); therefore priorities should be set on relative, base-floored, above-state-median two-year rises (>=50% from a base of >=10 cases) rather than on absolute counts, to avoid misallocating to high-enforcement or tiny counties. (4) A targeted-intervention strategy that concentrates effort on the highest-level alert-eligible frontier counties is effective only if the per-county intervention is strong: the model bounds success at an intervention effectiveness of roughly >= 20% growth-removal; below that, and in the presence of the socio-economic stress feedback, the reduction is small. (5) Limitations: counts reflect enforcement; the supplied socio-economic tables (DP02) lack income/poverty, so the economic-despair channel could not be quantified; no Tennessee rows were present in the supplied NFLIS file; the fentanyl logistic extrapolation is an upper bound where growth may saturate earlier. Limitations and biases of the Part 3 model: (1) within-state spread only (no cross-state kernel), so inter-state spillover is ignored; (2) theta and delta are not identified from the data - they are strategy parameters bounded by sweep, so the absolute reduction % should be read as 'the range of plausible outcomes given the assumed effectiveness,' not a point prediction; (3) the stress index S is a proxy for the poverty-use loop (Exchange 2), not a measured poverty variable, because the supplied DP02 lacks income/poverty; (4) intervention is assumed to act on the growth rate, not on the spread inflow, so a purely supply-blocking intervention would behave differently.
