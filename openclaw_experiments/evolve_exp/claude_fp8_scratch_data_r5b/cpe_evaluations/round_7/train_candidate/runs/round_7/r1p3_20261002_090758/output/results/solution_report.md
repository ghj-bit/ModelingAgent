## Subtask 1: Build a human-capital network model of ICM's 370-person organization from the supplied HR table (task 1). Scope: represe

### Problem

Build a human-capital network model of ICM's 370-person organization from the supplied HR table (task 1). Scope: represent the seven position levels, their headcounts, salaries, training and recruiting costs, and a network layer over which churn influence can act, with all assumptions stated explicitly. Monetary quantities are carried in sigma (the company median salary) units, the reporting currency ICM uses.

### Analysis

The supplied table1.csv was encoding-damaged (the sigma glyph was mangled to two bytes and one cell — the experienced-employee salary — was blank). I reconstructed the seven rows from the surviving digits and treated every dollar figure as a sigma multiple; the experienced-employee salary was set to 1 sigma, which is the value that makes the salary mass consistent with the company median being sigma. Two internal inconsistencies in the brief are noted and resolved: (a) the level headcounts sum to 370, i.e. 100% filled, yet the text says ICM runs at ~85% filled with 55 vacancies — I model the 85% fill ratio (the stated operating point) and treat the table headcounts as the full position structure, so 55 positions start vacant; (b) the text says the CEO salary is ~10x the median while the senior row is 8 sigma — with the median at 1 sigma the data-implied ratio is 8x, so I keep the data value and flag the 10x as the brief's approximate framing. Method choice: a compartment model by level (not a fully-individual agent model) because the data are level aggregates; a network layer is added as edge weights for churn-influence that are zero at base rate and switched on only in the diffusion sensitivity, so the model degrades gracefully to the level-compartment core. This is sound because it uses only the data actually supplied, keeps every parameter auditable, and isolates the one mechanism (contagion) the data cannot support so it can be toggled.

### Modeling Process

Levels i = 1..7 with positions N_i = [10,20,25,25,110,150,30] (senior mgr, junior mgr, exp supervisor, inexp supervisor, exp employee, inexp employee, admin clerk); total 370. Filled P_i(0) = round(0.85 N_i). Parameters per level (recruit months m_i, recruit cost r_i, salary s_i, training t_i, all in sigma): m=[7,6,5,4,3,1,2], r=[1.2,0.7,0.6,0.6,0.3,0.1,0.3], s=[8,4,2,1.5,1,0.9,0.9], t=[0.5,0.6,0.2,0.3,0.1,0.3,0.05]. Churn: middle-management levels (junior mgr, exp sup, inexp sup) churn at 2x the base rate c (the brief's 'twice the average'), others at c; base c = 0.18. Network layer: each employee is a node; edge weight w_ij encodes churn influence and is 0 in the base case (independence). The influence channel, when active, raises the quit hazard of a level whose members churned last year by a contagion factor kappa (kappa=0 base; 0.3 in the sensitivity). The model is the level-compartment core with the network edge weights factored as a multiplier on the quit hazard.

### Outcome Analysis

The model reproduces the stated operating point: 85% filled at start, middle levels at double churn. Limitations: (1) level aggregation hides within-level heterogeneity, so churn is treated as a uniform hazard within a level rather than tied to named individuals or edges; (2) the network layer is structural (edge weights exist but are zero at base), so the 'network' is a placeholder for influence that the supplied data do not quantify — the honest reading is that the data support a level model, and the network is the extension point for the multi-layer vision in task 6; (3) the 85% vs 100%-sum inconsistency is resolved by privileging the text's operating point; (4) the sigma normalization means all outputs scale with inflation, which ICM explicitly accepts. Bias: the model slightly under-states the cost of mid-level churn because it applies the 2x rate as a common-cause structural effect (advancement blockage) rather than as contagious, which would add a second, smaller channel (quantified in the next task).

## Subtask 2: Identify and incorporate the dynamic processes of (1) organizational churn (influence and dissatisfaction) and (2) direc

### Problem

Identify and incorporate the dynamic processes of (1) organizational churn (influence and dissatisfaction) and (2) direct and indirect effects on productivity (task 2). Scope: specify the churn mechanism, the distinction between independent and contagious churn, and how vacancies plus new-hire ramp-up translate into a productivity loss.

### Analysis

Two churn mechanisms are distinguished. (1) Independent/dissatisfaction churn: each filled position leaves with a level-specific annual hazard c_i; this is the dominant channel and is driven by individual causes (pay, promotion blockage, supervisor, outside offers). (2) Contagious/influence churn: a leaver modestly raises the quit hazard of close same-level coworkers; this is a secondary, network-mediated channel. The distinction matters for causation: the mid-level 2x rate is treated as a common-cause structural effect (many mid-level managers share the same 'stuck, no advancement' condition), not as one person pulling others out; contagion is kept as a separate, smaller, toggle-able channel so the model does not over-claim a causal diffusion the data do not support. Productivity loss has two components: a direct vacancy loss (empty positions do no work) and an indirect ramp-up loss (new hires and internal promotees run below full productivity for a ramp period). The ramp-up loss is the binding one for ICM's skill-based and managerial roles and cannot be offset by overtime, so it is modeled as an additional capacity reduction on top of the vacancy loss.

### Modeling Process

Annual discrete step. (i) Churn: left_i ~ Binomial(P_i, c_i + kappa_i c_i) where kappa_i>0 only if the level churned last year and the contagion channel is on (kappa=0 base, 0.3 sensitivity). P_i -= left_i; vacancy V_i += left_i. (ii) Internal promotion: a level draws only from the surplus of the level directly below it above its 85% baseline (a short-staffed company does not thin its own ranks to promote); promote_all=False tightens the qualified gate to a 90% fill of the donor level (experience requirement). (iii) External recruiting: capacity-capped at R <= 0.09 x 370 = 33/yr (the brief's 8-10% active hiring) and further limited to <= (2/3) of current vacancies; allocated middle-management-first. (iv) Productivity: Prod = (sum_i P_i - (1-ramp) x new)/370 where new = this year's hires + promotions and ramp=0.5 (new entrants at 50% productivity during the 3-12+ month ramp). ramp=1.0 disables the indirect loss for comparison.

Empirical parameters (not in the supplied dataset; each enters as a calibrated input):
- ramp = 0.5, interval [0.5, 1.0], source: expert exchange 2 (new hires run ~3-12+ months below full productivity for ICM's skill-based and managerial roles; 0.5 is the midpoint of that window, 1.0 the no-loss bound). Holds over the 3-12+ month post-hire window for managerial/technical roles; shorter for clerks.
- kappa = 0.3, interval [0.0, 0.3], source: expert exchange 1 (contagion is a real but secondary elevation of quit hazard among close same-level coworkers of a leaver; 0.3 x base hazard is the 'modest' upper bound, 0.0 the independent base case). Holds as a per-year, same-level approximation of the influence effect.
- middle-management churn multiplier = 2.0, interval [2.0, 2.0], source: task dataset/problem statement (middle levels 'suffer high turn-over, twice the average rate'). Fixed by the brief.
- external recruiting capacity = 0.09 x 370 = 33/yr, interval [0.08 x 370, 0.10 x 370], source: task dataset/problem statement (HR 'actively hiring about 8-10% of the ICM positions'). Fixed by the brief; 2/3-of-vacancies sub-cap from the same statement.
- vacancy decision bands: normal <=10%, crisis 15-20%, operational emergency >20-25% unfilled, source: expert exchange 3 (leadership tolerates low-single-digit to ~10% vacancy, treats ~15-20% as crisis and >20-25% as an operational emergency). Used only to interpret results, not in the dynamics.

### Outcome Analysis

At base churn (18%, mid 2x) with full recruiting capacity, fill falls 0.85 -> 0.784 (yr1) -> 0.676 (yr2); the ramp-up channel costs a further 4.46 percentage points of effective capacity (0.784 fill -> 0.739 productivity in yr1), i.e. ~16.5 person-equivalents per year of indirect loss that overtime cannot recover. The contagion sensitivity (kappa=0.3) lowers year-2 fill from 0.676 to 0.638 (-3.8 pp), confirming it is a real but secondary amplifier, consistent with the independent-hazard core being the right base case. Key structural finding: churn at base is ~67 people/yr but backfill capacity is only 33/yr, so the vacancy stock grows by ~27/yr no matter what — the system has no stable full-employment equilibrium at the given recruiting rate. Limitations: the binomial annual hazard smooths intra-year timing; the ramp is a single 50% depth rather than a per-level ramp curve (clerks ramp faster than managers); and contagion is applied at level granularity, not along named edges, so it bounds rather than measures the true network effect. Bias: the model is conservative on the independent channel and explicitly excludes unmeasured network amplification from the base case, so base-case fill is an upper bound on how fast the organization can be hollowed out by diffusion alone.

### Problem

Analyze ICM's budget requirements for talent management, in sigma, for recruiting and training over the next 2 years (task 3). Scope: total the annual salary mass, training mass, and one-time recruiting cost at the base churn rate over a 2-year horizon.

### Analysis

Talent-management budget = salary mass (people x level salary) + training mass (people x level training cost) + external recruiting cost (hires x level recruit cost), summed over two years. Salaries and training are recurring (depend on the filled headcount each year, which declines as vacancies accumulate), while recruiting cost is a one-time expense per hire. Because the headcount path is already determined by the churn model (task 2), the budget is a read-off of that trajectory at base churn (18%, mid 2x) with external recruiting active. I report recruiting and training separately as the question asks, plus the all-in total for context.

### Modeling Process

For each year y in {1,2}: Sal_y = sum_i P_i(y) s_i; Train_y = sum_i P_i(y) t_i; Rec_y = sum_i (hires_i(y)) r_i. Budget_2y = Sal_1+Sal_2 + Train_1+Train_2 + Rec_1+Rec_2. Using the base-case trajectory (P_total 315->290->250): Sal = 430.0 + 392.5 = 822.5 sigma; Train = 71.5 + 63.4 = 134.9 sigma; Rec = 16.4 + 17.7 = 34.1 sigma (33 hires/yr at the level-mixed cost).

### Outcome Analysis

Two-year talent-management budget at base churn: training 134.9 sigma, recruiting 34.1 sigma, salary 822.5 sigma, all-in 991.5 sigma. Recruiting is the smaller, one-time line (~3.4% of the 2-year total) while training is the larger recurring line (~13.6%); the salary mass dominates because it is 370 positions worth of compensation. Indirect reading: the budget is a declining function of the filled headcount, so as churn hollows out the organization the nominal budget falls (fewer people to pay and train) even though the organization is getting weaker — a fallacy of composition the HR office should not read as savings. Limitation: the 2-year window is shorter than the time to full steady-state decline, so this understates the cumulative recruiting cost if the vacancy gap is allowed to persist; and training cost is held at the per-level rate rather than scaled up for the extra onboarding that high churn forces. Bias: slightly under-states training because it does not add a churn-driven onboarding premium beyond the ramp-up productivity loss already in task 2.

## Subtask 4: Determine whether ICM can sustain its 80%-full status if the annual churn rate for all positions rises to 25%, and to 35

### Problem

Determine whether ICM can sustain its 80%-full status if the annual churn rate for all positions rises to 25%, and to 35%; give the costs and the indirect effects of those higher turnover rates (task 4). Scope: run the churn model at c=0.25 and c=0.35 (middle levels still at 2x), with full recruiting capacity, and compare the resulting fill, productivity, and budget to the base case against the leadership vacancy decision bands.

### Analysis

Sustainability of 80% full is tested by simulating 2 (and 4) years at the higher churn rates and checking the end-of-horizon fill against 0.80. Costs are read from the budget equations of task 3 at the new trajectories; indirect effects are the ramp-up productivity loss plus the promotion-pipeline blockage that deep vacancies cause (upper levels cannot be filled when the levels below them are empty). The decision bands for interpreting the vacancy rate are: normal <=10%, crisis 15-20%, operational emergency >20-25% unfilled.

### Modeling Process

Simulate c=0.25 and c=0.35 (mid = 2c) over 2 and 4 years with external recruiting at the 33/yr cap. Fill_y, Prod_y, and the 2-year budget are read off each run. Sustainability criterion: can fill_y >= 0.80 be held? Cost delta vs base: the change in (salary + training + recruiting) over 2 years. Indirect effect: Prod_y vs fill_y gap (ramp-up) plus the number of upper-level vacancies that cannot be backfilled internally because their donor levels are themselves short.

### Outcome Analysis

ICM cannot sustain 80% full at either rate. At 25% churn, fill is 0.719 (yr1) and 0.578 (yr2) — already below 80% in year 1 and deep in the emergency band (>20% unfilled) by year 2, declining to ~0.40 by year 4. At 35% churn, fill is 0.603 (yr1) and 0.446 (yr2) — 55% vacant by year 2, ~76% vacant by year 4. The root cause is the capacity gap: at 25% churn the outflow is ~85/yr vs 33/yr backfill, a net -52/yr; at 35% it is ~108/yr vs 33/yr, net -75/yr. Costs: the 2-year all-in budget is 928.0 sigma at 25% and 721.0 sigma at 35%, lower than the 991.5 sigma base case only because fewer people are left to pay — the organization is shrinking, not saving. Indirect effects: (a) ramp-up costs a constant ~4.5 pp of effective capacity each year on top of the vacancy loss; (b) the promotion pipeline jams — with the employee levels ~40-45% vacant, the supervisor and manager levels cannot be refilled internally, so leadership depth erodes and the company loses the very mid-level talent it cannot replace; (c) chronic overtime and deferred work absorb part of the headcount gap but the ramp-up capability loss cannot be bought back. Verdict: 80% full is not sustainable at 25% or 35% under the current 9% recruiting capacity; sustaining it would require roughly doubling recruiting throughput or cutting churn at the source (the mid-level advancement problem). Limitation: the discrete annual model overstates how fast the gap compounds in year 1 (recruiting lag is not intra-year smoothed) and does not model a deliberate retention intervention, so the decline shown is the no-intervention baseline. Bias: conservative on the positive side — it assumes full recruiting capacity is always available, so a real constrained hire process would decline faster.

### Problem

Simulate the impact of 30% churn in both junior managers and experienced supervisors (other levels at 18%) under (1) no external recruiting and (2) promoting only qualified employees, over the next two years, and explain the impact on HR health (task 5). Scope: set junior-manager and exp-supervisor churn to 0.30, all other levels to 0.18, external recruiting off, and promotion restricted to qualified (surplus-pool) candidates; report the two-year trajectory and interpret HR health.

### Analysis

This isolates the mid-management crisis: the two levels that the brief flags as 'stuck' and as suffering double turnover are pushed to 30%, while the backfill levers are deliberately removed (no external hires; only qualified internal promotion). The point is to show what happens to the organization's health when the exact population that is hardest to replace bleeds out and the company tries to hold itself together purely from the inside. HR health is assessed on four dimensions: overall fill, mid-level fill specifically, the promotion pipeline's viability (can the senior and supervisor levels still be filled from below?), and the budget (with recruiting at zero, only salary + training remain).

### Modeling Process

Simulate with mid_override = {junior mgr: 0.30, exp sup: 0.30}, all other levels at 0.18, external=False, promote_all=False (qualified-only: a level promotes only from its donor level's surplus above the 85% baseline, and the donor must be at/above its baseline to give). Two-year fill, per-level fill, and the salary+training budget are read off. A contrast run with promotion allowed from any surplus (promote_all=True) and a run with external recruiting on bound the effect of the two constraints.

### Outcome Analysis

With no external recruiting and qualified-only promotion, overall fill falls 0.85 -> 0.700 (yr1) -> 0.532 (yr2): 47% of all positions vacant by year 2, deep in the operational-emergency band. The damage is concentrated exactly where the stress was applied — junior managers drop to 8 of 20 filled and experienced supervisors to 13 of 25 — and it propagates downward: inexp supervisors fall to 9 of 25 and the employee levels to ~55-60% filled, because with no external hires the only source of new people is internal movement, and the qualified-only rule plus the 85%-baseline floor leaves almost no surplus to promote. The promotion pipeline is effectively frozen: by year 2 no level has the surplus to fill the one above it, so the organization cannot rebuild its own middle from the inside. The 2-year budget is 762.5 sigma (salary 655.9 + training 106.6, recruiting 0) — lower than the base 991.5 sigma purely because the organization has shrunk by ~115 people. Interpretation for the HR supervisor: this scenario is not survivable as a steady state. Removing external recruiting while the hardest-to-replace middle drains at 30% converts a manageable mid-level turnover problem into a company-wide staffing emergency within two years, and the qualified-only promotion rule — correctly applied — cannot rescue it because there is no qualified surplus left to promote. HR health degrades on every dimension: fill, mid-level depth, pipeline viability, and (implicitly) morale, since the remaining staff absorb the vacancies. The constructive reading: the scenario's value is that it quantifies how much external recruiting and a working promotion pipeline are worth — the contrast run with external recruiting on holds fill to 0.689 by year 2 instead of 0.532, a 15.7 pp gap, which is the price of the recruiting function the brief is asking to protect. Limitation: the model does not capture a morale feedback loop (bad health begets more churn), so the decline shown is a lower bound on how fast the scenario would actually deteriorate; and 'qualified-only' is proxied by the surplus-floor rule, which is a structural stand-in for the experience requirements the brief mentions. Bias: conservative in that it gives the organization every internal backfill it is entitled to under the qualified rule; a stricter reading of 'qualified' would decline faster.

## Subtask 6: Summarize the potential use of team science and multi-layered networks in fulfilling the HR manager's vision of connecti

### Problem

Summarize the potential use of team science and multi-layered networks in fulfilling the HR manager's vision of connecting the human-capital network to other organizational layers (information flow, trust, influence, friendship) (task 6). Scope: a qualitative synthesis that maps the model built in tasks 1-5 onto a multi-layer network framework and explains what each additional layer would add.

### Analysis

The HR manager's vision is that the HR office lead the integration of several organizational networks that other offices are building. The model from tasks 1-5 is currently a single layer (the reporting/churn layer) with a placeholder influence edge weight. The team-science and multi-layer-network literature provides the structure for generalizing it: the organization is a set of coupled networks sharing the same node set (the 370 employees) but with different edge sets — reporting structure (built), information flow, trust, influence, friendship — and the churn dynamics live in the coupling between them. The value of the HR office leading this is that churn is the one phenomenon that is driven by all the other layers simultaneously (a person leaves because of influence, lost trust, blocked information, or a broken friendship as much as pay), so the HR network is the natural hub that must integrate the others to predict it.

### Modeling Process

Formally, replace the single edge-weight layer of task 1 with a multilayer graph: nodes V (370 employees), and for each layer alpha in {reporting, information, trust, influence, friendship} an adjacency A^alpha. The churn hazard of node v in year t becomes a function of its state across all layers: c_v(t) = c_base(level(v)) + sum_alpha beta_alpha x (coupling term of v in layer alpha at t), where the coupling terms are, e.g., for influence the fraction of v's influence-layer neighbors who left last year (the contagion channel of task 2 promoted to a named-edge quantity), for trust the decline in v's trust-layer centrality after a neighbor's departure, and for information the loss of v's information-broker role (betweenness) when a key connector leaves. The beta_alpha weights are the layer importances to be calibrated; the base model of tasks 1-5 is the special case with all beta_alpha = 0 except the reporting layer's structural 2x for middle management. Productivity and the budget of tasks 3-4 then become functions of the multilayer state, not just the per-level fill.

### Outcome Analysis

What each added layer contributes to the HR manager's vision: (1) the influence layer turns the placeholder contagion weight of task 2 into a measurable, named-edge quantity, so the 'churn diffuses from employee to employee' claim becomes testable and the sensitivity (kappa=0.3 giving -3.8 pp fill) becomes a calibrated parameter; (2) the trust layer captures why replacing a person costs more than their salary — the informal trust ties they held do not transfer to the hire, which is the indirect loss the ramp-up term of task 2 approximates but the trust layer would localize to specific nodes; (3) the information-flow layer identifies the brokers whose departure disconnects parts of the organization (a betweenness-based risk score), giving the HR office a targeted retention list beyond the mid-level 2x rule; (4) the friendship layer captures the small-team cohesion that drives the 'team science' the supervisor invokes — teams that are tight friendship clusters either churn together (contagion amplified within the cluster) or are resilient (the cluster absorbs a departure). The HR office is the right leader because it owns the reporting layer and the churn outcome that all the other layers predict, and because the integration is a data-ownership problem as much as a modeling one. Practical path: keep the level-compartment core of tasks 1-5 as the validated backbone, then add one layer at a time, calibrating its beta_alpha against observed churn, so each new layer is justified by a measurable improvement in churn prediction rather than by theoretical appeal. Limitation: this is a synthesis, not a run — the multilayer model is not simulated here because the other offices' layer data do not exist in the supplied dataset, and the beta_alpha weights would be unfalsifiable guesses without them. Bias: the framing privileges the HR/churn layer as the hub, which is exactly the HR manager's position and so carries her prior; a neutral reading would treat the layers as peers and let the data pick the hub.
