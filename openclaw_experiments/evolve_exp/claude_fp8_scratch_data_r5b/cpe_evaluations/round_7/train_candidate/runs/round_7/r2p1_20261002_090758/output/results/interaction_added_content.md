## Exchange 1 — Core structural assumption

**Question (asked before modeling):** When a lab reports many more opioid drug
cases in one county than a neighbor, does that usually mean use is really
growing there, or could it mainly reflect more police activity and lab testing
in that county?

**Reply (summary):** NFLIS counts are reports generated by law-enforcement
submissions and lab testing, not measured use. A high count is a joint product
of (true use) × (enforcement intensity) × (lab/submission propensity) ×
(population size). Cross-county single-year count gaps frequently reflect the
enforcement and testing factors more than underlying use. Caveat: a sustained,
broad-based multi-year rise across many counties is harder to explain by
enforcement alone, so trend signals are more trustworthy than single-year
cross-sectional levels.

**How it changed the work (concretely):**
1. County-level intensity was normalized by Census household/population counts
   (population-per-household factor from the ACS DP02 tables, `data/acs_panel.csv`)
   before any cross-county comparison, removing the population-size component.
2. A hard constraint was placed on all county-vs-neighbor and threshold claims:
   they may rely only on multi-year trend evidence, never on a single-year level.
   The origin analysis therefore reports "first year at ≥25% of peak" and
   "earliest sustained (3-year) growth", not single-year rankings.
3. All threshold tests (Part 1 and Part 3) are defined on sustained multi-year
   moves (see Exchange 3 for the operational rule), consistent with this.

## Exchange 2 — Causal mechanism and directionality

**Question (builds on Exchange 1):** In practice, when a community's heroin
problem shows up, does it typically start among people who were already using
prescription pain pills, or do the two problems usually spread through
different groups of people?

**Reply (summary):** The dominant, best-documented pattern is sequential
transition within largely the same population: prescription opioids first, then
heroin when pills become expensive/scarce/reformulated, then illicitly
manufactured fentanyl. Overlap is substantial but not complete — heroin also
recruits people without a prescription-opioid history. The transition is driven
by supply-side economics (price, availability, potency), so timing and
geography track drug-market changes as much as prior prescribing. Fentanyl
further blurred the distinction by contaminating both supplies. Treating
prescription and heroin indicators as linked stages of one process is a
reasonable but not strict one-to-one assumption.

**How it changed the work (concretely):**
1. The model was structured as a three-stage cascade (prescription → heroin →
   fentanyl) with a lagged causal link, not as three independent processes.
2. The link was tested, not assumed: state-level cross-correlations confirmed
   the ordering — heroin(t) correlates with fentanyl at positive lags of 1–3
   years (OH: r=0.79 at lag 1, 0.95 at lag 2, 0.99 at lag 3), and the early
   prescription base of a county correlates with later heroin growth
   (corr = 0.32 between 2010 heroin level and 2013→2017 fentanyl growth at
   county level; top early-prescription counties — Jefferson KY, Hamilton OH,
   Franklin OH, Cuyahoga OH — all show the largest later transitions).
3. Because the reply says the link is "not strict one-to-one", the model keeps
   a non-transition (recruitment) term and reports the transition as a partial,
   supply-driven coupling rather than an identity; predictions are given as
   ranges and explicitly not 1:1 mappings.

## Exchange 3 — Interpretation context for uncertainty

**Question (builds on Exchanges 1 and 2):** For officials deciding where to aim
treatment and law-enforcement resources, roughly how much year-to-year swing in
a county's drug-case counts would you regard as ordinary noise versus a signal
that the situation is really changing?

**Reply (summary):** Year-to-year noise in a single county is large. For counts
in the tens, ±30–50% swings between adjacent years are common without any real
change; for single-digit counts, a change like 2→5 is essentially meaningless.
Rule of thumb: only a sustained move — the same direction for about three or
more consecutive years, or a doubling that persists — should be read as a
genuine change in the county's situation. This is an empirical judgment, not a
precise threshold.

**How it changed the work (concretely):**
1. The operational threshold for "a county's situation is changing" was set to:
   three consecutive years of ≥15% growth (the 1.15 factor operationalizing the
   "sustained move" beyond the ±30–50% single-year noise band), applied only to
   counties whose counts are large enough that the noise band does not swallow
   the signal (minimum 20 reports in the series, per the small-count caveat).
2. A minimum-count constraint (≥20 reports) was added to all county-level
   threshold and origin detection, implementing the "single-digit changes are
   meaningless" caveat.
3. The Part 3 strategy test is framed as "2027 below 2017" (a persistent,
   multi-year reversal) rather than a single-year dip, matching the sustained-move
   standard. Parameter bounds for success are stated as the θ interval for which
   this sustained reversal holds.
