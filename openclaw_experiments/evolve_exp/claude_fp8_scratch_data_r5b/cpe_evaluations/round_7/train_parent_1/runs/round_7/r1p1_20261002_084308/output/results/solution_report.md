## Subtask 1: Part 1. Build a mathematical model of the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid and heroin cases acros

### Problem

Part 1. Build a mathematical model of the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid and heroin cases across the five named states and their counties over 2010-2017; identify where specific opioid use likely started in each state; state the drug-identification thresholds at which government concern should trigger, where and when the model predicts those thresholds are crossed in the future, and what concerns follow.

### Analysis

The supplied MCM_NFLIS_Data.xlsx 'Data' sheet covers only four of the five named states - Ohio, Kentucky, West Virginia and Virginia. Tennessee has zero rows in the file (confirmed: the file's State column contains only OH, VA, KY, PA, WV), so Tennessee cannot be modeled from the supplied data and is documented as a data-coverage gap rather than silently dropped; Pennsylvania, also in the file, is outside the five named states and is excluded. Data cleaning: substance names were trimmed (trailing spaces in the source, e.g. 'Desmethylprodine '), and three analytically meaningful categories were defined from SubstanceName - (i) heroin (SubstanceName=='Heroin'), (ii) the fentanyl class / synthetic opioids (any name containing 'fentanyl', 60+ entries), and (iii) traditional synthetic/semi-synthetic narcotics (oxycodone, hydrocodone, morphine, hydromorphone, oxymorphone, methadone, buprenorphine, tramadol, meperidine, pethidine, pentazocine, methorphan, codeine, dihydrocodeine, propoxyphene, dextropropoxyphene, desmethylprodine, nalbuphine, butorphanol). DrugReports were summed per county-year per category, then a full county-year grid (320 counties x 2010-2017) was built so that a missing county-year is an explicit zero, not a dropped row. Key structural assumption from expert consultation (Exchange 1): a zero is an ascertainment/reporting artifact, not true absence - labs submit voluntarily and low counts are dominated by detection noise - so all series are 2-year smoothed (rolling mean, min_periods=1) before any growth or ignition test, and fentanyl (near-zero 2010-2012 everywhere) is treated with an emergence rule rather than a baseline-exceedance rule. The spread is assumed to propagate as lagged spatial diffusion (Exchange 2), with a 1-3 year neighbor lag (median 1) measured directly from the data (Exchange 2). Method choice is sound because it works only from the supplied columns, is reproducible, and separates the genuine long-run signal (growth rate, diffusion lag, epicenter) from reporting noise (smoothing).

### Modeling Process

Variables: C(s,k,t) = reported DrugReports for county k in state s at year t, for category in {heroin, fentanyl, trad_syn}. (A) State-level growth model: S(s,c,t)=sum_k C(s,k,c,t); fit compound growth S(s,c,t)=S0(s,c)*exp(r(s,c)*t) by ordinary least squares on ln(S) over years with S>0; r(s,c) is the annual compound growth rate (the fitted slope), S0 the level. (B) County emergence/epicenter: smooth c_hat=rolling2(C); for fentanyl, state emergence year e(s,c)=first t with S(s,c,t)>=max(0.05*S(s,c,2017),10) and S(s,c,t)>=1.5*S(s,c,t-1); the origin/epicenter is the county with maximum c_hat in e+1. For heroin/trad_syn (present since 2010) the epicenter is the county with maximum c_hat in the state's peak year. (C) Spatial diffusion: build a county graph G=(V,E) with edges to the k=6 nearest same-state county centroids; for each county cross-correlate its smoothed series with the neighbor-mean series at lags L=0..3; the dominant lag is the diffusion lag (measured median=1 yr, mean=0.95). (D) Threshold: define the state concern threshold T(s,c) as the 95th percentile of S(s,c,t) over 2010-2017 (a level the state has historically rarely reached). (E) Forecast: project S(s,c,t) for t=2018..2021 by S(t)=S(t0)*exp(r(s,c)*(t-t0)) using r fitted on the growth phase (2014-2017 for fentanyl, full series otherwise); the crossing year is the first forecast t with S>=T. (F) Warning window: per Exchange 3, a crossing is actionable 2-3 years in advance; because the last data year is 2017, a 2018 crossing means the warning window is already open. All constants come from the data or the three exchanges: smoothing window=2 yr, emergence floor=5% of 2017, neighbor k=6, diffusion lag median=1 yr [1,3], threshold=95th pct, warning lead=2-3 yr, horizon cap=2021.

### Outcome Analysis

Growth rates (annual compound, r; x/exp factor): FENTANYL is the fast riser - OH r=+0.99/yr (x2.68/yr), KY r=+0.79 (x2.20), WV r=+0.62 (x1.86), VA r=+0.59 (x1.81). HEROIN is much slower and mixed - KY r=+0.24, VA r=+0.14, OH r=+0.10, WV r=-0.01 (flat/declining). TRADITIONAL synthetic narcotics are declining in every state - KY r=-0.16, WV r=-0.17, VA r=-0.05, OH r=-0.01 - the prescription era is contracting while the illicit fentanyl era is expanding. Origins: fentanyl emerged 2013 in VA and 2014 in OH, KY, WV; epicenters (leading counties): OH - Hamilton/Cuyahoga (the Cincinnati and Cleveland metros), KY - Campbell/Kenton/Fayette/Boone (the Lexington area), VA - Fairfax/Henrico (the Northern Virginia/D.C. suburbs), WV - Kanawha/Wood (Charleston). Heroin epicenters coincide with the same urban cores (Hamilton, Cuyahoga, Jefferson/Fayette, Henrico, Kanawha). Thresholds (95th pct of state annual totals) and crossing years: fentanyl T: OH=14611, KY=2116, VA=1663, WV=273 - all four states already crossed by 2017 (OH 16728, KY 2636, VA 2008, WV 298 vs their T), so the 2018-2021 forecast is far beyond threshold (e.g. OH fentanyl 2021 ~ 480,700 at the observed rate). Heroin T: KY=4297 (crossed 2014), OH=22482 (2015), VA=4349 (2013), WV=1738 (2013); only KY is forecast to re-cross (2019), OH is approaching (2021 ~22111 vs T 22482), WV is flat below. Main concerns: (1) the fentanyl epidemic is in an exponential phase in all four states and is already above its historical 95th-pct threshold in every state as of 2017, so the 2-3 year warning window is fully open - this is the urgent headline; (2) it is concentrated in the same large urban/suburban counties that already drove the heroin peak, so population-heavy metros carry the highest absolute risk; (3) Tennessee, a named target state, has no supplied data, so any national picture is incomplete. Limitations/biases: DrugReports counts submissions to crime labs, not true incidence, so growth rates confound real spread with increased testing/reporting; the fentanyl 'growth' may overstate true prevalence growth; the k-6 centroid adjacency approximates true contiguity (VA and WV city/counties without coordinates fall back to state centroids); 2-year smoothing biases rapid 1-year spikes; the compound-growth extrapolation is sensitive to the last observation and is unreliable past the 2021 cap (Exchange 3: forecast skill decays beyond ~3 yr); TN absence and PA exclusion bound the generalizability of the five-state claim.

## Subtask 2: Part 2. Using the supplied U.S. Census (ACS 2010-2016, DP02 table) socio-economic data, test whether opioid use or trend

### Problem

Part 2. Using the supplied U.S. Census (ACS 2010-2016, DP02 table) socio-economic data, test whether opioid use or trends-in-use is associated with any of the provided socio-economic variables; if so, modify the Part 1 model to include the important factors.

### Analysis

The ACS_xx_5YR_DP02 tables are the 'Households by type / Relationship / Marital status / ... / Educational attainment / Disability / Place of birth / ...' demographic table. They contain NO income, poverty, vehicle-ownership or unemployment columns - the only usable socio-economic variables are household counts, educational attainment (pop 25+, high-school graduates, bachelor's), disability status, and population (used as a scale proxy via pop25_plus). This bounds what 'association' can mean: we can test household scale, educational attainment, and disability, but not the income/poverty hypotheses the problem lists. Data cleaning: each year's file was parsed (first row = HC header codes, second row = descriptions, then data); because column positions and some descriptions shift slightly between the 2010 layout (599 columns) and the 2013+ layout (611 columns), variables were located by stable description text and, where the Census percent cells are suppressed ('(X)', as for the 2010 education-percent columns), percentages were re-derived from the raw counts (pct_hs_grad = 100*hs_grad/pop25_plus). Rows were kept only for the four covered states (FIPS 39,21,54,51) at county level, yielding 2776 county-year records (397 counties). Association test: join NFLIS to ACS on county FIPS + year (FIPS, not name, because Virginia city/county display names differ), compute per-capita rates (10,000 x category/pop25_plus), and report Spearman correlations both cross-sectionally (2016) and pooled (2010-2016), plus a log-log scale check and an OLS / partial-correlation control for household scale.

### Modeling Process

Let H(k) = total households, E1(k)=pct high-school graduates (25+), E2(k)=pct bachelor's (25+), D(k)=pct disability (available 2013-2016 only), and P(k)=pop25_plus (population scale proxy). Rates: R_f(k,t)=10000*fentanyl(k,t)/P(k), R_h(k,t)=10000*heroin(k,t)/P(k), R_a(k,t)=10000*(fentanyl+trad_syn)(k,t)/P(k). Tests: (1) cross-sectional Spearman rho between each X in {H,E1,E2} and each R at 2016 (n=393 counties); (2) pooled Spearman rho over all 2010-2016 county-years (n=2755); (3) scale decomposition: Spearman rho between ln H and ln R, and OLS ln R = b0 + b1*ln H + b2*E1 + b3*E2; (4) partial correlation of E1,E2 with R controlling ln H. Model modification: because household scale (a population/size effect, not a socio-economic risk factor) dominates the raw association, the Part 1 model is modified by (i) reporting per-capita rates R in addition to raw counts, and (ii) conditioning the spatial-diffusion and threshold analysis on population scale so that 'hotspot' ranking is not driven purely by big counties. The important factor that survives the scale control is educational attainment (weakly), recorded as a bounded covariate.

### Outcome Analysis

The strong raw association is a SCALE effect, not a socio-economic risk factor: total households correlates with per-capita opioid rates at Spearman rho = +0.54 (fentanyl), +0.51 (heroin), +0.25 (all-synthetic) in 2016, and rho=+0.50-0.54 in log-log - large counties have more cases partly because they have more people and more labs/reporting. After controlling for household scale (partial correlation, 2016), educational attainment shows a weak but consistent signal: high-school-grad rate correlates positively with opioid rates (partial rho +0.15 fentanyl, +0.18 heroin, +0.13 all-synthetic) and bachelor's rate negatively (partial rho -0.12 fentanyl, -0.09 heroin, -0.29 all-synthetic) - i.e., counties with less higher-education have modestly higher per-capita use, but the effect sizes are small (|partial rho| < 0.3). Disability (2013-2016 only) and the raw pooled correlations (rho up to 0.49 for scale) reinforce that scale dominates. Conclusion for the model: the dominant 'association' is population scale, so per-capita rates and scale-conditioned hotspot ranking were added to the Part 1 model; educational attainment enters only as a weak secondary covariate. Limitations/biases: the DP02 table lacks the income, poverty, unemployment and vehicle variables that the problem's competing hypotheses actually implicate, so the null result for income/poverty is a limitation of the supplied data, not evidence that those factors are irrelevant; per-capita rates use pop25_plus (adult population) as the denominator, which slightly understates rates in counties with large youth populations; disability is missing for 2010-2011 (Census layout), so its time series is short; the small partial correlations for education mean socio-economic differentiation is weak in this region, and the scale confound cannot be fully removed without an independent population/assay-intensity covariate, which the data do not provide.

### Problem

Part 3. Combine the Part 1 and Part 2 results to identify a possible strategy for countering the opioid crisis, test the strategy's effectiveness with the model, and identify the significant parameter bounds that success (or failure) depends on.

### Analysis

The Part 1 finding is that fentanyl is in an exponential phase (r = +0.57 to +0.99/yr) and has already crossed its historical 95th-percentile threshold in all four states by 2017; Part 2 shows the risk concentrates in large-population counties and that educational attainment is a weak secondary signal. The strategy therefore targets the fastest-growing threat (fentanyl), in the largest counties (where absolute cases and overdose burden concentrate), triggered inside the 2-3 year warning window (Exchange 3) rather than after a threshold is crossed. Because the 95th-pct threshold is already exceeded in 2017, 'stay below the threshold' is infeasible as a 2021 target; the decision-relevant goal is instead CONTAINMENT - bounding how far the 2021 level compounds above the 2017 level. Effectiveness is tested by treating the countermeasure (early interdiction + diversion of users to treatment, which acts by reducing the effective growth rate) as a fraction delta cut to the compound growth rate r, and solving for the delta that holds 2021 to a chosen multiple of the 2017 level.

### Modeling Process

Let r(s) be the state fentanyl growth rate from Part 1 and S17(s) the 2017 level (backed out as S17 = proj(2018)/exp(r)). Without action, S(2021)=S17*exp(4r). With a countermeasure that cuts the growth rate by fraction delta (0<delta<1), S(2021;delta)=S17*exp(4r(1-delta)). Success target: hold S(2021;delta) <= M*S17 for a chosen containment factor M (M=2 for 'double the 2017 level', M=3 for 'triple'). Solving gives the minimal effective growth-rate cut delta_min(M,s) = 1 - ln(M)/(4*r(s)). The binding parameter bounds: (i) delta_min must be achievable by the countermeasure; if the maximum feasible cut delta_max < delta_min, the target M fails. (ii) The result is insensitive to the exact trigger year inside the 2-3 yr window because the threshold is already crossed (the constraint is magnitude, not timing). (iii) The bound is tightest (easiest) where r is smallest (WV) and hardest where r is largest (VA/OH), and it scales with 1/ln(M): a looser target (M=3 vs M=2) materially lowers the required cut. (iv) A second bound comes from the 2021 forecast cap: extrapolation beyond 4 yr is unreliable (Exchange 3), so success is only defined through 2021.

### Outcome Analysis

Results - minimal effective growth-rate cut to hold 2021 fentanyl to the target: (M=2, double the 2017 level): OH 79.4%, VA 79.8%, KY 78.4%, WV 69.8%. (M=3, triple the 2017 level): OH 67.3%, VA 68.0%, KY 65.8%, WV 52.1%. Without any action the 2021 fentanyl level compounds to roughly 28x (KY 65,745), 28x (VA 62,405), 6x (WV 2,957) and 28x (OH 480,713) the 2017 level at the observed rates - i.e., unmitigated, the crisis multiplies by an order of magnitude within four years. The significant parameter bound is the growth-rate cut: success in containing 2021 to 2-3x the 2017 level requires a 52-80% effective reduction in the fentanyl growth rate, and this is the single number the strategy hinges on. Two important caveats frame the bound: (1) a growth-rate cut is an accounting proxy - real interventions (naloxone, treatment capacity, interdiction) reduce mortality and incidence with lags and partial effects, so the 'effective cut' is a policy target, not a guarantee; whether 52-80% is achievable is exactly the empirical question the bound exposes. (2) The model's effectiveness is bounded by the same assumptions as Part 1: if the reported 'growth' partly reflects increased lab testing rather than true spread, the required cut is overstated; conversely, if true spread is faster than reported, it is understated. Limitations: the target multiple M is a chosen decision parameter, not derived from the data; the strategy is tested only through 2021 (the forecast cap); Tennessee is absent; and the model does not capture feedback (e.g., diversion shrinking the fentanyl supply and altering r endogenously), which would make the required cut path-dependent rather than a fixed fraction.
