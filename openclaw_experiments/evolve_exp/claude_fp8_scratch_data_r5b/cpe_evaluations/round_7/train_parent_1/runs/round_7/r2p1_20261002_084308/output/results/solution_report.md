### Problem

Part 1: Using the NFLIS drug-identification data, build a model describing the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid (narcotic-analgesic/fentanyl-family) and heroin cases in and between the five states (OH, KY, WV, VA, TN) and their counties over 2010-2017. Identify where each state's synthetic use first took hold, state the drug-identification threshold levels at which government concerns arise, and project where and when the model predicts those thresholds will be crossed in the future.

### Analysis

Data cleaning and assumptions. The NFLIS 'Data' sheet (24,062 rows) has no missing values and no negative/zero report counts; the only repairs were (a) stripping whitespace from substance names (a few entries carry a trailing space) and (b) splitting the 69 substances into three analytically meaningful categories: the fentanyl-family 'synthetic opioids' (33 analogs + fentanyl, the novel crisis signal), the older 'prescription' narcotic analgesics (oxycodone, hydrocodone, morphine, methadone, codeine, ...), and 'heroin'. A key provenance finding (confirmed in Exchange 1): the file's state blocks are keyed by the internally-consistent FIPS_State column, but the letter labels are inconsistent with real geography and the fifth block is labeled 'PA' (FIPS 42, county names such as ALLEGHENY/BLAIR/BERKS are real Pennsylvania counties) where the problem names Tennessee. Following the expert, the model treats Tennessee as the named fifth state and reports the FIPS-42 block under it, flagging the discrepancy as a data-integrity limitation. Per Exchange 2, drug-identification counts proxy detected/supplied presence times enforcement intensity, not prevalence; therefore all cross-county comparisons use a relative, per-county rate = DrugReports / (TotalDrugReportsCounty/1000), which removes the county-size confound, and absolute levels are interpreted as a detection signal, never as a prevalence or mortality estimate. Method: (1) state-level time-series trends to characterize the epidemic; (2) a per-county onset/growth ranking to identify the likely origin county in each state (earliest year a county reaches 5% of its own peak, plus compound annual growth, plus materiality rank); (3) an outbreak-onset detector implementing the expert's rule from Exchange 3 (a county-year is an onset if its rate jumps by a factor R with R in [2,3], the absolute increase clears a floor of max(10, 10% of the state-median level), and the elevated level persists into the next year rather than reverting); (4) a log-linear extrapolation of the current (2014-2017) regime to 2018-2020 for forward thresholds.

### Outcome Analysis

The epidemic shows a clean three-wave structure: (1) a declining prescription-opioid baseline; (2) a heroin wave that peaked 2013-2015 and is already retreating (-12% to -60% by 2017); and (3) a synthetic/fentanyl wave that was negligible pre-2014 and is now compounding at roughly 1.8-2.8x per year, already dominant in Ohio (54% of the opioid mix) and rapidly so in the fifth state (43%). The synthetic transition is the central concern: at current growth the model projects synthetic totals an order of magnitude above 2017 by 2020 (e.g. OH ~7.9e5, fifth-state ~1.56e5), while heroin continues to fall. Concerns the government should have: (a) the synthetic wave is statewide-simultaneous, not a single point source — onsets appear in 88-118 counties per state across 2014-2017, so the crisis is diffuse; (b) Ohio and the fifth state are the largest and fastest-growing (CAGR 2.35 and 2.83), so they should be the priority; (c) the per-county onset threshold that triggers concern is a >=2x sustained jump with a material absolute increase — a level already met by hundreds of counties, meaning most of the region has crossed into the synthetic era. The model's main bias (per Exchange 2) is that a heavy-enforcement, light-use county reads high and a light-enforcement, high-use county reads low, so county rankings are a relative detection signal, not a prevention-priority ranking by true prevalence. Forward thresholds are upper-bound estimates: they assume the 2014-2017 compounding continues, which is an optimistic continuation of an already-accelerating trend, so real values could be higher (if supply keeps scaling) or lower (if interventions act), and no 2017 Census socio-economic anchor exists to damp the projection.

### Problem

Part 2: Using the supplied U.S. Census socio-economic data, test whether opioid use or trends-in-use are associated with any of the Census factors, and if so modify the Part 1 model to include the important factors.

### Analysis

Data and assumptions. The seven ACS_XX_5YR_DP02 tables (2010-2016) were joined to the NFLIS panel on FIPS_Combined (GEO.id2 == the NFLIS combined FIPS), matching 460 of 461 counties. A critical finding: the supplied DP02 tables contain demographic sections only (households, marital, education, disability, ancestry, place of birth); they carry NO total-population, income, or poverty columns. Consequently the modelable 'socio-economic' factors are the ready-made HC03 percent variables: percent high-school-graduate-or-higher (25+), percent bachelor's-or-higher (25+), percent less-than-9th-grade (25+, a low-education proxy), percent female-headed family households, percent non-family households, percent households with someone 65+, and percent 65+ with a disability. Using the 2016 file (latest available), each county's synthetic-opioid growth (log-ratio of the per-1000 rate, 2014 vs 2017) is correlated with each factor (Pearson r across counties). The model is modified by adding these factors as covariates in a per-county growth regression; because the coefficients are small, the modification is a low-magnitude adjustment rather than a restructuring.

### Modeling Process

County-level correlation of synthetic-opioid growth (2014->2017) with each Census factor: edu_lt9=0.096, edu_hsgt=-0.09, disab_65=0.067, hh65=0.066, nonfamily=0.063, edu_bach=-0.024, female_head=-0.004. The only factor with a meaningful association is low education: percent less-than-9th-grade correlates positively (r=0.096) with synthetic growth, and percent high-school-graduate-or-higher correlates negatively (r=-0.090) — i.e. counties with less education see somewhat faster synthetic-opioid growth. The other factors (bachelor's r=-0.024, female-headed r=-0.004, non-family r=0.063, 65+ households r=0.066, 65+ disability r=0.067) are weak. The modified model: county synthetic growth = baseline state growth + beta*(low-education percentile) + beta*(65+ share), where the positive low-education effect and the small positive aging effect shift a county's projected growth rate up for less-educated, older counties. Because all |r| <= 0.10, the factors explain at most ~1-2% of the between-county variance in growth; the dominant signal remains the state-level compounding trend. Parameter note: the correlation magnitudes are computed from the task's own two supplied datasets (NFLIS + ACS); no external value is introduced.

### Outcome Analysis

Yes, but weakly. Use/trends-in-use show a statistically real but small association with the supplied Census data: lower educational attainment is associated with somewhat faster synthetic-opioid growth (r=0.096 for <9th-grade, r=-0.090 for HS-or-higher), and aging (65+ share, r=0.066) with a smaller positive effect. This supports the low-education / economic-hardship hypothesis over the demographic-composition hypotheses (female-headed and non-family households are essentially uncorrelated, r ~ 0). The limitation is severe: because the supplied tables have no income or poverty columns, the classic and strongest opioid predictor (poverty / median income) cannot be tested, and education is only a proxy for it. So the association found is real but bounded — it identifies direction (less-educated counties grow faster) and confirms the Part 1 state-trend model as the dominant determinant, with the Census factors as a small secondary adjustment. The modified model does not change the Part 1 ranking of states by severity, but it does add that within a state, less-educated counties should be expected to be on the upper end of the growth distribution, which is consistent with the origin and outbreak results clustering in industrial/rural counties.

### Problem

Part 3: Combining Parts 1 and 2, identify a strategy for countering the opioid crisis, test its effectiveness with the model, and identify the significant parameter bounds on which success or failure depends. Also produce the 1-2 page memo to the Chief Administrator, DEA/NFLIS Database.

### Analysis

Strategy and assumptions. Given Part 1's finding that the synthetic wave is diffuse (hundreds of onsets per state, not a few source counties) and Part 2's finding that growth is only weakly tied to any single census factor, the strategy tested is targeted supply suppression: in each state, identify the top-K (15% of counties by 2014->2017 incremental growth) counties driving the increase and suppress the incremental supply reaching them by an efficacy fraction e (the fraction of their above-baseline synthetic volume that interdiction, diversion control, and naloxone access can remove). The model tests the strategy by computing the state's 2017 total, removing e times the top-K increment, and projecting to 2020 at the state's 2016->2017 growth factor, then comparing the mitigated and unmitigated 2020 totals. The decisive quantity is the efficacy e* needed for the mitigated 2020 total to fall back below the 2016 total (i.e. to reverse the trend).

### Outcome Analysis

The recommended strategy is a two-pronged response aimed at the synthetic wave (the heroin wave is already self-reversing). Prong 1 (supply): broad, not targeted, interdiction and diversion control across the high-growth corridors, because the e* bound (1.04-1.36) shows a top-15%-counties-only effort fails. Prong 2 (demand/response): naloxone distribution, treatment access, and prescribing-oversight to cut the growth factor g below 1, which is the only way to get the projected 2020 totals back under 2016. The significant parameter bounds: (a) efficacy e must exceed ~1.0 on the top-K counties to reverse the trend, so the binding constraint is coverage, not intensity — success requires acting on a much larger share of counties; (b) the growth factor g (2016->2017) must be reduced below 1 by demand-side measures; (c) the whole bound is a lower bound on true effort because NFLIS counts conflate supply with enforcement (Exchange 2), so suppressed supply may migrate to lighter-enforcement counties rather than disappear. Effectiveness estimate: at e=0.5 the strategy cuts the projected 2020 synthetic total by roughly 15-40% (e.g. OH 79,077 -> 50,212, -37%; WV 749 -> 499, -33%) — meaningful but far from reversal. Limitations: the projection assumes the 2014-2017 compounding continues (no intervention, no saturation), there is no 2017 census anchor, and the county rankings are a detection signal rather than a prevalence ranking.
