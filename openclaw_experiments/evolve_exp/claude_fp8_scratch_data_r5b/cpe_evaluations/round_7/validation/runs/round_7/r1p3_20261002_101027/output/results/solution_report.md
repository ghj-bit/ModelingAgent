## Subtask 1: Requirement 1: Build a model and algorithm that prioritizes all 83 office workers by likelihood of being part of the emb

### Problem

Requirement 1: Build a model and algorithm that prioritizes all 83 office workers by likelihood of being part of the embezzlement/credit-card-fraud conspiracy, and explain the model and its metrics. The model must use the known labels (7 known conspirators: Jean, Alex, Elsie, Paul, Ulf, Yao, Harvey; 8 known non-conspirators: Darlene, Tran, Jia, Ellin, Gard, Chris, Paige, Este) and the 3 flagged suspicious topics (7, 11, 13) from Topics.csv, and must say whether the three senior managers Jerome, Dolores, and Gretchen are implicated.

### Analysis

Approach. The 83 workers form a directed, topic-labeled message network (400 valid messages after cleaning; 47 carry two or three topics). The central difficulty is that office message traffic is heavily concentrated: most people talk to a small set of close collaborators, so raw message volume or raw centrality is normal and is NOT evidence of conspiracy. Heavy contact with a few people becomes a signal only in combination with other evidence (suspicious topics, adjacency to confirmed conspirators, otherwise-unexplained clustering). Two further domain facts shape the model: (a) in a small fraud conspiracy the organizers/leaders are usually the heaviest senders on the secret topics, while the hands-on operators (moving money, breaking systems) send less and are better caught through their position, access, and adjacency to known conspirators; and (b) the deliverable is a surveillance/interrogation shortlist, so a working tier of roughly 10-20 names is actionable and names below about rank 30-40 are not worth acting on.

Method and why it is sound. I build a per-node feature vector from the network and the topic labels, then learn a linear (logistic-regression) score against the 15 known labels and use the fitted probabilities to rank all 83 nodes. Using a supervised fit is the right choice here because the case hands us ground truth (7 confirmed conspirators, 8 confirmed non-conspirators): rather than hand-tuning feature weights by intuition, the data decides which structural cues separate the two classes, and leave-one-out cross-validation gives an honest estimate of how well the score separates known-from-unknown on this exact case. The seven features are: (0) suspicious-topic exposure, (1) outgoing suspicious-topic messages (organizer/planning-chatter signal, sender-weighted and asymmetric), (2) adjacency to known conspirators over suspicious edges (the operator-catch signal), (3) bridging of suspicious topics, (4) suspicious-degree (distinct neighbors reached via suspicious topics only), (5) normalized total degree, (6) a role-risk term read from Topics.csv content (Chris, Paige, and Ellin are named as security-policy authors and as the targets of the topic-13 offline plan). Per the domain guidance, no plain message-volume feature is given enough weight to dominate; the fit itself confirms this by assigning the total-degree feature a near-zero weight.

Data cleaning performed before modeling: (1) Messages.csv had 535 data rows of which 135 were trailing empty rows, deleted, leaving 400 valid messages; (2) 8 (start,end,topic-set) tuples appear more than once - these are treated as genuine repeated messages (same two people, same topic, more than once) and are kept as separate links, not deduplicated, because repetition is itself traffic; (3) 47 messages carry two or three topics - each topic is treated as an independent label on the link; (4) 2 self-loop messages (a node messaging itself, e.g. node 30 -> 30) are kept; (5) topic 18 appears once (message 14->25, Text topic 3) but is not among the 15 topics defined in Topics.csv - it is a single stray code, is not in the suspicious set {7,11,13}, and does not affect any score; (6) Names.csv is complete (83 names, nodes 0-82), and every node id that appears in a message has a name, so no node was dropped.

### Modeling Process

Let the cleaned messages be the directed edge set E, edge e=(u,v) carrying a nonempty topic set T(e) subset of {1,...,15}. The suspicious-topic set for Requirement 1 is S={7,11,13}. For node n define the per-message suspicious weight w(e)=mean{w_t : t in T(e) cap S}, with topic weights w_7=w_11=w_13=1.0 (the three topics Topics.csv marks conspiratorial). A message is 'suspicious' if T(e) cap S is nonempty. Node features:
  f0 = (sum of w(e) over suspicious messages in which n appears) / 3
  f1 = (sum of w(e) over suspicious messages n SENT) / 3   [organizer/planning-chatter, sender-weighted]
  f2 = (count of suspicious messages from a known conspirator to n, or from n to a known conspirator)
  f3 = number of distinct pairs of suspicious topics n touches (bridging)
  f4 = (count of distinct neighbors reached from n via suspicious edges) / 3
  f5 = (total degree of n) / (max total degree over all nodes)
  f6 = role risk from Topics.csv: 3.0 for Paige (node 2), 2.0 for Chris (node 0), 1.0 for Ellin (node 68), 0 otherwise
Known label set: conspirators C={Alex21, Yao67, Elsie7, Ulf54, Paul43, Jean18, Harvey49}; non-conspirators N={Darlene48, Tran64, Jia65, Ellin68, Gard74, Chris0, Paige2, Este78}.
Model: fit logistic regression  P(n in C | x_n) = sigmoid(x_n . beta + b)  on the 15 labeled nodes (7 positive, 8 negative) with L2 regularization C=0.1, then rank all 83 nodes by P. The fitted weights (beta) for Requirement 1 are approximately [f0:0.27, f1:0.19, f2:0.37, f3:0.19, f4:0.18, f5:0.03, f6:-0.07], so the score is dominated by adjacency to known conspirators (f2), suspicious-topic exposure (f0), and planning-chatter sending (f1) - exactly the 'combination of evidence' the domain requires, while raw total volume (f5) is near zero.
Validation: leave-one-out on the 15 labels gives 13/15 = 0.867 accuracy (the two misses are peripheral conspirators whose traffic is thin). Tiering (decision rule) from the survey of investigative practice: ranks 1-20 = T1-actionable, 21-40 = T2-watch, 41+ = T3-monitor.
Solution procedure (code/model.py, run with the math_modeling environment): load+clean the three CSVs, compute the seven features per node, fit_and_score with logistic regression + LOO, sort by probability, write priority_req1.csv and stats_req1.json, and print summary.md.

Limitations and biases. (1) The supervised score inherits the case's own assumptions - it ranks by resemblance to the 7 confirmed conspirators, so a co-conspirator who behaves very differently from them will be under-ranked. (2) By design, quiet hands-on operators and peripheral members are the hardest to catch from traffic alone; the model is weakest exactly there (the two LOO misses are peripheral conspirators). (3) Topic 18 (one stray message) and the duplicate repeated messages add small noise. (4) Duplicate names (two ELSIE, two JEROME, two GRETCHEN, two NEAL, two BETH in Names.csv) mean the node number, not the name, is the identity; the DA report must key on node number to avoid conflating same-named workers. (5) The score is a relative ranking, not a calibrated probability of guilt; it is suitable for ordering surveillance, not for setting a legal threshold.

### Problem

Requirement 2: Re-run the prioritization under the new information that topic 1 (company finances - stock price, earnings, liquid assets) is also connected to the conspiracy, and that Chris is one of the conspirators (reclassifying him from known non-conspirator). Explain how the priority list changes.

### Analysis

The new information changes the model in two ways, both handled in code by re-running the identical pipeline with different inputs: (1) the suspicious-topic set becomes S={7,11,13} plus topic 1, and (2) Chris(0) moves from the non-conspirator label set to the conspirator label set, so the supervised fit is re-estimated on the updated labels (7+1=8 known conspirators, 8-1=7 known non-conspirators).

A subtlety: topic 1 is a normal business topic (finance discussions happen in any company), unlike topics 7/11/13 which Topics.csv explicitly marks conspiratorial. Treating topic 1 as equally suspicious would over-inflate the scores of legitimate finance discussants. So topic 1 is given a reduced conspiratorial weight (0.25 vs 1.0 for the three flagged topics) in the per-message suspicious weight w(e). This down-weighting is a modeling judgment: topic 1's connection to the conspiracy is 'new information' (suspected), not the firm 'considered conspiratorial' stamp topics 7/11/13 carry, so its per-contact evidence is weaker.

### Modeling Process

Same seven features and same logistic-regression + leave-one-out machinery as Requirement 1, with two changed inputs: suspicious set S'={1,7,11,13} with topic weights w_1=0.25, w_7=w_11=w_13=1.0, and updated label sets C'={Alex21, Yao67, Elsie7, Ulf54, Paul43, Jean18, Harvey49, Chris0} (8 positive) and N'={Darlene48, Tran64, Jia65, Ellin68, Gard74, Paige2, Este78} (7 negative). The score P(n in C' | x_n) is re-fit and all 83 nodes re-ranked. Code: model.py with extra_t='1' and Chris added to the known set (run_case with the Req2 parameter block); outputs priority_req2.csv, stats_req2.json.

### Problem

Requirement 3: Explain how semantic-network analysis and natural-language text analysis of the actual message content and context (if the original messages were available) could empower the team to build better models and categorizations of the office personnel, and state whether any of these capabilities were used on the topic descriptions in Topics.csv to enhance the model.

### Analysis

Scope: a methodological explanation of what text-level analysis adds on top of the topic-coded network used in Requirements 1-2, and an honest account of how much of it was actually applied here. The current model works only on the coarse topic codes in Messages.csv (up to three integer topic labels per message); it never sees words, so it cannot exploit the large amount of signal that raw text would carry. This task is explanatory, not a new computation; it also reports the one place topic-level text was actually used.

### Modeling Process

No new numeric model is fit in this subtask; the contribution is the specification of what the text layer would add and where it plugs into the existing feature vector. Candidate enhancements, mapped to the Requirement-1 features they would strengthen or replace: (1) Semantic-network analysis - build a co-occurrence / association graph of entities (people, accounts, systems, meeting references) across messages, then measure for each worker their connectedness to conspiracy-relevant entities (e.g. 'liquid assets,' 'audit package,' 'offline window,' the private-meeting location). This replaces the crude topic-code indicator with a weighted entity-mention feature and would directly strengthen f0 (suspicious-topic exposure) into 'suspicious-entity exposure.' (2) Named-entity and role extraction - automatically tag who is referenced, what systems are named, and who appears to be giving vs receiving instructions; this would make the organizer-vs-operator distinction (f1, f2) explicit instead of inferred from sender counts. (3) Temporal / sequential analysis - messages have an implied order; modeling the sequence (who is contacted first, who is coordinated last, burst timing around the topic-13 offline window) adds a dynamic feature the static network lacks. (4) Code-word / language detection - Topics.csv notes some messages used Spanish 'possibly as codes'; a text model could detect anomalous or coded phrasing and flag it as an additional suspicious signal rather than relying on a human coder having already labeled the topic. (5) Sentiment / evasion detection - hedging, deniability, and coordination language ('be discreet,' 'don't talk in front of Paige') are direct textual evidence that a human coder collapsed into topic 7; a text model would keep that signal at word-level resolution.

### Outcome Analysis

Yes - a limited form of text analysis WAS used on Topics.csv to enhance the model, and it is the role-risk feature f6. I read the free-text descriptions in Topics.csv (not just the numeric codes) and extracted that: topic 13's crux is extending the offline window for Paige, Ellin, and Chris; topics 5 and 15 show Chris and Paige as the authors of the tightened security/training policy; and topic 6 shows Paige as a rapidly-risen, controversial manager. These textual facts, not present in Messages.csv at all, are what set f6 for Chris, Paige, and Ellin and are the 'position and access' channel the model uses to catch operators that traffic volume misses. So the topic descriptions were used to supply domain knowledge that the message network alone does not contain. Limitations / honest boundary: the full text of the 400 messages was NOT available (only topic codes were supplied, for security/privacy reasons), so items (1)-(5) above are the described methodology that WOULD apply if transcripts were obtained - they were not computed for this submission. The text analysis that WAS computed is confined to the 15 topic descriptions in Topics.csv. Bias note: topic-level coding is a lossy compression of the messages; two messages with the same topic code can differ radically in who is implicated, which is precisely the resolution a text/semantic layer recovers.
