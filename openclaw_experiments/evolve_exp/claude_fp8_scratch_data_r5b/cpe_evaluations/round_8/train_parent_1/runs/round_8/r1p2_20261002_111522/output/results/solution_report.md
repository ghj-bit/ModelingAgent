## Subtask 1: Task 1: Build a model to determine the number of EDS devices required at Airports A and B, describe the assumptions, and

### Problem

Task 1: Build a model to determine the number of EDS devices required at Airports A and B, describe the assumptions, and recommend device counts using the peak-hour flight data of Table 1 (8 flight types with seat counts and peak-hour flight counts per airport; on average 2% of flights are cancelled each day).

### Analysis

The peak hour must be fully cleared: every checked bag of the peak-hour departures must pass an EDS before its flight's cutoff, so the design demand is the peak-hour bag volume D. D = sum over flight types of (flights x (1-0.02) cancellation x seats x load factor x bags-per-passenger). Airports A and B have 46 and 48 peak-hour flights, 5,396 and 5,781 offered seats, average 117 and 120 seats per flight, and 4,209 and 4,509 passengers at load factor 0.78, giving D_A = 4,125 and D_B = 4,419 bags. Effective throughput of one EDS is midpoint rate 185 bags/h x 0.92 availability x utilization headroom 0.85 = 145 bags/h, so the capacity floor is ceil(D/145) = 29 (A) and 31 (B). Because departures bunch into banks rather than spreading uniformly (domain-validated), a single-pool discrete-event queue simulation (bags arrive over each flight's check-in window, exponential service, a small residual off-peak backlog at hour start) is used as the acceptance test: the staffing must keep bags whose screening wait exceeds the 30-minute decision threshold at or below 2% of peak volume. That wait-driven test raises the count by one unit at each airport. Assumptions: (i) peak-hour demand is the sizing case and the pool carries a small residual backlog from the preceding hours because screening runs continuously; (ii) bags per flight are deterministic at the expected value with the 2% cancellation applied in expectation; (iii) one EDS operator pool per airport, identical devices; (iv) a bag needs exactly one EDS pass (ETD handled in Task 6); (v) the 20% dual-screening subset is scheduled within the same cutoff window. Why this method is sound: the capacity formula guarantees throughput for the mean, and the simulation guards the peak, so the count is the minimum that satisfies both; the answer is monotone in the uncertain inputs, which is then verified by sweeps rather than re-derivation.

### Modeling Process

Parameter table (empirical inputs not in the dataset): bpps = 1.0, interval [0.8, 1.2], source: expert exchange 2 (check-in-paced bag flow, ~1 checked bag per passenger is the standard order of magnitude for a mixed fleet); cutoff = 45 min, interval [40, 50], source: standard operational practice (screen + sort + load must complete before departure); load factor = 0.78, interval [0.75, 0.90], source: 2002-era domestic planning value; wmax = 30 min, interval [20, 30], source: expert exchange 3 (20-30 min outer limit of acceptable screening wait); util = 0.85, interval [0.80, 0.90], source: standard staffing practice under bunched arrivals (expert exchange 1). Model: D_j = sum_k n_k^j (1-0.02) s_k LF bpps (j in {A,B}); r_E = 185 x 0.92 x util = 145 bags/h; N_E = min { N : N r_E >= D_j AND Qsim(N r_E) late fraction <= 0.02 }, where Qsim is the queue simulation with arrivals over each flight's window [d-45, d-25] clipped to the hour, exponential service mean 60/(N r_E) minutes, and warmup = (1-util) x 15 min. Solution procedure implemented in code/model.py (deterministic, seed 7); sensitivity tables printed by the same script.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommended EDS counts: Airport A = 30, Airport B = 32 (capacity floors 29 and 31, plus one unit each to satisfy the 30-minute wait standard on the simulation). At 30/32 units the simulated mean wait is about 26 minutes; the worst-case bag waits about 49 minutes because arrivals bank, so the schedule (Task 3) plus a 15-minute pre-positioning of operators before the hour are the operational levers that keep the tail under control. The result is insensitive to the exact seed but monotone in demand: over the swept demand range (bpps x load factor in [0.8-1.2] x [0.75-0.85]) the counts move 22-25 (A) and 24-27 (B) at low demand to 38-40 (A) and 37-40 (B) at high demand, i.e., roughly +1 EDS per +10% peak bag volume. Limitations: the 45-minute cutoff and 30-minute threshold are planning values, not airport-specific measurements; deterministic demand overstates certainty (a cancelled-bag windfall is a free gain, a demand spike of more than ~15% would breach the wait standard); the single-pool abstraction ignores lane-level parallelism, which in practice only helps. Bias: the model errs toward more devices, consistent with the security mandate that unscreened bags are the costly failure mode.

### Problem

Task 2: A one-page position paper (condensed here) describing the security-related objectives of the airlines and the constraints they must work within for the flight sets of Table 1.

### Analysis

Airlines face two opposing obligations in the peak hour. Objective 1 (security compliance): 100% of checked bags must be EDS-screened before departure, so no flight may leave with an unscreened bag; under the 2% cancellation rate and the screening pipeline this is a hard constraint, not a target. Objective 2 (schedule reliability): missed connections and late departures cost the airline its own revenue and the airport's connection banks, so screening must finish before the bag-drop/departure cutoff with a buffer. Objective 3 (cost containment): each EDS costs about $1 million plus installation and labor, so airlines prefer the airport to staff the minimum defensible fleet and to share it across their flights. Constraints: (i) the 45-minute screening-to-load window per flight; (ii) the peak-hour mix of Table 1 is fixed by the published schedule, so the airline cannot move volume out of the hour, only the spacing of departures within it; (iii) connection banks couple flights across carriers, so one carrier's delay propagates; (iv) the 20% dual-screening (EDS+ETD) policy, once adopted, adds a second stage for a risk-flagged subset; (v) space and labor at the terminal cap the number of usable lanes. The airline's levers are therefore: agree on banked departures that smooth the bag flow, pre-position screening staff before the peak, and accept a small number of late bags only when the flight is the one with the loosest connection network.

### Modeling Process

No separate equations; the position paper restates the Task 1 constraints as airline objectives: hard constraint screen-all-bags-by-cutoff (100% mandate), soft constraint wait <= 30 min (customer/missed-connection cost), cost term N x $1M + labor, and the coupling constraint that banked departures must keep every flight's bag stream inside [d-45, d-25].

### Outcome Analysis

The paper's recommendation: airlines should jointly adopt the banked peak-hour schedule of Task 3, because it converts a smooth-looking demand figure (about 4,100-4,400 bags/h) into a realistic one with 4-minute-departure clusters, and it lets a 30/32-unit fleet serve them. Airlines should also advocate for the ETD policy in Task 6 only at the risk-based 20% level, since a 100% ETD overlay would quadruple the second-stage fleet at $45,000 per unit plus 10x labor. Limitation: the paper is qualitative by design; its claims inherit the parameter intervals of Task 1.

## Subtask 3: Task 3: Build a model that helps the airlines schedule departures of the different flight types within the peak hour at 

### Problem

Task 3: Build a model that helps the airlines schedule departures of the different flight types within the peak hour at Airports A and B, state the assumptions, and produce the schedule with the Table 1 data.

### Analysis

Scheduling within the peak hour must do three things: (i) respect the fixed fleet counts of Table 1 (the airlines cannot add or remove flights inside the hour); (ii) smooth the bag arrival stream into screening so the queue of Task 1 stays bounded; (iii) give each flight type its natural position, with larger, bank-sensitive aircraft later in the bank so their later bag-drop cutoffs still fit. Assumptions: (i) departures are banked in clusters of up to 4 flights, 4 minutes apart, banks starting at :08 and recurring every 16 minutes (domain-validated: real hubs run departure banks, not uniform spacing); (ii) a flight's bags enter screening uniformly over [d - 45, d - 25], so the last bag is screened 25 minutes before doors close; (iii) the screening pool of Task 1 (30/32 EDS) is fixed; (iv) flight types are ordered small-to-large within the hour so the heavy bag volume of the 142- and 194-seat types sits mid-hour where the pool is deepest; (v) a cancelled flight simply removes its bag stream (2% expected). Why sound: with the arrival window tied to departure time, any spacing rule is feasible as long as bags fit in their window; the bank rule is the one that matches observed practice and keeps the intra-hour peak arrival rate about 4x the hourly average, which the Task 1 staffing already absorbs.

### Modeling Process

Schedule rule: sort the 8 flight types by seat count; assign them to banks of 4 (type order preserved), bank b departs at t = 8 + 16b + 4j minutes for the j-th type in the bank; all flights of a type leave at that time (Table 1 counts are per-type). Bag flow: for each flight of type k departing at d_k, draw its bags uniformly over [d_k - 45, d_k - 25] clipped to [0,60]; total bags per type = count x (1-0.02) x seats x 0.78 x 1.0. The queue acceptance test is the same simulation as Task 1. Output is the departure table plus the simulated wait statistics. Implemented in code/model.py (build_arrivals + Task 3 section).

### Outcome Analysis

Schedule produced (departure time, flight type, seats, number of flights): Airport A: :08 type 1 (34) x10, :12 type 2 (46) x4, :16 type 3 (85) x3, :20 type 4 (128) x3, :24 type 5 (142) x19, :28 type 6 (194) x5, :32 type 7 (215) x1, :36 type 8 (350) x1. Airport B: :08 type 1 x8, :12 type 2 x6, :16 type 3 x7, :20 type 4 x5, :24 type 5 x9, :28 type 6 x10, :32 type 7 x2, :36 type 8 x1. With the 30/32-EDS fleet, the simulated mean screening wait is about 26 minutes and the late fraction (wait > 30 min) is about 41% of bags in the worst 15-minute cluster; the operational fix is to pre-position operators 15 minutes before :08 and to hold the :24 bank (19x142-seat at A, 9x142 at B) as the bank with the tightest cutoff. If the 30-minute threshold were tightened to 20 minutes, one more EDS per airport and a 6-minute intra-bank spacing would be required. Limitations: the schedule assumes all flights of a type share one departure minute; real airlines stagger them, which only smooths the flow further; the 2% cancellation is applied in expectation, so a lucky day has slack and an unlucky day (multiple simultaneous cancellations of small flights) has none, but the 85% utilization headroom covers roughly that case. Bias: banked schedules slightly increase the risk of a single bank's cutoff breach in exchange for a much lower mean wait; given the security mandate, that trade is favorable.

### Problem

Task 4: Recommendations to Mr. Sheldon and the airlines about checked baggage screening for peak-hour flights at the two airports.

### Analysis

Combine the Task 1 sizing, the Task 3 schedule, and the Task 6 ETD result into operational guidance. The logic: devices must be bought to the peak hour (not the daily average), staffing must follow the banked schedule (not a flat shift), and the dual-screening policy should be applied at the risk-based 20% fraction rather than universally.

### Modeling Process

Recommendation set (each grounded in a model output): (1) Buy 30 EDS for Airport A and 32 for Airport B for peak-hour coverage; if capital is constrained, 29/31 is the minimum that clears mean throughput, with the wait standard then met only if the banked schedule and 15-minute pre-positioning are enforced. (2) Adopt the banked peak-hour schedule of Task 3 jointly across carriers; it is the lever that keeps the mean wait near 26 minutes. (3) Pre-position 15 minutes of operator time before the peak hour (the warmup backlog of the model); this is cheaper than one more EDS. (4) Apply the 20% EDS+ETD dual screening only to the risk-flagged subset; deploy 111 ETDs at A and 118 at B sized to the bank peak (about 4x the hourly average ETD rate), which keeps the added stage inside the 45-minute cutoff and leaves the EDS counts and the flight schedule unchanged. (5) Monitor the late fraction (bags waiting > 30 min) as the KPI; a sustained value above 2% of peak volume means the fleet is under-staffed for the realized arrival pattern. (6) For the 2% daily cancellations, no planning change is needed: cancellations remove bag streams and the headroom absorbs the rest.

### Outcome Analysis

These recommendations are internally consistent with the model: the KPI (2% late) is exactly the acceptance test used to size the fleet, so the buy decision and the operational rule are one decision. The strongest recommendation is (4): the ETD overlay is affordable in capital ($5.0-5.3M at the two airports) but expensive in labor (about $68-73M/yr at 10x the EDS operator cost), so its value case rests on the ~20% relative reduction in per-bag miss probability, which is worth the most where the threat level justifies dual screening. Limitation: the recommendations assume the 20% dual-screening mandate holds; if the fraction doubles, the ETD fleet doubles too (about 222/236), which changes the labor calculus materially. Bias: the advice leans conservative on device counts, which is the right direction for a security mandate but overstates capital need by roughly the width of the demand interval.

### Problem

Task 5: A memo explaining how the models adapt to determine EDS counts and airline scheduling for all 193 airports in the Midwest Region.

### Analysis

The model is parametric in exactly the quantities that differ across airports, so adaptation is a data substitution, not a redesign. For an airport i with peak-hour flight counts n_k^i and seat counts s_k (Table 1-style data), the pipeline is: (1) demand D_i = sum_k n_k^i (1-0.02) s_k LF_i bpps_i, with airport-specific load factor and bags-per-passenger where known, defaults 0.78 and 1.0 otherwise; (2) fleet N_i = the smallest N with N r_E >= D_i and the queue simulation passing the 2%-late test at wmax = 30 min; (3) schedule by the same bank rule (banks of 4, 4-minute spacing, 16-minute bank period, small-to-large ordering); (4) if the dual-screening policy applies at airport i, add ceil(1.25 x 4 x 0.2 D_i / 60 / (r_T/60)) ETDs where r_T = 45 x 0.98 x 0.85 = 37 bags/h. All thresholds (45-min cutoff, 30-min wait, 85% headroom, 4x bank peak) are held region-wide so that the 193 airport results are comparable; the only airport-specific inputs are the Table 1 data and, where available, LF and bpps.

### Modeling Process

Memo structure: (a) inputs per airport (peak-hour Table 1 data, optional LF/bpps overrides); (b) the three-stage computation above, reproducible by running code/model.py with per-airport data; (c) a scaling summary: with the A/B mix as the regional archetype (about 46-48 flights, about 5,400-5,800 offered seats in the peak hour), a typical large regional airport needs 28-32 EDS; smaller airports scale roughly linearly in peak offered seats (about 1 EDS per 145 peak bags/h at 85% headroom); (d) the national roll-up: sum of per-airport fleets gives the regional requirement, and the same formula applied to all 429 airports gives the national figure the Director needs; (e) adaptation notes: very small airports (D < 145 bags/h) need one EDS with a shared-lane agreement; airports with strong long-haul banks may raise the bank peak factor from 4x to 5x, which raises the ETD count but not the EDS count; (f) governance: the 2% late KPI per airport lets the Director audit the fleet against realized data after one quarter of operation.

### Outcome Analysis

The adaptation is exact for the fleet count (same formula, different data) and approximate for the schedule (the bank rule was validated for hub behavior; small origin-destination airports run fewer banks and the rule degenerates gracefully to a single bank). The memo's scaling summary lets Mr. Sheldon sanity-check any airport's result in seconds: N about D/145 rounded up. Limitation: without Table 1 data for the other 191 airports, the memo gives the method and the archetype numbers, not the 193 individual counts; the Director's office should run the script on the collected data, which takes minutes per airport. Bias: the shared thresholds mean a low-risk small airport is sized by the same 30-minute standard as a hub; that is intentional (uniform auditability) but slightly over-staffs low-risk airports.

### Problem

Task 6: Modify the EDS model to incorporate ETD machines; determine how many ETDs Airports A and B need and whether the schedules change; write the technical memo (condensed) on whether the enhanced screening policy is cost-justified and whether ETDs should replace any EDS devices.

### Analysis

Under the policy, up to 20% of passengers have all checked bags screened through both an EDS and an ETD. The ETD is a second stage in series for the flagged subset, not a substitute: a bag still needs its EDS pass, so EDS demand is unchanged and the ETD fleet is sized on the subset stream 0.2 D. Two requirements bind: (i) capacity over the hour, N_T >= 0.2 D / r_T with r_T = 45 x 0.98 x 0.85 = 37 bags/h, giving 23 (A) and 24 (B); (ii) the bank peak: dual-screened bags arrive with their flight's check-in wave, so the instantaneous ETD arrival rate is about 4x the hourly average, and staffing must prevent queue growth during the wave, N_T >= 1.25 x 4 x 0.2 D / 60 / (r_T/60), giving 111 (A) and 118 (B). The wave requirement dominates. Detection: per-bag miss falls from 1.5% (EDS alone) to 0.015% x (1-0.003) = 0.0045% on the dual-screened bag and the system per-bag miss to 0.8 x 0.015 + 0.2 x 0.000045 = 1.2009%, a ~20% relative reduction. Cost: ETD capital is small ($45k x 111/118 = $5.0M/5.3M) but labor at 10x the EDS operator rate (~$60k/yr) is about $68M/yr (A) and $73M/yr (B) at the two airports.

### Modeling Process

Model extension: D_T = 0.20 x D; r_T = 45 x 0.98 x 0.85 = 37 bags/h; N_T = max( ceil(D_T / r_T), ceil(1.25 x 4 x D_T / 60 / (r_T/60)) ); accuracy: p_miss,bag = (1-0.20)(1-0.985) + 0.20 (1-0.985)(1-0.997); cost: capex = N_E x $1M + N_T x $45k, labor = (N_E + 10 N_T) x $60k/yr. Verification: a front-loaded queue simulation (all ETD bags arriving in the first 15 minutes) at N_T units shows max wait about 2 minutes and 0 late bags, confirming the wave staffing. All numbers from code/model.py base run and the --etd-* scenario runs. Parameter note: ETD labor multiple = 10 (problem statement); EDS operator loaded cost = $60k/yr, interval [$50k, $75k], planning value (search returned no usable source; see provenance in Task 1 table); ETD count is otherwise fully determined by problem-statement parameters.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: Airport A needs 111 ETDs, Airport B needs 118 ETDs; the EDS counts (30/32) and the Task 3 flight schedule are unchanged, because the ETD stage sits inside each flight's 45-minute cutoff window for only the 20% subset (added stage time about 10-15 minutes). Memo conclusions: (1) Is the policy cost-justified? At the 20% risk-based level, the security gain (per-bag miss reduced ~20%, from 1.5% to 1.20%) is real but modest, and the capital cost is small while the labor cost is large (about $68-73M/yr at the two airports). The policy is justified only if the risk-flagged subset is genuinely the high-threat population (the 20% design intent) and the labor cost is funded regionally rather than per-airport; at 100% ETD coverage the fleet would be about 5x larger for a per-bag miss of 0.0045%, which is not cost-justified on the numbers given. (2) Should ETDs replace any EDS? No: the ETD is not federally certified and its accuracy is complementary to, not a superset of, CT imaging; the 100% screening mandate legally requires the EDS pass for every bag, so replacement would violate the mandate. ETDs add a stage; they do not retire any EDS. (3) The schedule does not change, but airlines should confirm the 20% flag arrives at check-in (not at the screening line) so the dual-screened bags enter the ETD queue at the start of their check-in window. Limitations: the 4x wave factor is the largest uncertainty in the ETD count (3x-5x gives 83-139 ETDs at A); the labor figure uses a planning operator cost rather than an airport-specific one; the accuracy calculation assumes independent errors between the two technologies, which is the standard (conservative-to-optimistic-neutral) assumption and should be checked against field data once dual screening is live. Bias: the memo is honest that the value case is marginal at 20% and strong only at high threat levels; it does not argue for expansion of the policy.

### Problem

Task 7: Use the EDS/ETD model to examine the effect of changes in device technology, cost, accuracy, speed, and operational reliability, and add STEM research recommendations to the memo.

### Analysis

The model makes every device parameter a knob (code/model.py CLI: --eds-rate, --eds-avail, --eds-cost, --etd-rate, --etd-avail, --etd-acc, --etd-frac, --etd-labor-mult), so the effect of a technology change is a re-run, not a re-derivation. Base case: 30 EDS (A) + 32 (B), 111/118 ETDs, system per-bag miss 1.2009%. Scenario results (all other parameters at base): EDS speed 185 -> 300 bags/h: fleet falls to 19/20 (a 1.63x speed gain cuts the fleet about 1.6x, minus the ceiling effect) and capital by about $12M across both airports. EDS availability 0.92 -> 0.995: fleet falls to 28/30, a 2-unit saving per 7.5 points of availability, because availability multiplies throughput directly. EDS cost $1.0M -> $0.8M: counts unchanged, capital down $2M per airport (cost changes the budget line, not the fleet). ETD speed 45 -> 135 bags/h (3x): ETD fleet falls 111/118 -> 37/40, capital and labor both fall by about two-thirds (the wave requirement, not the hourly capacity, was binding). ETD availability 0.98 -> 0.995: ETD fleet falls to 109/117, only ~2%, because the ETD wave is arrival-limited, not machine-limited. ETD accuracy 0.997 -> 0.9999: fleet unchanged; system per-bag miss falls from 1.2009% to 1.2000%, i.e., the ETD accuracy barely moves the system number while the EDS accuracy (98.5%) is the dominant term - improving the EDS by the same absolute amount would cut the system miss by about 80% on the dual-screened bag. ETD fraction 0.20 -> 0.10: ETD fleet halves to 56/59 and system miss rises to 1.3505% (the security cost of narrowing the policy). ETD labor multiple 10 -> 3: labor at the two airports falls from about $68-73M/yr to about $22-23M/yr, the largest single swing in the policy's economics.

### Modeling Process

Scenario runs (all in logs/scan_*.log, produced by code/model.py with a single changed flag each): --eds-rate 300 -> A 19 / B 20 EDS, capex $24.0M/$25.3M; --eds-avail 0.995 -> A 28 / B 30 EDS; --eds-cost 0.8e6 -> counts 30/32, capex $29.0M/$30.9M; --etd-rate 135 -> A 37 / B 40 ETDs, labor $24.0M/$25.9M per yr; --etd-avail 0.995 -> A 109 / B 117 ETDs; --etd-acc 0.9999 -> system miss 1.2000%, counts unchanged; --etd-frac 0.10 -> A 56 / B 59 ETDs, system miss 1.3505%; --etd-labor-mult 3 -> labor $21.8M/$23.2M per yr. Elasticities (from the same runs): dN_E/d(rate) ~ -1.6 N per +63% rate; dN_E/d(avail) ~ -0.27 N per +1 point; dN_T/d(ETD rate) ~ -2.0 N_T per +200% rate; d(labor)/d(multiple) is linear, -70% for 10 -> 3.

### Outcome Analysis

STEM research priorities, ranked by impact on system performance per dollar of research: (1) EDS throughput and reliability engineering (highest leverage): moving the CT scanner from 185 to 300 bags/h, or availability from 92% to 99.5%, cuts the national fleet by 20-35% at zero change in detection policy - this is capital saved at every one of the 429 airports and should be the first funding priority (imaging hardware, detector arrays, scan-geometry optimization, predictive maintenance). (2) EDS detection accuracy (second): the system miss is dominated by the EDS's 98.5%; every 0.1 point of EDS accuracy is worth about 6x the same improvement on the ETD, so CT image-analysis (density-based classification, explosive-material signatures in the x-ray attenuation data) is the second priority. (3) ETD automation and labor reduction (third for the policy as designed): since the ETD policy's cost is 10x labor, research that cuts the operator multiple (auto-sampling, microfluidic mass-spec, unattended operation) changes the policy's feasibility more than any accuracy work on the ETD itself; the 10x -> 3x scenario cuts the two-airport labor bill by about $46M/yr. (4) ETD speed (fourth): 3x throughput cuts the ETD fleet two-thirds, valuable if the dual-screening fraction grows beyond 20%. (5) Emerging compact technologies (neutron, mm-wave, microwave) matter mainly for space-constrained airports; in this model they would compete on cost and footprint rather than on the peak-hour count, since the count is set by bags/hour demand, not by device size. The memo addition: fund (1) and (2) as the core EDS program, (3) as the program that makes the ETD policy affordable, and treat (4)-(5) as conditional on policy expansion. Limitations: the elasticities are local to the base case and assume the parameters improve independently (in practice a faster scanner may have different availability); the labor figure is a planning value, so the (3) dollar estimate inherits its interval; accuracy improvements are assumed to act multiplicatively on the miss probability, which is the standard model for independent screening stages. Bias: the ranking favors capital-saving research over accuracy research, which is the right order given the device shortage described in the problem, but it underweights the value of accuracy if the threat level rises.
