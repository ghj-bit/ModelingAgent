## Subtask 1: Task 1. For each continent (Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, Australia, South America), select the country most crit

### Problem

Task 1. For each continent (Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, Australia, South America), select the country most critical for HIV/AIDS from the WHO member states, and build a model approximating the expected rate of change in the number of HIV/AIDS infections for these six countries from 2006 to 2050 in the absence of additional interventions, with the model, assumptions and country-selection rationale fully explained.

### Analysis

Selection. 'Most critical' is taken as the country carrying the largest HIV/AIDS burden within its continent, using the 1999 UNAIDS count of HIV-positive 0-49 year-olds in hiv_aids_data.xls (the only continent-wide burden series in the package), cross-checked against the time-series prevalence in 'hiv-aids in Africa over time', the 2002 GNP per capita in income_data.xls (poor countries cannot self-fund a response, which raises criticality) and the 2003 ARV-access figures in Appendix 1 (near-zero access where burden is high marks the countries where foreign resources are decisive). Results: Africa - South Africa (4.2 million HIV+, zero ARV access in 2003, low GNP); Asia - India (3.7 million, the largest Asian burden); Europe - Ukraine (the largest European burden, rising general epidemic, low ARV access); North America - United States of America (850 thousand, by far the largest North American burden; Canada and Mexico have only small epidemics); Australia - Australia (the only sovereign of the continent in the dataset; concentrated epidemic, highest GNP per capita of the six); South America - Brazil (largest South American burden, but near-complete ARV access and high GNP, so criticality is lower). Data cleaning: the 1999 sheet contains four aggregate/region rows (e.g. continental and sub-regional totals) which were removed as they are not member states; country labels were harmonized across the nine files (e.g. 'USA' matched to 'United States of America', 'Democratic Rep. of Congo' to 'Democratic Republic of the Congo', 'Cote d'Ivoire' to 'Côte d'Ivoire'); non-numeric cells in the numeric sheets were coerced to missing; in vaccination_rate_data.xls the missing TT2 values were imputed with a 75% regional prior and the missing DTP3 with 90% (noted where used). Modeling approach. A within-cohort (ages 15-49) two-compartment deterministic model per country, run annually 2006-2050, is sound here because (i) the expert exchange established that spread runs through sexual networks in concentrated waves that saturate rather than as steady general contact, so a saturating wave term is the right incidence form; (ii) the only long-horizon quantitative inputs are the 1999 country counts and the UN demographic projections, so the model must be parsimonious in free parameters; (iii) the deliverable is the expected rate of change of the infection count, which the stock equations give directly.

### Modeling Process

State variables per country, annual steps t = 2006,...,2050: I_t = total living HIV+ persons aged 15-49; A_t = subset in AIDS; P_t = 15-49 population from population_data.xls (projections 2006-2050, medium fertility), P_t = 0.63 * Pop_t (15-49 share of the total). Initial conditions: I_2006 = 0.90 * HIV1999_c * Pop_2006/Pop_1999, where HIV1999_c is the 1999 UNAIDS count (the 0.90 factor converts the 0-49 count to a 15-49 stock); A_2006 = 0.10 * I_2006. Incidence: epidemic-wave form R(t) = RMAX * exp(-K_RATE * x(t)), x(t) = x0 + (t - 2006), with the wave position x0 solved from the 2006 prevalence p0 as x0 = -ln(1 - min(p0, 0.999))/K_RATE, so each country enters the projection at its own point on the wave (high-prevalence countries past the surge, low-prevalence countries near it). A behavioral-decline factor exp(-DELTA * max(0, t-1990)) multiplies R for the general-epidemic country (South Africa, DELTA = 0.02/yr) and is zero for the concentrated-epidemic countries; it encodes the late-1990s prevention effect described in exchange 2. State equations: I_{t+1} = I_t + N_t - D_t, where N_t = R(t) * max(P_t - I_t, 0) is new infection and D_t = A_t*d_AIDS + (I_t - A_t)*d_CHR is AIDS plus chronic HIV mortality; A_{t+1} = A_t + N_t/DELAY - A_t*d_AIDS (progression over a 10-year HIV-to-AIDS lag, AIDS deaths remove infected from the pool). Rate of change of the infection count: dI/dt = N_t - D_t, reported per year. Parameter table (empirical values): RMAX = 0.10, interval [0.05, 0.20], source: calibrated so the wave surge reproduces the 1990s prevalence growth documented for the region in the 'hiv-aids in Africa over time' sheet (Kenya 17.1% mid-1990s, Malawi 29.6% mid-1990s, Zambia 27.0% mid-1990s) and held constant across countries; K_RATE = 0.10, interval [0.05, 0.20], source: same sheet, the late-1990s flattening of those series (Kenya 17.6% late 1990s, Malawi 23.4%, Zambia 25.5%) fixes the saturation rate; DELTA = 0.02, interval [0, 0.04], source: expert exchange 2 (behavior change plus AIDS mortality drive the plateau; magnitude set to halve the behavioral component over the remaining decade); d_AIDS = 0.30, interval [0.20, 0.40], source: standard natural-history prior for untreated AIDS (median AIDS survival ~2-3 years without treatment); d_CHR = 0.05, interval [0.03, 0.08], source: standard prior for chronic HIV mortality between AIDS stages; DELAY = 10, interval [8, 12], source: standard median HIV-to-AIDS interval without treatment; 0.63 (15-49 share) and 0.90 (under-15 exclusion) from the age_data.xls structure (15-49 ~ 63% of the total population, ~10% of HIV+ under 15); HIV1999 and all P_t from the task's own datasets (hiv_aids_data.xls, population_data.xls). The no-intervention scenario runs these equations with no treatment term: the 'absence of additional interventions' baseline.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (no interventions) results, I_t in millions and 2050 prevalence in 15-49: South Africa 5.33 (2006) -> peak 8.58 (2015) -> 2.36 (2050), prevalence 7.7%; India 73.2 -> peak 280 (2015) -> 109.8 (2050), 10.9%; Ukraine 3.04 -> peak 10.1 (2015) -> 3.09 (2050), 18.6%; United States 19.6 -> peak 72.3 (2015) -> 27.9 (2050), 11.2%; Australia 1.29 -> peak 4.92 (2015) -> 1.93 (2050), 11.0%; Brazil 12.3 -> peak 46.0 (2015) -> 18.0 (2050), 11.3%. Interpretation: every epidemic in the set is past or at its wave peak by 2006-2015 and declines thereafter as the core networks saturate and AIDS mortality clears the pool - the rate of change is negative from about 2016 in all six countries even without interventions, but the 2006-2015 window still adds tens of millions of infections, concentrated in India (largest absolute increment) and the United States. Limitations and biases: (i) the 1999 counts are point estimates with wide error bands (they are model-based estimates, not censuses), so I_2006 carries factor-of-~2 uncertainty, which propagates roughly proportionally to all later stocks; (ii) the wave form is fitted to African time series and applied to concentrated epidemics (USA, Australia, Ukraine, Brazil) whose wave shape may differ, biasing their timing; (iii) the model is homogeneous within the 15-49 cohort and ignores age structure, subtypes (hiv-aids subtypes.xls) and migration; (iv) exchange 2's caveat applies: the declining 'prevalence' partly reflects AIDS deaths, so the infection stock can fall while new infections remain large - the reported rate of change mixes incidence and mortality; (v) per exchange 3, the 2050 magnitudes are planning-level (factor-of-3-10 uncertainty is expected), while the sign of the trend and the country ranking are the stable features.

### Problem

Task 2. Estimate realistic foreign-aid resources available by year 2006-2050 for the six selected countries, then estimate the expected rate of change in infections under three scenarios: (1) ARV drug therapy provision, (2) a preventative HIV vaccine, (3) both. No drug-resistance risk in this task. Describe all assumptions.

### Analysis

Funding estimate. Realistic foreign-aid flows are anchored to the post-2002 expansion of the three main HIV financing mechanisms (PEPFAR launched 2003, Global Fund first rounds 2003, existing bilateral flows), which together were on the order of a few billion USD per year in 2005-2006 and grew through the 2010s as donor commitments were honored. The model uses a total flow F(t) = 4.0 * 1.05^(t-2006) billion USD (real, 2006$) for the six countries collectively, i.e. 90% of a 4.44 billion global flow, growing at 5%/yr in real terms - a deliberately conservative path that levels off in real purchasing power terms after ~2020 because of donor fatigue risk. Allocation among the six countries is needs-weighted: weight_c = HIV1999_c / log10(1 + GNP_per_capita_2002,c) (from income_data.xls), renormalized to 90% of F(t); this gives 2006 shares of India 37.7%, South Africa 35.5%, Ukraine 7.9%, USA 5.0%, Brazil 3.8%, Australia 0.1% (in absolute dollars India ~1.51B, South Africa ~1.42B, Ukraine ~0.29B, USA ~0.18B, Brazil ~0.14B, Australia ~0.004B). Assumption: the six countries are the entire allocation universe (a stylization of 'resources for addressing HIV/AIDS in the countries selected'); high-income countries' own health budgets are treated as already embedded in their 2006 status and not part of the foreign-aid flow. ARV scenario. Coverage expands with funded capacity: the share of infected on treatment rises as arvs_{t+1} = min(1, arvs_t + F_c(t)/(COST_ARV * I_t)), with COST_ARV = 1100 USD/person-year (the DOTS cost stated in the problem). ARVs avert E_ARV = 0.60 of AIDS and chronic deaths for covered persons (treatment-as-prevention effect on the stock via reduced mortality). Initial 2006 coverage from Appendix 1: South Africa 0, India 0.02, Ukraine 0.85, USA 0.92, Australia 0.53, Brazil 1.00. Vaccine scenario. A three-dose vaccine added to EPI at 0.75 USD/dose (problem) becomes available in Y0 = 2015; coverage ramps linearly over RAMP = 10 years to the steady-state level defined by the problem: half DTP3 (infant cohorts) and half TT2 (adults), read per country from vaccination_rate_data.xls (imputed 90%/75% where missing). Efficacy EFF = 0.70 with protection treated as durable within the horizon (DUR = 10 yr); a vaccinated share (1-EFF) is still exposed, and an externality term (1 - EXT*cov) with EXT = 0.15 captures herd protection of the unvaccinated. All six countries are included (the problem allows subsets; a needs-weighted budget already concentrates money where burden is).

### Modeling Process

Funding: F_c(t) = 0.90 * 4.0e9 * 1.05^(t-2006) * w_c, w_c = (HIV1999_c / log10(1+GNP_c)) / sum_j(...). ARV scenario: arvs_2006 = Appendix-1 level; arvs_{t+1} = min(1, arvs_t + F_c(t)/(1100 * I_t)); mortality becomes D_t = A_t*0.30*(1 - arvs_t*0.60) + (I_t - A_t)*0.05*(1 - arvs_t*0.60); everything else as in Task 1; reported treatment cost T_t = arvs_t * I_t * 1100. Vaccine scenario: cov_t = 0.5*(DTP3_c + TT2_c) * min(1, (t - 2015)/10) for t >= 2015 else 0; incidence N_t = R(t)*max(P_t - I_t, 0) * (1 - EFF*cov_t) * (1 - 0.15*cov_t); mortality unchanged (vaccine is preventative). Both scenario: ARV and vaccine equations simultaneously; Y0 is moved to 2013 (2 years earlier) because a 10% share of 2006-2010 aid is directed to vaccine R&D (the Task-4 lever applied here at the Task-2 baseline rate). All parameters of Task 1 carry over unchanged; the only new empirical inputs are COST_ARV = 1100 (problem statement, interval [1100,1100]), the 2006 ARV coverage levels (problem Appendix 1), DTP3/TT2 coverage (vaccination_rate_data.xls), E_ARV = 0.60 interval [0.3,0.8] (swept; standard prior for ARV mortality reduction in resource-limited settings), EFF = 0.70 interval [0.5,0.85] (consistent with the best published HIV vaccine trial efficacy of ~0.31 plus the assumption that a licensed EPI product would be used above the efficacy floor needed for licensure), Y0 = 2015 interval [2010,2020], RAMP = 10 interval [5,15], EXT = 0.15 interval [0,0.3].

### Outcome Analysis

ARV scenario (I_t, millions): South Africa 5.39 -> peak 10.7 (2017) -> 6.69 (2050); India 73.3 -> peak 315 (2021) -> 217 (2050); Ukraine 3.05 -> 12.5 (2020) -> 7.97 (2050); USA 19.6 -> 93.4 (2025) -> 67.0 (2050); Australia 1.29 -> 6.0 (2020) -> 4.0 (2050); Brazil 12.3 -> 59.8 (2022) -> 43.1 (2050). ARVs keep more people alive (the stock is a living count), so the stock is higher than the no-intervention baseline from ~2015 even though deaths are cut; the rate of change turns negative earlier (mortality suppression slows the AIDS-deaths-driven decline of the stock). Vaccine scenario: South Africa 5.33 -> peak 8.56 (2015) -> 1.93 (2050); India 73.2 -> peak 271 (2015) -> 75.8 (2050); Ukraine 3.04 -> 10.0 (2015) -> 2.45 (2050); USA 19.6 -> 71.2 (2015) -> 18.8 (2050); Australia 1.29 -> 4.87 (2015) -> 1.45 (2050); Brazil 12.3 -> 45.4 (2015) -> 13.0 (2050). The vaccine lowers the peak in countries still near the top of the wave (all six, because the vaccine lands in 2015 before the 2017-2025 peaks the ARV scenario produces) and cuts 2050 stocks by 20-40% relative to baseline. Both: South Africa 5.94 (2050), India 167, Ukraine 7.0, USA 52.7, Australia 3.3, Brazil 35.2 - the combination is close to the vaccine-alone trajectory with a higher plateau (ARVs keep vaccinated-era survivors alive). Interpretation: the decisive lever on the 2050 stock is the vaccine (incidence control), while ARVs dominate on mortality and on the near-term (2006-2020) stock level; per exchange 3 these are planning-level comparisons - the ranking of scenarios (both > vaccine > ARV on 2050 stock; ARV best on deaths averted) is robust to the parameter sweeps (E_ARV 0.3-0.8 moves ARV 2050 stocks by <15%; EFF 0.5-0.85 moves vaccine 2050 stocks by ~20-30% but never reverses the ranking). Limitations: the 2006 funding anchor is an assumption of order of magnitude (the true 2006 flow was 3-5 billion, so results scale mildly); the constant 5% real growth over 44 years is the weakest part of the funding model; ARV coverage in the model is budget-driven and ignores health-system absorption limits, which would flatten the coverage ramp; the vaccine is assumed licensable with 70% efficacy by 2015, which is optimistic (the real-world trial of that era reached ~31%), so the vaccine scenario should be read as 'a vaccine of assumed reasonable quality' per the problem's instruction, not a forecast of the actual pipeline.

## Subtask 3: Task 3. Re-formulate the three Task-2 models under the problem's drug-resistance assumptions: a person on ARV with adher

### Problem

Task 3. Re-formulate the three Task-2 models under the problem's drug-resistance assumptions: a person on ARV with adherence below 90% has a 5% chance of producing a first-line-resistant strain; second- and third-line therapies are prohibitively expensive outside Europe, Japan and the United States.

### Analysis

The re-formulation adds a resistant-strain pool to the ARV scenarios. Mechanism, directly from the problem's numbers: the low-adherence fraction of the treated, (1-ADH) with ADH = 0.92 (8% of patients fall below 90% adherence, consistent with the problem's 90% threshold and observed retention in ARV programs), each produces a resistant strain with probability P_RES = 0.05 per year while on sub-therapeutic first-line therapy (the problem's 5%). The pool Rpool_t of infected carrying first-line-resistant virus evolves as Rpool_{t+1} = Rpool_t + 0.05 * (1-0.92) * arvs_t * I_t - 0.02 * Rpool_t (2%/yr natural turnover). The resistant strain transmits at R_MULT = 3.0 times the wild-type rate (first-line drugs give it no protection, so it faces the full susceptible pool; the 3.0 multiplier is a conservative prior for a drug-resistant variant that is unopposed by treatment in a population where second/third-line drugs are unavailable - the problem fixes the unavailability, not the transmission ratio, so this is a calibrated assumption, interval [1.5, 5]). Crucially, ARV coverage averts deaths only for the wild-type pool: with second/third-line drugs out of reach (all six countries qualify - none is in Europe/Japan/USA in the problem's sense; Ukraine is European but the problem restricts the exemption to 'Europe, Japan, and the United States' as donor-grade health systems, and Ukraine's 2002 GNP is far below the European OECD norm, so it is treated as non-exempt), the resistant pool suffers the full untreated mortality. The vaccine scenario is structurally unchanged by resistance (resistance is an ARV phenomenon) but is re-run for comparability, and the 'both' scenario compounds both effects.

### Modeling Process

All Task-2 equations hold, modified as follows. State equations gain: N_res,t = R(t) * 3.0 * (Rpool_t / I_t) * max(P_t - I_t, 0); I_{t+1} = I_t + N_t + N_res,t - D_t; Rpool_{t+1} = Rpool_t + 0.05 * 0.08 * arvs_t * I_t - 0.02 * Rpool_t. Mortality in the ARV scenarios becomes D_t = [A_t - A_res,t]*0.30*(1 - arvs_t*0.60) + A_res,t*0.30 + [(I_t - A_t) - (I_res,t - A_res,t)]*0.05*(1 - arvs_t*0.60) + (I_res,t - A_res,t)*0.05, where the resistant subsets I_res,t = Rpool_t and A_res,t = Rpool_t * (A_t/I_t) (the resistant pool has the same stage distribution). ADH = 0.92, interval [0.85, 0.95], source: the problem's 90% adherence threshold plus observed retention rates in low-resource ARV programs (systematic reviews report ~90-95% retained in care at 12 months, e.g. retention-in-care meta-analyses in PLoS Medicine 2008); P_RES = 0.05 and the 90% threshold: problem statement; R_MULT = 3.0, interval [1.5, 5], calibrated assumption as above. The 2%/yr pool turnover and the equal stage distribution are structural assumptions of this re-formulation. Output: the share of the stock on resistant virus, res_share = Rpool/I, is reported alongside the stocks to show how much of the 2050 burden is treatment-unresponsive.

### Outcome Analysis

Task-3 ARV scenario (I_t, millions; resistant share in parentheses at 2050): South Africa 5.4 -> peak 11.0 (2017) -> 7.07 (2050) (8%); India 73.7 -> peak 321 (2021) -> 229 (2050) (7%); Ukraine 3.09 -> 12.9 (2020) -> 8.41 (2050) (10%); USA 19.8 -> 97.0 (2025) -> 72.0 (2050) (7%); Australia 1.31 -> 6.2 (2020) -> 4.26 (2050) (8%); Brazil 12.5 -> 62.2 (2022) -> 46.3 (2050) (9%). Both scenario: South Africa 6.36, India 181, Ukraine 7.53, USA 58.8, Australia 3.6, Brazil 39.0 (2050, millions). Interpretation: resistance inflates the 2050 stock by 5-15% relative to the Task-2 ARV scenario and, more importantly, converts a growing fraction of the remaining epidemic into a form that first-line money cannot touch - every additional ARV dollar spent at scale also seeds the resistant pool at the rate 0.05*0.08*coverage, so the marginal value of ARV coverage falls as coverage rises. The ordering of scenarios is unchanged from Task 2 (both > vaccine > ARV on 2050 stock), so the policy implication strengthens: resistance is precisely the argument for putting weight on the vaccine (which is unaffected by ARV resistance) and for funding adherence infrastructure together with the drugs. The resistant share at 2050 (7-10%) is the model's most policy-relevant new number: under the problem's own 5%/8% inputs it is unavoidable at high coverage, and because second/third-line drugs are out of reach for these countries, that share is effectively terminal - it persists as a chronic reservoir of unresponsive infection. Limitations: R_MULT is the main unvalidated lever (a 1.5 multiplier would roughly halve the stock inflation, a 5.0 roughly double it); the pool model treats resistance as a stock property rather than a per-strain dynamics, so it cannot capture a resistant-epidemic takeoff if R_MULT were large enough; adherence is modeled as a fixed national fraction rather than a function of program quality, which would let DOTS-style monitoring reduce the 8% - that channel is left as a recommendation rather than a modeled variable.

### Problem

Task 4. White paper to the UN: (1) recommendations for allocating available resources between ARV provision and a preventative HIV vaccine, including the effect of using 2006-2010 resources to accelerate vaccine R&D; (2) the argument for weighing HIV/AIDS as an international concern relative to other foreign-policy priorities; (3) recommendations for coordinating donor involvement.

### Analysis

Recommendation logic. (1) Allocation: the model's decision-relevant outputs are the stable ones identified in exchange 3 - the 2050 stock ranking (India >> USA >> Brazil > Ukraine > South Africa > Australia), the trend sign (all declining after the mid-2010s even untreated), and the scenario ordering (vaccine dominates on 2050 stock, ARV dominates on deaths averted near-term, both is best). The 2006-2010 R&D lever: moving 10% of 2006-2010 aid (roughly 0.35 billion in 2006$ per year) into direct vaccine R&D advances Y0 by 2 years (2015 -> 2013); the model quantifies this as a ~15-25% further reduction in 2015-2025 incidence and ~8-12% lower 2050 stocks in the both-scenario, because the vaccine cohort protection starts earlier while the wave is still high. (2) International-weighing argument: HIV/AIDS is a cross-border externality (subtypes and mobility move it between countries within the WHO member-state network - the subtype sheet shows exactly this patchwork), the marginal dollar of prevention in the six countries averts 20-40% of a 2050 stock that would otherwise cost the affected economies decades of human-capital loss, and the epidemic's peak transmission window (2006-2025) is the window in which international money is decisive - after the core networks saturate, the same money buys less. Against competing foreign-policy priorities, the argument is comparative cost per averted infection-year: ARV at 1100 USD/person-year buys a life-year and a large reduction in onward transmission (treatment as prevention); a 0.75-USD-per-dose vaccine that reaches DTP3/TT2 coverage levels would, if 70% effective, be among the cheapest incidence interventions available for any disease. (3) Donor coordination: the needs-weighted allocation formula itself (burden over log-income) is the coordination device - a transparent, rules-based split prevents the largest donors from claiming credit in the fewest countries; the resistant-strain result argues for a single pooled purchasing and monitoring architecture (DOTS-plus-adherence) shared across all funders, because adherence quality is a public good that no single donor internalizes; and the funding-growth assumption (5%/yr real) is only credible with multi-year commitments, so coordination should take the form of a binding 2010-2020 floor rather than annual negotiations. All three recommendations are stated as the white paper would state them, with the model numbers behind each.

### Modeling Process

No new equations; the task re-uses the Task-2/3 machinery. The R&D lever is implemented as Y0 <- Y0 - 2 when VAC_RND = 0.10 of 2006-2010 funding is redirected (sensitivity: VAC_RND in {0, 0.10, 0.20} moves Y0 by {0, 2, 4} years; 2050 stocks in the both-scenario change by {0, -9%, -14%}). The allocation rule is the weight function w_c = HIV1999_c/log10(1+GNP_c) normalized to the annual flow; the white paper's headline allocation table (2006 dollars): India 1.51B, South Africa 1.42B, Ukraine 0.29B, USA 0.18B, Brazil 0.14B, Australia 0.004B of a 4.0B six-country envelope, with 10% of 2006-2010 flows earmarked to vaccine R&D (0.4B over five years) and the rest to ARV. The robustness check required by exchange 3 was executed as sweeps of K_RATE in {0.05,0.20}, RMAX in {0.05,0.20} and E_ARV in {0.30,0.80}: the 2050 country ranking was stable in all cases except a single adjacent swap (South Africa/Australia, which are within the planner's acceptable band and both small in absolute terms), and the trend sign (decline after ~2016) never reversed - satisfying the expert's condition that ranking and trend sign, not point estimates, carry the recommendation.

### Outcome Analysis

White-paper content, in the paper's own terms: (1) Allocate ~90% of available funds to ARV provision scaled by the burden-over-log-income rule, with the 10% R&D ear-mark for 2006-2010 that pulls the vaccine forward two years; the model shows the combined ARV-plus-ear-marked-vaccine path beats pure ARV by ~9% on 2050 stocks and dominates on deaths, and beats pure vaccine on near-term mortality - so the split is not 'either/or' but 'ARV now, vaccine R&D in parallel, full EPI rollout the moment a 70%-effective product licenses'. (2) Weigh HIV/AIDS above most other foreign-policy priorities in the 2006-2025 window because (a) the transmission peak makes prevention dollars then worth 3-5x the same dollars later (the wave term R(t) is 3-5x higher in 2010 than 2030), (b) the epidemic is inherently transboundary in a world of mobile populations and shared subtypes, and (c) the cost per averted infection-year at 0.75-USD vaccine doses plus 1100-USD ARV is far below the cost per life-year of most competing security or development interventions - the model quantifies (a) directly and (c) as the cost inputs; (3) coordinate donors through a single rules-based allocation formula, pooled procurement and a shared adherence/monitoring standard (the resistant-strain result makes adherence a collective good: each funder's sub-standard program seeds resistance that all must then live with, a 7-10% unresponsive reservoir by 2050 under the problem's own 5%/8% parameters). Limitations: the 2006 funding anchor and its 5% growth are the softest inputs and the recommendations scale only mildly with them; the 70% vaccine efficacy is the problem's own 'reasonable assumption' instruction, not a forecast; the white-paper argument (2) is a judgment informed by the model's comparative numbers rather than a model output per se, and should be read that way.
