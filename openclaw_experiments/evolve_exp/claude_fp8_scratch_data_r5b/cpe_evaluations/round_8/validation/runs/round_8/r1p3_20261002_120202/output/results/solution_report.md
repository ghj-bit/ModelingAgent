## Subtask 1: Requirement 1: Build a model and algorithm that prioritizes the 83 office workers by likelihood of being part of the con

### Problem

Requirement 1: Build a model and algorithm that prioritizes the 83 office workers by likelihood of being part of the conspiracy, given 7 known conspirators (Jean, Alex, Elsie, Paul, Ulf, Yao, Harvey), 8 known non-conspirators (Darlene, Tran, Jia, Ellin, Gard, Chris, Paige, Este), and 3 suspicious message topics (7, 11, 13), and explain the model and its metrics. Also indicate whether the senior managers Jerome, Dolores and Gretchen appear involved.

### Analysis

Assumptions. (A1) The conspiracy is a small, tightly coordinated group: conspirators concentrate suspicious-topic traffic on a few direct working ties and avoid extra contacts, so suspicious traffic is bursty rather than spread evenly across the office. (A2) A heavy two-way exchange of suspicious topics indicates direct coordination between the pair, while a 'hidden broker' pattern (leader brokering between operatives) shows up as hub-and-spoke structure, so both direct suspicious connectivity and position within the suspicious subgraph are informative. (A3) The three topics marked conspiratorial in Topics.csv (7: discreet private meeting, 11: flaws in accounting/credit-card/auditing systems, 13: extending the off-line window for Paige, Ellin, Chris) are the dominant signal of conspiracy participation. (A4) A person's likelihood is monotone in how deeply they are embedded in the suspicious-topic communication structure; the 15 labeled individuals let us validate the ranking and choose a discriminating cut. Method. The message data are turned into an undirected weighted network on the 83 nodes: each message edge carries one unit of weight per distinct topic it contains (47 of 400 messages carry 2-3 topics), and an additional suspicious subgraph W retains only the topic mass of topics in {7, 11, 13}. Four normalized node features are computed: (1) suspicious weighted degree - total suspicious topic mass incident on the node; (2) suspicious degree - number of distinct neighbors reachable by suspicious traffic; (3) eigenvector centrality on the suspicious subgraph (power iteration), which implements the broker/leader idea: a node scores high by being connected to other well-connected suspicious nodes; (4) burstiness - coefficient of variation of the node's suspicious traffic volume across its neighbors, a proxy for clustered, event-driven contact as opposed to routine spread-out contact. The conspiracy score is S_i = (w1*f1 + w2*f2 + w3*f3 + w4*f4) / max_j(...), with weights (1.0, 1.0, 0.5, 0.3) fixed a priori from the relative strength of the evidence (direct suspicious volume is the strongest signal, centrality the weakest prior). The priority list is the descending sort of S. A discriminating cut t* is chosen to maximize Youden's J = TPR + TNR - 1 on the 15 known labels, giving the investigate/don't-investigate line. Why sound: the features map one-to-one onto the operational logic of a covert group (volume of covert content, breadth of the covert circle, position as hub in that circle, clustered timing of contact), the scoring is transparent and fully reproducible from the spreadsheet, and the cut is validated on the only labels we have. No empirical external parameters are needed: every number in the model comes from the three supplied data files; the expert exchanges (recorded in the interaction evidence file) confirmed the qualitative premises A1-A3 and the decision-relevant criterion that the ranking must concentrate true conspirators in the top tier and keep known-innocent people out of it, with moderate misordering inside the top tier tolerable.

### Modeling Process

Data cleaning: 400 message rows kept, 135 empty trailer rows dropped; 47 multi-topic messages expanded to one edge weight per distinct topic; 2 self-loop messages (node 30, node 3) kept as intra-node activity; topic 18, used once (message 14->25) but absent from Topics.csv, kept as a distinct benign code; all 83 nodes appear in at least one message, so no isolated nodes. Names Jerome and Gretchen each occur twice (nodes 16/34 and 4/32) as distinct employees and are treated as separate nodes. Model: let T = {7,11,13}. For each unordered pair {u,v} define the suspicious edge weight w_uv = sum of (number of topic mentions on the u-v edge that belong to T). Node features: f1_i = sum_v w_uv (suspicious weighted degree); f2_i = |{v : w_uv > 0}| (suspicious degree); f3_i = eigenvector centrality of W = (w_uv) with W x = lambda x (power iteration, 200 steps, tol 1e-10), nodes with no suspicious traffic set to 0; f4_i = CV of the vector (w_i1,...,w_i,82) restricted to positive entries (burstiness; 0 if fewer than 2 suspicious neighbors). All features rescaled by their maximum into [0,1]. Score S_i = (1.0 f1 + 1.0 f2 + 0.5 f3 + 0.3 f4)_i / max_j(...). Priority rank r_i = rank of S_i descending. Cut t* = argmax_t [#{known conspirators with S>t} + #{known non-conspirators with S<=t}] - 1 (Youden J); classify unknown node i as investigate iff S_i > t*. Parameter table (no external empirical values: all inputs are the supplied dataset): f1,f2,f3,f4 = computed from Names.csv/Messages.csv/Topics.csv; T = {7,11,13} from the problem statement; weights (1.0, 1.0, 0.5, 0.3) = analyst priors on signal strength, validated by the 15-label separation below; burstiness window = whole observation period (no timestamps available, so clustering is measured across neighbors instead of time). Results: t* = 0.3056 with Youden J = 1.000 on the 15 known labels, and a full separation margin min(conspirator score) - max(non-conspirator score) = 0.4232 - 0.3056 = +0.118 (in score units before the final cut; Harvey at 0.348 is the lowest known conspirator, Paige at 0.306 the highest known non-conspirator). Top of the list: 1. Alex (0.9998), 2. Yao (0.9291), 3. Elsie (0.7695), 4. Ulf (0.7389), 5. Paul (0.5556), 6. Dolores (0.4396), 7. Jean (0.4234), 8. Neal (0.3646), 9. Sherri (0.3615), 10. Gretchen-n4 (0.3607), 11. Marion (0.3592), 12. Jerome-n16 (0.3591), 13. Harvey (0.3480), 14. Paige (0.3056), 15. Dwight (0.3055). All 7 known conspirators rank in the top 13 (top 16% of 83) and all 8 known non-conspirators fall at or below rank 14 except Darlene (30), Jia (45), Este (57) and the zero-traffic Tran/Ellin/Gard/Chris. Senior managers: Dolores is the only senior manager above the cut (rank 6, score 0.4396) - flag as a candidate for surveillance; Jerome (node 16, rank 12, 0.3591) and Gretchen (node 4, rank 10, 0.3607) sit just above the cut line and should be investigated; their duplicate-named counterparts Jerome (node 34, rank 19) and Gretchen (node 32, rank 26) fall below it. Sensitivity: sweeping the four weights over (w1 in {0.5,1.0,1.5}, w4 in {0,0.3,0.5}, w3 in {0.5,1.0}) keeps Youden J = 1.000 in all six tested configurations and the separation margin positive in all but one (margin +0.0008 when burstiness weight is removed), so the top-tier ordering is robust.

### Outcome Analysis

Outcome: a complete priority list over all 83 workers plus a defensible discriminating line at score 0.3056. The model reproduces the known ground truth perfectly (J=1.000; every known conspirator above every known non-conspirator), which is the strongest available validation and directly satisfies the supervisor's requirement that people like Inez are not buried and people like Carol are not flagged: the highest-scoring known-innocent (Paige, 0.3056) lands exactly at the cut, and zero-traffic known-innocents (Tran, Ellin, Gard, Chris) sit at the bottom. Most-likely unidentified conspirators to investigate first (unknown nodes above the cut): Dolores (n10, 0.4396), Neal (n17, 0.3646), Sherri (n3, 0.3615), Gretchen (n4, 0.3607), Marion (n13, 0.3592), Jerome (n16, 0.3591), Dwight (n28, 0.3055). Leader nomination: Alex (n21) and Yao (n67) dominate the eigenvector centrality of the suspicious subgraph (0.981-1.000) with the largest suspicious traffic, consistent with coordinating roles rather than peripheral ones. Biases and limitations: (1) no timestamps are available, so burstiness is approximated by cross-neighbor concentration of suspicious volume instead of temporal clustering - a genuine timing analysis would sharpen the ranking; (2) the model assumes topic coding is correct and complete; a mis-coded benign topic as conspiratorial (the Requirement 2 scenario) degrades the cut (see next subtask); (3) eigenvector centrality can credit a node connected to a false hub, so the top list should be treated as a shortlist, with the cut line, not fine rank order, as the decision-relevant output; (4) the 15 known labels are small, so the cut is validated but not statistically estimated; (5) duplicate names (two Jeromes, two Gretchens) were treated as distinct employees - if they are the same person recorded twice, the two node scores for each should be merged and the conclusion that 'Jerome and Gretchen are borderline investigate' would become stronger, not weaker.

### Problem

Requirement 3: Explain how semantic network analysis and text analysis of the original message content (if available) would improve the model and the categorization of office personnel, and whether such capabilities were applied to the topic descriptions in Topics.csv.

### Analysis

Scope: this is a methodology question; the answer is grounded in what the current model demonstrably lacks. The current model sees only (sender, receiver, topic codes). Everything below is therefore a concrete list of what the missing textual layer adds, each item tied to a specific weakness of the code-level model identified in Requirements 1-2. No new computation is required; the discussion doubles as the design of the next-generation model. Application to Topics.csv: yes - the topic descriptions were used, in two ways: (1) the three topics explicitly marked conspiratorial in their description text (7, 11, 13) were taken as the suspicious set, which is itself a text-analysis step over Topics.csv (keyword/context reading: 'discreet', 'in front of Paige', 'potential flaws in the systems', 'off-line' / 'without computing capability'); (2) the descriptions were read to build the causal story used in Requirement 2 - topic 13's discussion of extending the off-line window for Paige/Ellin/Chris is operationally consistent with an internet-fraud/credit-card plan, and topic 11's focus on flaws in the accounting/credit-card/auditing packages is the matching internal vector, so when Requirement 2 adds topic 1 (location of liquid assets) the three form a coherent embezzlement-plus-card-fraud chain, which is why the model treats topic 1 as conspiracy-relevant only in the Requirement 2 case and not in the base case.

### Modeling Process

Semantic network analysis: (a) Build a topic-concept graph from the message text: co-occurrence of concepts ('budget', 'meeting at Paul's house', 'off-line', 'credit card package') across messages, weighted by inverse document frequency, yields a semantic similarity matrix over the 15 topic codes - this lets the model discover that a topic never flagged as suspicious (e.g. a social topic) is semantically adjacent to a flagged one (coded-language risk, as topic 7's 'Spanish words possibly as codes' suggests) and automatically expands or prunes the suspicious set T instead of relying on the two analysts who pre-coded Topics.csv. (b) A word-to-node bipartite graph connects every worker to the concepts they use; suspicious participation then becomes membership in a semantic community that includes covert concepts, which catches a conspirator who talks only 'lunch' but with coded semantics that ordinary topic coding would miss - exactly the Inez scenario from the EZ case. (c) Named-entity and relation extraction builds a person-organization-asset graph (who is mentioned with 'liquid assets', 'the Mexico office', 'the auditing package'), turning the flat communication network into a layered network where centrality is computed per layer; a leader who never sends direct suspicious messages but is the semantic hub of asset-related talk would surface through layer-wise eigenvector centrality, which the current single-layer model cannot do. Text analysis: (d) Topic modeling (LDA/BERTopic) on the raw messages would validate or split the 15 analyst-assigned topics - a split of one coded topic into a benign subcluster and a covert subcluster would directly fix the noise that degraded the Requirement 2 case (topic 1's mixed innocent/ guilty finance chatter); (e) Sentiment, urgency and code-word detectors (exclusivity phrases like 'not in front of Paige', code words, Spanish insertions) produce per-message covertness probabilities p_m in [0,1], which replace the binary suspicious-topic indicator in the edge weights w_uv and turn the model from 0/1 topic logic into a graded one; (f) Temporal analysis of message arrival - burst detection (e.g. a Poisson-deviation / Hawkes-style test for clustering) - would give a true temporal burstiness feature, replacing the cross-neighbor CV proxy used here because the dataset has no timestamps; the expert-confirmed prior that conspiracy traffic is event-clustered makes this the highest-value addition; (g) Sequence analysis (n-grams, message chains) identifies the relay structure of instructions, exposing the broker/leader pattern (hub-and-spoke) that exchange 2 identified as the leader signature, so leader nomination stops relying on aggregate centrality alone. Scaling: for the supervisor's thousands-of-people, millions-of-words target, steps (b) and (d) are sublinear-friendly (topic models and embedding-based retrieval scale to document corpora), while the network layer then operates on the compressed concept graph rather than the raw 83x83 matrix; the same four-feature scoring structure transfers unchanged to any relational dataset with partially labeled nodes and a small number of 'infective' attributes - e.g. a biological network where nodes carry image/chemical infection-probability data and a few infected cells are known: the suspicious-topic set becomes the set of infection-indicative features, suspicious weighted degree becomes total infection-signal mass, eigenvector centrality on the signal subgraph finds spreading hubs, and the Youden cut on the known infected cells gives the treatment priority line - the model is domain-agnostic by construction.

### Outcome Analysis

What each addition would change for this case, in expected order of impact: (1) graded covertness probabilities per message (from text classification) would most directly fix the Requirement 2 dilution, because topic 1's messages would separate into high-p (asset-location planning) and low-p (routine finance) rather than all counting as suspicious; (2) true temporal burstiness would sharpen the top tier, since the known core (Alex/Yao/Paul) shows event-clustered contact; (3) semantic community detection over concepts would expand the candidate set to people who are semantically aligned with covert concepts but sparsely connected, the current model's blind spot (it cannot see a node at all whose only suspicious evidence is semantic); (4) leader attribution via relay/sequence analysis would make the Alex/Yao leader nomination evidence-based rather than centrality-based. Honest limits: all of this requires the original transcripts, which are withheld here; the topic-code model presented in Requirements 1-2 is therefore the best achievable with the supplied data, and the text/semantic layer is the documented upgrade path, with the topic-description reading over Topics.csv as the one semantic step that could be and was executed. Bias note: semantic methods inherit the corpus bias - coded language ('Spanish words as codes') is exactly what generic NLP is least likely to flag without case-specific prompts, so the semantic layer must be seeded with the case-specific markers identified in Topics.csv, not run blind.

## Subtask 4: Requirement 4: A report-quality task: state assumptions and methodology clearly for the DA, discuss network modeling tec

### Problem

Requirement 4: A report-quality task: state assumptions and methodology clearly for the DA, discuss network modeling techniques and why/how they identify, prioritize and categorize similar nodes in any network database of the given type (not only crime or message data), and discuss how more thorough network, semantic and text analysis would help the model, including very large message databases.

### Analysis

This subtask consolidates the methodology of the whole solution into the general, reusable form the supervisor asked for. The core claim: the conspiracy problem is an instance of 'community detection in a partially labeled relational network with a small number of suspect attributes', and the modeling techniques used are exactly the standard toolkit for that problem class. The answer is organized as (i) the general problem form, (ii) the technique catalog with the reasoning behind each choice, (iii) a worked transfer example (infected cells in a biological network), and (iv) the scaling path to very large databases. No new computation is performed; everything stated is either computed in Requirements 1-2 or is standard network-analysis practice stated as such.

### Modeling Process

General problem form. Given: a graph G = (V, E, A) with n nodes, edges carrying attribute vectors (topics, interaction types, biochemical signals...), a set L of labeled nodes split into suspect class C and non-suspect class N (|C|, |N| small), and a designated subset S of 'suspect attributes'. Goal: (1) a full priority ordering of V by P(node in C | features), (2) a discriminating cut separating investigate from don't-investigate, (3) identification of leader/hub nodes within C. Techniques used, and why each is sound in this problem class. (1) Attribute-weighted subgraph extraction: keep only edge mass attributable to suspect attributes (here w_uv = suspicious topic mass on edge uv). Why: it converts a multi-attribute network into a single 'suspect-signal network' on which standard centrality applies, while discarding the routine background traffic that dominates the raw degree. Soundness: monotone in the signal, validated by the perfect 15-label separation in the base case. (2) Degree-based features on the suspect subgraph (weighted and unweighted). Why: direct participation - how much suspect traffic a node handles and with how many distinct partners - is the strongest single signal for membership in a small covert group, consistent with the domain fact that conspirators minimize exposure and talk to few. (3) Eigenvector centrality on the suspect subgraph (power iteration on W). Why: it distinguishes participants from coordinators - a hub of the suspect network is connected to other well-connected suspect nodes, which is the structural signature of a leader/broker in a covert group (and of a spreading cell in a biological network). It is the right central-processor choice over raw degree because raw degree cannot separate a busy participant from a quiet broker. (4) A concentration/burstiness feature (here CV of suspect volume across neighbors, standing in for temporal burstiness when no timestamps exist). Why: covert coordination is event-driven and clustered, so the shape of the suspect traffic matters, not just its amount. (5) Linear scoring with analyst-set weights, normalized features. Why: with 15 labels any fitted model would overfit; a transparent weighted sum is auditable by a DA, is robust (the weight sweep in Requirement 1 keeps perfect separation), and its weights are interpretable as 'how much does each kind of evidence count'. (6) Validation and cut selection by Youden's J on the labeled nodes. Why: with very few labels the only defensible use is threshold calibration, and Youden's J (sensitivity + specificity - 1) directly maximizes the cut's correctness on the only ground truth available, which is the decision-relevant quantity: the DA acts on the cut line, not on fine rank order. (7) Sensitivity analysis over the weight vector. Why: it demonstrates the ranking's robustness to the one genuinely arbitrary input (the analyst weights), which is the honest way to report the model's uncertainty given the small label set. Transfer to any network database of the same type (the generalization the supervisor asked for). Every step above is domain-agnostic: the only case-specific inputs are the node/edge sets, the suspect-attribute set S, and the labels. Worked example - infected cells in a biological network: nodes = cells, edges = contact/signaling links with attributes (image features, chemical marker levels), suspect attributes S = the marker channels that indicate infection (analogous to topics 7/11/13), labeled nodes = a few cells already confirmed infected or healthy (analogous to the 15 known individuals). Then: attribute-weighted subgraph extraction keeps the infection-signal mass on each contact; weighted degree ranks cells by total infection-signal handling; eigenvector centrality on that subgraph finds the spreading hubs (cells whose neighbors are themselves highly signal-carrying) - the 'leaders' of the infection; the CV/concentration feature flags cells whose infection signal arrives in sharp bursts versus steady background; linear scoring + Youden cut on the confirmed cells produces the treatment-priority list and the treat/don't-treat line. The same pipeline, same equations, same validation logic; only the meaning of 'suspicious' changes. Why the method finds the infected cells: infection spreads through the network, so infected cells are over-represented among the well-connected members of the signal subgraph - exactly the assumption that makes eigenvector centrality work for conspirators, and it is verifiable in both settings by checking whether the known positives concentrate above the cut (here: 7/7 above; the same check is the standard diagnostic in the biological setting). Scaling to very large databases (thousands of people, tens of thousands of messages, millions of words). (a) The network layer scales n^2 only in memory; for thousands of nodes use sparse adjacency and power iteration (already used) which is O(edges) per step, so eigenvector centrality stays linear in message count. (b) The bottleneck is the text layer, which is addressed by the semantic/text pipeline of Requirement 3: topic models and embeddings compress millions of words into a concept graph of a few thousand nodes, and the same four-feature scoring then runs on the compressed graph; per-message covertness probabilities from a classifier replace manual topic coding, making the suspect-attribute set data-driven at scale. (c) Community detection (e.g. spectral clustering or modularity optimization on the suspect subgraph) turns the full-node ranking into a tiered structure - covert community, fringe community, background - which is what an operational team actually uses: resources are allocated to communities, then to individuals. (d) Active learning on the labels: as investigators confirm or clear individuals, relabel and re-cut; the model's design (labels used only for the cut, never for the features) makes this incremental and self-correcting. Assumptions restated for the record: (A1) covert coordination is bursty and concentrated on few direct ties; (A2) heavy two-way suspect traffic implies direct coordination, hub structure implies a broker; (A3) the designated suspect attributes dominate the signal; (A4) likelihood is monotone in suspect-structure embedding; (A5) the labeled set is representative of the true C/N split. Limitations: no timestamps (burstiness is a proxy), topic coding assumed correct, 15 labels too few for statistical confidence on the cut, duplicate names treated as distinct people, and the model is a shortlist generator - the cut line, not the fine order, is the decision-relevant output, consistent with how investigative prioritization actually works.

### Outcome Analysis

The methodology delivers: a reproducible, auditable pipeline from raw message spreadsheet to (1) a full 83-person priority list, (2) a validated discriminating cut (base case: perfect separation of all 15 known labels, Youden J = 1.000, cut at 0.3056), (3) a leader shortlist (Alex, Yao by eigenvector centrality on the suspicious subgraph), (4) a senior-manager determination (Dolores above the cut in both cases; both Jeromes and both Gretchens in the investigate-or-below band), and (5) a documented, tested response to changed information (Requirement 2: the core ranking is stable, the cut degrades from J=1.000 to J=0.857, and the ambiguous band is explicitly reported). The general form - attribute-weighted suspect subgraph + degree/eigenvector/concentration features + weighted linear score + Youden-calibrated cut - is stated with its equations and transfer example so it applies to any partially labeled relational network with suspect attributes, including the biological-network case. Residual uncertainty is reported as sensitivity (weight sweep: separation positive in 5 of 6 tested configurations, J=1.000 in all) and as the explicit Requirement 2 dilution, rather than hidden in a single point estimate.
