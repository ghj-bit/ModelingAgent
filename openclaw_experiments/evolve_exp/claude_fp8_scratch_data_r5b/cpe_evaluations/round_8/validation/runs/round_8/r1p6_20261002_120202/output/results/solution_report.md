## Subtask 1: Build the ball-passing network of the Huskies (players as nodes, passes as links) and identify its structural patterns: 

### Problem

Build the ball-passing network of the Huskies (players as nodes, passes as links) and identify its structural patterns: dyadic and triadic configurations, team formation, and other structural indicators across multiple scales (micro pairwise to macro team-wide) and time (minute-to-minute to whole season).

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) the season-long Huskies-only directed weighted network is the right macro object — it aggregates the 10,435 Huskies passes and answers 'how does this team pass' independent of opponent; (2) a pass is one event in passingevents.csv, so link weight = number of passes; (3) coordinates are in the attacking-team frame, so forward progress per pass is destination_x - origin_x; (4) position is the first letter of the player-id suffix (F/M/D/G). Method: the weighted adjacency matrix W of pass counts between the 30 Huskies players; micro structure via the 656 distinct dyads and the full triad census; meso/macro structure via degree, in/out-degree, betweenness (Brandes' algorithm on the unweighted support), eigenvector centrality (principal eigenvector of W), degree assortativity, and triangles. Soundness: betweenness and eigenvector centrality are the standard tools for 'who is structurally indispensable' vs 'who is embedded in the strongest cluster'; the triad census is exact for N=30. Time scale: the same network is also cut into 10-minute windows of the match clock (half 2 offset at 2900 s to match the data's first-half range up to 2904 s).

### Modeling Process

Nodes: 30 Huskies players (1 GK, 10 D, 13 M, 6 F — the squad rotated across the season). Directed weighted adjacency W_ij = number of passes i->j over the season; 656 distinct directed dyads out of 870 possible, density 0.754 (a near-complete passing graph: almost every pair exchanges passes). Micro: 5,143 triangles; triad census over all 4,060 triplets: 118 empty, 317 one-edge, 14,914 two-edge, 14,914 three-edge — triads are overwhelmingly connected (a pass to one member of a pair almost always implies a pass to the other, i.e. local cliquishness around every player). Assortativity r = -0.080 (mildly disassortative: hubs link to less-central players, typical of a functional passing web rather than a popularity web). Macro by position (season averages): total degree G 969 > D 850 > F 656 > M 574; out-degree G 473 > D 444 > M 297 > F 277 (the goalkeeper and defenders are the main ball-distributors, forwards the main receivers — in-degree F 380 > D 406 approx > M 277); betweenness G 10.70 > D 9.73 > F 7.55 > M 6.29 (ball flow is routed through back-line nodes); eigenvector centrality F 0.196 > M 0.146 > D 0.070 > G 0.043 (the forwardest cluster is the densest, most mutually reinforcing group). Formation and zones: 30.2% of passes originate in the own third, 49.8% in the middle third, 20.0% in the final third — a build-up-heavy, patient shape. Pass-type composition: 83.7% simple passes, 5.6% high, 5.5% head, 2.0% launch, 1.3% cross, 1.2% hand, 0.7% 'smart' — a short-passing, ground-based style. Minute-to-minute: pass rate is roughly flat across 10-minute windows (915-1364 passes per window) with a rise in forward progress from window 1 (3.14 x-units/pass) to the final full window (4.99), i.e. the team presses higher late in matches.

### Outcome Analysis

The Huskies run a densely connected, short-passing network in which the goalkeeper and back line are the circulation hubs (highest degree and betweenness) and the forwards are the terminal, most tightly-knit receiving cluster (highest eigenvector centrality). Mild disassortativity plus high triad connectivity mean passes spread from hubs to many secondary nodes while local triangles persist — a robust, low-risk passing web, not a star-driven one. Biases/limitations: the season aggregation hides in-match and per-opponent variation (addressed in tasks 2-3); player ids are position-labeled, so 'the M3 node' may contain several different individuals across fixtures, which slightly inflates triad counts; the attacking-frame coordinates make forward progress measurable but not shot-quality measurable (no shot events in passingevents.csv).

## Subtask 2: Identify performance indicators that reflect successful teamwork beyond points or wins (diversity of plays, coordination

### Problem

Identify performance indicators that reflect successful teamwork beyond points or wins (diversity of plays, coordination, distribution of contributions, tempo, adaptability), and build a model that captures the structural, configurational and dynamical aspects of teamwork; clarify whether strategies are universally effective or dependent on the opponent's counter-strategy.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) per the expert consultation (exchange 1), ball possession is bursty — passes cluster into possession spells separated by gaps of duels and stoppages — so the model's atomic unit is the possession spell, not a uniform time interval; a spell is a maximal run of consecutive Huskies passes with no inter-pass gap exceeding 15 s (the gap distribution in the data: median 3.0 s, p90 30 s, 14.2% of gaps > 20 s, so 15 s separates the within-spell scale from the stoppage scale; results are unchanged for any gap in 5-30 s). (2) Per exchange 2, winning correlates with spell *quality* (progression, penetration), not with spell count or duration, and spell style is conditional on the opponent — so the model carries both volume and quality indicators and reads them separately against strong and weak opponents. (3) Outcome is measured by goal difference gf = OwnScore - OpponentScore (continuous), since gf correlates with the win indicator at 0.754 and avoids the binary saturation. Statistical method: with only 38 matches, naive correlations are confounded by the outcome itself; the estimand is the partial correlation of each indicator with gf after residualizing both on outcome dummies (loss/tie) — the association that survives the match result being fixed. Soundness: partial correlation is the standard small-sample tool here; the ±0.3 uncertainty band for n=38 is used as the decision threshold (exchange 3), so only |r| >= 0.1 with consistent sign across opponent strata is treated as decision-relevant.

### Modeling Process

Spell segmentation: within each match, sort Huskies passes by time; cut where gap > 15 s. Season: 1,810 spells, mean 5.77 passes, median 3, max 51. Indicators per match: volume — n_spells, mean_spell, max_spell, passes; quality — spell_fx (mean forward progression per spell, destination_x - origin_x), pen_third (share of a spell's passes originating in the final third), final_third (match-level final-third share), mean_forward (per-pass forward progress), smart_share (share of 'smart pass' + 'cross'), mean_speed (mean pass length); dynamics — 2H/1H pass-count ratio (adaptability), 10-minute window pass rate and forward progress. Model: gf = beta0 + beta1*I(loss) + beta2*I(tie) + beta3*x + eps, and the reported statistic is corr(residuals) between x and gf. Results (partial correlation with outcome-adjusted gf, n=38): spell_fx 0.272 (strongest), mean_forward 0.169, final_third 0.124, mean_speed 0.103, mean_spell 0.074, max_spell 0.073, pen_third 0.060, passes 0.059, n_spells -0.034, smart_share 0.022. Outcome profile: winners averaged 50.5 spells (vs 43.8 ties, 47.7 losses), max spell 30.8 (vs 24.2/28.7); 2H/1H shift is 0.72 for wins, 0.83 for ties, 1.10 for losses (winners sustain output; losers frantically increase it). Opponent conditioning (strong = Opponent2,3,4,5,9,13, the six with worst combined gd; 12 matches, 9 losses/3 draws, gd in [-4,0]): final_third +0.172, mean_spell -0.367, n_spells -0.322, smart_share -0.313; against the other 26 matches: final_third +0.026, mean_spell +0.185, n_spells -0.013. The same indicators flip sign by opponent class — the conditioning the expert described is empirically present.

### Outcome Analysis

The teamwork model is a spell-based decomposition: volume indicators (how many spells, how long) are weakly related to result (|r| <= 0.075), while quality indicators (how far each spell advances the ball, how deep it plays) are the strongest teamwork signals (spell_fx 0.272). Strategies are NOT universally effective: against the six strongest opponents, long spells and high spell counts correlate negatively with the result while final-third penetration still helps — prolonged possession without forward threat is punished by organised defences, the opponent-conditioning mechanism identified in exchange 2. Against weaker opponents, the same volume indicators are neutral-to-positive. Limitations: n=38 makes every coefficient uncertain to about +/-0.3, so single-indicator claims are planning-level, not operational; the strong-opponent stratum has 12 matches and is 75% losses, so its correlations are driven by a mostly-losing context; pen_third is a coarse proxy for 'chance creation' because shot events are not in the passing file; no player-tracking data means off-ball movement (a real coordination channel) is invisible.

### Problem

Use the teamwork model to advise the Huskies coach on which structural strategies were effective this season and what network-analysis-based changes to make next season to improve success.

### Analysis

The advice follows only indicators that cleared the decision threshold from task 2 (|partial correlation| >= 0.1 with consistent direction, per the uncertainty standard of exchange 3: with 38 matches, below this band a claim is indistinguishable from noise). Assumption: the squad can keep its existing position labels (1 GK, 10 D, 13 M, 6 F) but adjust roles and, per exchange 3, roster dual-competence rather than a single style. The three actionable findings are: (1) spell forward progression is the strongest teamwork signal; (2) final-third penetration helps overall and specifically against strong opponents; (3) long spells and high spell counts backfire against strong opponents.

### Modeling Process

Recommendation logic, stated as decision rules computed from the model: RULE 1 (build progression into spells): next season's in-game target is spell_fx, not pass count; a spell that advances the ball less than the team's 3.9 x-unit/pass average is a candidate to end early with a launch or direct pass. Rationale: spell_fx 0.272 is the largest partial correlation of any indicator, and forward progress per 10-minute window already rises from 3.14 to 4.99 across a match — the team already accelerates late; the rule extends that to spell-by-spell control. RULE 2 (penetrate the final third against strong blocks): against the strong-opponent class, play to maximize pen_third/final_third (+0.172 there, +0.124 season) — i.e. arrive with numbers in the final third (zone share is only 20% of passes today, versus 50% middle-third) rather than circulating in midfield. Rationale: this is the only indicator that is positive in both strata. RULE 3 (do not over-possess strong opponents): against the six toughest teams, mean spell length and spell count both correlate negatively with result (-0.367, -0.322) while the team went 0-9-3 in those fixtures; the structural prescription is to shorten spells (more launches, fewer recirculation chains) and convert possession into penetration. RULE 4 (keep the hub structure): the existing GK/back-line hub topology (degree G 969, D 850; betweenness G 10.7, D 9.7) is a strength, not a defect — the mild disassortativity (-0.08) means the hubs spread play to many secondary nodes; do not concentrate play on one or two 'smart' players (smart_share 0.02, no association with result). RULE 5 (adaptability, per exchange 3): roster and train for both spell styles — patient build-up (long spells, useful vs weak/low opponents where mean_spell +0.185) and quick transition (short spells, penetration-first) — so the team has an answer when the opponent's counter-strategy removes the conditions of one style; judge in-season performance by spell quality (spell_fx, pen_third), with spell length/count as diagnostic only.

### Outcome Analysis

What worked this season: the dense, hub-routed short-passing network (density 0.754, 5,143 triangles) that keeps the ball moving with low individual risk, plus a late-match acceleration in forward progress (3.14 -> 4.99 x-units/pass). What did not: the 0-9-3 record against the six strongest opponents, where long possession spells (mean_spell -0.367) and high spell volume (n_spells -0.322) coincided with failure — possession without penetration. The concrete next-season change set: (i) shorten spells against top opponents and shift origin zones from middle to final third (20% -> higher); (ii) keep the GK/defensive hub structure; (iii) train dual spell-style competence; (iv) track spell_fx and pen_third as the operational KPIs. Bias caveat: rules 2-3 rest on a 12-match strong-opponent stratum, so they should be re-validated as the new season's data accrues; the model contains no defensive-side indicators (no opponent pressing data), so 'shorten spells against a press' is inferred from the outcome pattern, not from press measurements.

### Problem

Generalize the soccer findings to the design of more effective teams in general (interdisciplinary groups, societies), and state what additional aspects of teamwork a generalized model of team performance would need to capture.

### Analysis

The soccer setting is 'controlled' in the sense that the task (score goals), the unit (the possession spell), the links (passes) and the outcome (goal difference) are all directly observed, unlike in most social teams. The transferable structure is therefore: (a) a team is a directed weighted interaction network, and its robustness comes from hub-routed, locally cliquish structure rather than from star nodes (eigenvector vs betweenness separation); (b) the atomic unit of work is an episode (the possession spell), and performance correlates with episode quality (progress made toward the goal) rather than episode count or duration (exchange 2); (c) effectiveness is conditional on the 'opponent' — the environment's counter-strategy — so a style optimized for one environment is brittle (exchange 3); (d) a small team's decision-relevant signal must be thresholded against the uncertainty of small samples (exchange 3). Assumption in generalizing: 'opponent' generalizes to the external constraints, competing groups, or countervailing processes a team faces.

### Modeling Process

Generalized design rules, one per transferred finding: (1) Network design: build directed, weighted interaction networks with distributed hubs (high betweenness on 2-3 backbone nodes, e.g. the GK/back line) and high local clustering (triads) — this gives robustness to node loss (a substitute player) because alternative paths exist; avoid star topologies where one person's absence disconnects the team (the Huskies' mild disassortativity, r = -0.08, is the healthy case: hubs connect to many, not to other hubs only). (2) Process design: measure and optimize episode quality — the goal-relevant progress per episode (spell_fx analogue: units of task progress per work episode) — and treat episode count/duration as diagnostic; this is the direct transfer of the finding that spell_fx (0.272) dominates spell volume (<=0.075). (3) Environmental conditioning: do not prescribe one working style; calibrate the process to the counter-strategy of the environment, exactly as spell length should be shortened against strong opponents and extended against weak ones; the model of a team must therefore be a two-level model (team process x environment state -> outcome), not a single-level one. (4) Adaptability as a roster property: the roster should contain dual-competent members who can execute both styles (patient build-up / quick transfer), because a single-style team is left without a viable plan once the environment invalidates the conditions that style relies on. (5) Statistical discipline: with small n, threshold claims at the level of the correlation's standard error (~0.3 at n=38) before acting on them — the thresholding rule adopted in task 2.

### Outcome Analysis

What a generalized model of team performance must add beyond this soccer model: (i) heterogeneous, evolving ties — passes are observed links; in social teams most interaction (especially informal and off-ball/off-record) is unobserved, so the model needs a latent-link or sampling component and longitudinal tie evolution (the season network here is a static average over 38 games); (ii) a defensive/opponent side — here only the Huskies' events are analyzed; a full model needs the opposing team's process (pressing, counter-strategy) as a coupled network, since the opponent-conditioning effect is the key environmental finding; (iii) individual state and fatigue — tempo dips late and after stoppages (exchange 1), but the data has no physiological state; generalized models need per-member state variables (fatigue, morale, skill drift); (iv) hierarchy and leadership — the data has a single CoachID across the season, so leadership effect cannot be identified; generalized models need explicit leadership and communication-channel terms; (v) multi-goal and delayed outcomes — a soccer match's outcome is immediate and scalar; most real teams optimize multiple, delayed objectives (innovation quality, learning, reputation), needing a multi-objective or discounted-reward outcome structure; (vi) selection effects — the squad is fixed by the club, whereas designed teams choose members; a generalized design model needs the composition step (who to hire) as a first-class input, which this dataset cannot inform. Limitation of the generalization itself: the transfer rests on one team, one season, one sport; the conditional (opponent-dependent) effects in particular are estimated on 12 matches, so they are hypotheses for other settings, not validated laws.
