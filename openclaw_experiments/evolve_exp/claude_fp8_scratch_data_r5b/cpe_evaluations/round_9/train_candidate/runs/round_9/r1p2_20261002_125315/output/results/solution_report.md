# Solution

## Subtask 1: Subtask 1 — Data cleaning and characterization of the supplied 2017 MCM Problem C dataset for the four roads of interest

### Problem

Subtask 1 — Data cleaning and characterization of the supplied 2017 MCM Problem C dataset for the four roads of interest (I-5, I-90, I-405, SR-520) in Thurston, Pierce, King, and Snohomish counties. Goal: produce a clean, per-segment table of demand (average daily traffic count), lane count, and road type that the capacity model can consume. Scope: all 224 data segments in 2017_MCM_Problem_C_Data.csv.

### Analysis

The raw file is a per-milepost-segment inventory. Assumptions: (1) each row is one directed segment between two mileposts; (2) the two lane columns (DECR MP and INCR MP directions) are the two opposite directions, and the peak-direction lane count is the larger of the two, since peak flow concentrates in the direction with more lanes (e.g. southbound I-5 into the city); (3) 'Average daily traffic counts Year_2015' is the 24-hour, all-day average daily traffic (AADT) for that segment, not a peak-hour figure. Repairs performed: (a) the file carries a UTF-8 BOM on the first header cell, removed on read; (b) the 'Comments' column is free text with missing values and is not used in the model; (c) no duplicate segments after inspection (milepost intervals are contiguous and non-overlapping per route); (d) lane counts are integers 2-5 with no missing values; (e) ADCT values are all positive integers 13,000-242,000. No rows were dropped. Result: 224 clean segments — I-5: 135, I-405: 47, I-90: 27 (24 IS + 3 SR), SR-520: 15. Network mean ADCT is 139,938 vehicles/day.

### Modeling Process

Data schema after cleaning (one row per segment): Route_ID (5, 90, 405, 520), startMilepost, endMilepost, ADCT (veh/day, Year_2015), RteType (IS/SR), Lanes = max(Lanes_DECR, Lanes_INCR), seg_len = endMilepost - startMilepost (mi). Lane counts observed: 3-lane (116 segs), 4-lane (45), 2-lane (32), 5-lane (10), plus asymmetric 4/3 (11), 3/4 (5), 2/3 (4), 5/4 (1). Per-route demand: I-5 sum ADCT = 21,098,000; I-405 = 6,693,000; I-90 = 2,499,000; SR-520 = 1,056,000. The lane-mix differentiates the corridors: I-90 and SR-520 carry a meaningful share of 2-lane (state-route) segments, while I-5 is predominantly 3-4 lane and I-405 a mix — which is why the same AV share produces different corridor loads across routes.

### Outcome Analysis

The cleaned dataset is a demand-and-geometry inventory with no time dimension, no speed, and no peak/off-peak split. This drives the whole model's calibration burden: every peak-hour quantity must be derived from the daily average. The largest data gap is that AADT understates peak-hour bottleneck intensity (it is a 24h, segment-length average, not a point reading at the merges and lane drops that actually jam). The data are fit for corridor-level planning (how loaded is each road overall) but not for pinpointing exact jam severity at a specific interchange; the model is used accordingly and the gap is carried as an explicit uncertainty band rather than hidden.

## Subtask 2: Subtask 2 — Model of the effect on traffic flow of the number of lanes, peak/average traffic volume, and the percentage 

### Problem

Subtask 2 — Model of the effect on traffic flow of the number of lanes, peak/average traffic volume, and the percentage of vehicles using self-driving cooperating systems; applied to the four roads at AV shares of 10%, 50%, and 90%. Goal: quantify how per-lane capacity and corridor load change as the AV share rises, and whether free-flow equilibria exist at each share. Scope: all four routes, AV share swept 0-100% with the three reported points 10/50/90% highlighted.

### Analysis

Method: a per-lane fundamental diagram with a hard capacity, chosen because dense near-capacity flow is governed by a jam/shockwave equilibrium rather than smooth unbounded flow (a small disturbance amplifies backward into stop-and-go once flow approaches capacity; well below capacity flow is smooth). A corridor is a series of segments, so its throughput is set by its least-capable segment (a series bottleneck) and a free-flow equilibrium exists only while the worst segment load is below 1. Soundness: the model is deliberately low-dimensional (one capacity parameter per lane, scaled by AV share) so that the qualitative conclusions — which regimes jam, and how much capacity is added per AV share — rest on the data and on a small number of documented calibration constants, not on a high-parameter fit that would overfit 224 segments.

### Modeling Process

Variables: s = AV share (0-1); C(s) = per-lane capacity (veh/h); ADCT_i = daily traffic on segment i; L_i = lanes on segment i; phi = peak-hour fraction of AADT; kappa = bottleneck-concentration factor. Parameter table (calibrated inputs, name = value, interval, source): HUMAN_LANE_CAP = 1800 veh/h/lane, interval [1600, 2200], source: standard freeway per-lane design capacity (HCM 6th ed. Exhibit 23-1 typical freeway basic-segment capacity 1,800-2,200 veh/h/lane; conservative 1,800 used) [reference: Highway Capacity Manual, 6th ed., Transportation Research Board]; AV_LANE_CAP_FACTOR = 1.6, interval [1.4, 2.0], source: cooperative connected AVs shorten the following time gap from ~1.0 s to ~0.5-0.6 s, raising per-lane capacity; the 1.6 midpoint reflects that lane-change and merge dynamics, not steady car-following, bind in mixed traffic [reference: The Impact of Automated Vehicles on Traffic Flow and Road Capacity on Urban Road Networks, doi:10.1155/2021/8404951]; FREE_FLOW_SPEED = 65 mph, interval [55, 75], source: posted design speed on I-5/I-90/I-405/SR-520 WA segments (dataset context); JAM_DENSITY = 150 veh/mi/lane, interval [120, 180], source: standard blocked freeway density [reference: Highway Capacity Manual, 6th ed.]; phi (PEAK_HOUR_FRACTION) = 0.10, interval [0.08, 0.15], source: exchange 2 (AADT is a 24h average; ~10-15% of daily volume concentrates in the peak hour); kappa (BOTTLENECK_CONCENTRATION) = 2.0, interval [1.5, 3.0], source: exchange 2 (segment-average AADT understates point peak-hour bottleneck intensity by a factor of order 2). Equations: C(s) = C_human * (1 + (AV_FACTOR - 1) * s), a linear interpolation between the all-human lane (C_human) and the all-AV lane (C_human * AV_FACTOR) — linear, not super-linear, because it does not assume AVs self-platoon in a way a single uncoordinated human cannot disrupt. Peak-hour demand per segment: q_i = phi * ADCT_i. Per-lane demand: vhl_i = q_i / L_i. Segment load: load_i = vhl_i / C(s). Corridor (length/flow-weighted) load: load_corr = sum_i q_i / sum_i L_i * C(s). Bottleneck load: load_bott = max over the top-5%-of-ADCT/L segments of kappa * phi * ADCT_i / (L_i * C(s)) — the concentration factor applies only to the natural bottlenecks, matching the spatial bias that segment averages understate exactly those points. Free-flow equilibrium exists iff max_i load_i < 1. All quantities computed by code/model.py over the sweep.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (per-lane capacity and loads; full table in results/model_results.json): C(s) rises from 1800 (s=0) to 1908 (10%), 2340 (50%), 2772 (90%). Corridor load at the reported shares: I-5: 2.29 (10%), 1.87 (50%), 1.58 (90%); I-405: 2.68, 2.18, 1.84; I-90: 1.66, 1.35, 1.14; SR-520: 1.84, 1.50, 1.27. Bottleneck (concentrated) load is higher still: I-5 8.46/6.89/5.82; I-405 9.28/7.56/6.39; I-90 5.66/4.62/3.90; SR-520 5.71/4.66/3.93. Key findings: (1) Every route's bottleneck is saturated (load >> 1) at all tested AV shares, including 0% — under the AADT-derived peak demand, these corridors are structurally over-capacity today and at 10/50/90% AV. (2) A free-flow equilibrium does NOT exist on any route at any tested share; the best case (I-90 at 90% AV) still has a corridor load of 1.14 and a bottleneck load of 3.9. (3) The relative relief of the bottleneck by AVs is identical across routes (bottleneck is a minimum-lane segment in each): the bottleneck load falls by 5.7% (10% AV), 23.1% (50%), 35.1% (90%) relative to the all-human baseline. (4) The benefit of AVs is real but bounded: a 1.6x per-lane capacity factor at 90% share cannot offset a corridor whose demand is 1.5-2.7x its (already-boosted) capacity. Limitations and biases: the absolute load magnitudes inherit the AADT -> peak-hour conversion (phi) and the concentration factor (kappa); a factor-of-two error in either flips the exact load number but NOT the qualitative conclusion that all four corridors jam. The linear C(s) is conservative — it overstates the human drag on AVs in mixed traffic, so the true capacity at a given share is likely at least as high as modeled, which only strengthens the 'no equilibrium' finding. The model does not resolve shockwave propagation speed or queue spillback; it is a capacity/planning model, not a microscopic simulator.

## Subtask 3: Subtask 3 — Equilibria, tipping points, and the dedicated-lane policy question. Goal: state whether equilibria exist, id

### Problem

Subtask 3 — Equilibria, tipping points, and the dedicated-lane policy question. Goal: state whether equilibria exist, identify any tipping point where performance changes markedly, and answer under what conditions (if any) lanes should be dedicated to self-driving cars, plus any other policy changes the model suggests. Scope: the four routes at the tested AV shares, with the dedicated-lane question evaluated as a cross-section trade-off.

### Analysis

Equilibria: a free-flow equilibrium exists only where the worst segment load is below 1; a jammed (metastable) equilibrium exists where demand exceeds capacity and the corridor sits in the congested branch of the fundamental diagram. Tipping point: the AV share at which a route's load crosses a decision-relevant threshold (here, saturation, load = 1). Dedicated lane: evaluated as a 4-lane cross-section trade-off — one lane reserved for AVs (carrying the AV fleet at the pure-AV capacity) vs. the same four lanes all mixed — because the decision-break is a sign-level question (does the dedicated lane add or remove net capacity), and a few-percent error in the magnitude does not change the recommendation while a sign error does.

### Modeling Process

Equilibrium indicator: eq_exists(route, s) = (max_i load_i < 1). Tipping point search: bisect s in [0,1] for the first share at which the bottleneck load drops below 1.0 (min_av_share_to_clear). Dedicated-lane trade-off on a 4-lane cross-section at AV share s: with no dedicated lane, cross-section capacity = 4 * C(s). With one AV-reserved lane, capacity = min(C_AV, s * C(s)) + 3 * C(s), where C_AV = C(1) is the pure-AV lane capacity and s*C(s) is the AV fleet's demand (the AV lane is only as useful as the AV fleet is large to fill it); the 3 remaining lanes carry the human fleet at the mixed capacity. Net gain = with_dedicated - no_dedicated; the lane is beneficial iff gain > 0. Computed in code/model.py.

### Outcome Analysis

Equilibria: no free-flow equilibrium on any route at 10/50/90% AV (or at 0%). All four corridors are in the jammed regime — demand exceeds even the AV-boosted capacity — so the relevant equilibrium is the congested one, and AVs shift the corridor along that congested branch (lower load, shorter queues, lower delay) rather than switching it to free flow. Tipping point: there is NO AV share at which any route clears to free flow (even at 100% AV the bottleneck load stays above 1), so the model finds no 'AV tipping point' in the sense of a share that flips a route to uncongested. The performance change that IS marked is the dedicated-lane decision: the net capacity of a reserved lane rises monotonically from -1717 veh/h (10% AV) to -277 veh/h (90% AV) on a 4-lane cross-section and never crosses zero, i.e. a dedicated AV lane REMOVES general-purpose capacity at every tested share and becomes defensible only at AV penetration well above 90%. Dedicated-lane answer: do NOT dedicate lanes at 10%, 50%, or even 90% AV share — the AV fleet is too small to use a reserved lane efficiently, and the lost general-purpose lane costs more than the AV lane returns. Other policy changes the model supports: (a) the binding constraint is peak-hour bottleneck intensity at the lane drops and ramps, not average corridor load — so ramp metering, extended merge tapers, and targeted capacity at the identified bottleneck segments (the top ADCT/lane segments) would relieve congestion at current AV penetration far more than a blanket AV policy; (b) because the benefit of AVs scales with their share and with platoon formation, the larger capacity upside is in letting AVs coordinate in mixed traffic (shared-lane operation with V2V) rather than segregating them into a dedicated lane; (c) the corridors are structurally over-capacity even at high AV penetration, so demand management (transit, staggered travel) and, where feasible, adding lanes at the specific bottlenecks are more reliable than AV penetration alone. Uncertainty: the dedicated-lane sign conclusion is the one that would change the policy if wrong; it is robust as long as the AV fleet is below roughly the capacity ratio C_AV/C_human, which under the 1.4-2.0 factor interval holds through ~90% share. The 'no free-flow equilibrium' finding is robust to a factor-of-two in phi or kappa because the loads are 1.5-9x above saturation.
