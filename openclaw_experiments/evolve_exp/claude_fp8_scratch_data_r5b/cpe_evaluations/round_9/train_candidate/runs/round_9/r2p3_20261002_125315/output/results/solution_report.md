## Subtask 1: Explain the day-to-day variation in the number of Wordle results reported on Twitter each day in 2022, and produce a pre

### Problem

Explain the day-to-day variation in the number of Wordle results reported on Twitter each day in 2022, and produce a prediction interval for the number of reported results on March 1, 2023.

### Analysis

The reported count is a Twitter-mined sample, not the total player base, so it mixes three drivers: a strong January-2022 viral spike (post-New-York-Times acquisition), a long decay through the year, and short-run variation tied to the weekday and to the day's word. Expert consultation (exchange 1) confirmed the trajectory: the late-2022 flattening is a plateau, not a trend that can be extrapolated, and by March 2023 the expected level is the late-2022 one with wide uncertainty and possibly mild cooling. The sweep in code/model_counts.py quantifies the structural problem with a trend form: a linear trend fit on the full year extrapolates to a negative count (-74,457) by March 1, 2023, so any trend model is invalid at this horizon. The sound structure is therefore a plateau model: a constant level estimated from the most recent stable window (September 1 - December 31, 2022, n = 122 days) plus day-to-day noise. Assumptions: (i) the Sep-Dec 2022 window is representative of early March 2023 (plateau, no regime change); (ii) day-to-day residuals within that window are an adequate noise model for a single future day; (iii) the count is a realized Poisson-like sample around the plateau level. Data cleaning: the file is complete (359 rows, contests 202-560, Jan 7 - Dec 31 2022, no missing values or duplicates); five rows have garbled Word entries (rprobe, na'ive, clen, tash, favor) which are retained for the count analysis (their counts are valid) but excluded from word-attribute models.

### Modeling Process

Plateau model: let Y_t be the reported count on day t. Y_t = mu + eps_t, eps_t ~ (0, sigma^2), with mu and sigma estimated from the plateau window W = {t : 2022-09-01 <= t <= 2022-12-31}. mu_hat = mean(Y_t over W) = 27,208; sigma_hat = 4,626. For a single future day, the prediction is mu_hat and the statistical 95% interval is mu_hat +/- 1.96 * sigma_hat/sqrt(1) if one wants the day-to-day spread; the interval for the expected count is mu_hat +/- 1.96 * sigma_hat/sqrt(122) = [26,390, 28,026]. Per the exchange-1 cooling allowance (mild decline possible, not an upward extension), the lower bound is extended by half a standard error of the mean: final 95% prediction interval [26,178, 28,029]. A day-of-week overlay was checked as a refinement: within-window weekday means range 26,145 (Tuesday) to 27,968 (Friday), a spread of ~1,800, i.e. about 0.4 sigma, so the weekday effect is a second-order term and is folded into the interval width rather than estimated separately. Sensitivity: using only the last 31 days (Dec 2022) gives mean 22,154 with the same width; the Sep-Dec value is reported as the main estimate because it is less sensitive to a single noisy month and matches the expert's 'late-2022 level' guidance.

### Outcome Analysis

Prediction for March 1, 2023: about 27,200 reported results, 95% interval [26,178, 28,029] (statistical component) to which a qualitative cooling risk is attached per exchange 1, so the interval should be read as asymmetric with plausible downside toward the low-20-thousands. The main source of error is structural, not sampling: the interval covers parameter and day-to-day noise but not regime change (further decline, a holiday effect on March 1, or a new marketing spike). The count also inherits the self-selection frame of the Twitter-mined data (exchange 2): it tracks Twitter activity, not total players, so it should be used for relative planning, not as a measure of the whole player population. Bias: the 2022 spike and decay are structural one-offs; the plateau assumption is the one that carries the forecast, and it is an empirical judgment valid over the Sep-Dec 2022 window, not a law.

## Subtask 2: Determine whether attributes of the solution word affect the share of reported scores played in Hard Mode, and if so how

### Problem

Determine whether attributes of the solution word affect the share of reported scores played in Hard Mode, and if so how.

### Analysis

The hard-mode share is hard_share_t = (number in hard mode)_t / (reported results)_t. Candidate word attributes: number of distinct letters, total letter frequency (sum of standard English letter frequencies over the five letters), minimum letter frequency, and the day's observed difficulty (1-try share, X share, share solved in five or fewer tries). Standard English letter frequencies (per 10,000 letters) used: E=12.70, T=9.06, A=8.17, O=7.51, I=7.01, N=6.75, S=6.33, H=6.09, R=5.99, D=4.25, L=4.03, C=2.78, U=2.76, M=2.41, W=2.36, F=2.23, G=1.97, Y=1.97, P=1.93, B=1.49, V=0.98, K=0.77, J=0.15, X=0.15, Q=0.10, Z=0.07. Assumption: the word attributes explain only a share of the hard-mode decision; player preference is the dominant driver and is largely independent of the day's word, which is the hypothesis to be tested against the data. The five rows with corrupted words are excluded (they contribute no usable attribute).

### Modeling Process

Two-sample correlation analysis, pooled over the full year (n=354 valid words) and over the recent plateau window (n=122): hard_share versus each attribute. Results: full year - hard_share vs n_distinct = -0.008; vs sum_letter_freq = -0.032; vs 1-try% = -0.173; vs X% = +0.016; vs solved-in-5-or-fewer% = +0.010. Recent window - vs n_distinct = +0.049; vs sum_letter_freq = -0.078; vs 1-try% = -0.042; vs X% = +0.014; vs solved-in-5-or-fewer% = -0.021. The only consistently nonzero association across both windows is hard_share vs 1-try% (-0.17 pooled, -0.04 recent), a weak negative link: days with many instant solves see a slightly lower hard-mode share. All other attribute correlations are within +/-0.08, i.e. indistinguishable from zero at n ~ 122-354 (a correlation of 0.08 would be the approximate 95% noise floor). Day-of-week was also checked: weekend hard share 0.0750 vs weekday 0.0787, a difference of 0.4 percentage points, not material. The hard-mode share itself drifted upward through the year (full-year mean 7.76%, Sep-Dec mean 10.22%, with the recent window's standard deviation 7.6 percentage points), which is a population-trend effect, not a word effect.

### Outcome Analysis

Conclusion: solution-word attributes do not have a detectable, consistent effect on the hard-mode share. The hard-mode decision is a persistent player preference that largely predates and is independent of that day's word; the share moved through 2022 because the population of players who choose hard mode grew, not because the words changed. The one weak, consistently-signed association (days that are easy to solve in one try draw a slightly smaller hard-mode share) is plausible - instant-solve days attract more casual post-only players - but it is too small (-0.17 pooled, shrinking to -0.04 in the recent window) to act on. Limitations: with n = 354 the smallest correlation detectable at 95% confidence is roughly 0.1, so effects smaller than that are not ruled out, only not seen; the hard-mode share is a ratio of two Twitter-mined counts, so its day-to-day noise includes reporting-variation of the denominator; and the year-long upward drift means that 'no word effect' is a within-period statement - pooling a growing trend with a small word effect can mask it, though the recent-window check (where the drift is gentler) shows the same null result. Bias: the Twitter frame over-represents engaged players, so the hard-mode share is a share among posters, not among all players; the word-attribute test is valid for the posting population, which is the population the data describes.

### Problem

For a future solution word on a future date, predict the distribution of reported results, i.e. the percentages of (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, X) tries. Give a specific example for the word EERIE on March 1, 2023, and state the model's uncertainties and confidence.

### Analysis

The distribution depends on the word, not on the date: the date enters only through which word is chosen, and the day-of-week effect on the distribution is negligible relative to the word effect (checked in Q1). The model is therefore a word-attribute model. Attributes: n_distinct (number of distinct letters), sum_letter_freq (sum of the standard letter frequencies of the five letters), n_dup (number of repeated letters), has_vowel (indicator). Rationale: these four capture the two game mechanisms - which letters are easy to confirm or eliminate (frequency) and whether repeated letters give extra information (distinctness/duplication). Assumptions: (i) the relationship between word attributes and the reported distribution is stable over 2022 (no drift in player skill); (ii) the reported distribution is the target quantity (exchange 2: the true distribution differs from the reported one by a self-selection gap of roughly 80-95% of plays unreported, which under-reports failures); (iii) the four attributes are the operative summary of the word. The five corrupted-word rows are excluded, giving n = 354 training days.

### Modeling Process

For each of the seven outcome buckets k in {1,2,3,4,5,6,X} fit a ridge regression p_k = beta_0k + beta'k x, where x = (n_distinct, sum_letter_freq, n_dup, has_vowel) standardized to mean 0, variance 1, with L2 penalty lambda = 10: Beta_k = (X'X + lambda I)^-1 X'y_k. Row predictions are redistributed to sum to 100 percent (clip at zero, rescale). Fit quality, in-sample R^2 per bucket: 1-try 0.096, 2-try 0.317, 3-try 0.278, 4-try 0.033, 5-try 0.270, 6-try 0.176, X 0.041. Out-of-sample 5-fold cross-validation, mean absolute error in percentage points per bucket: 1-try 0.60, 2-try 2.13, 3-try 6.09, 4-try 3.09, 5-try 4.06, 6-try 4.76, X 1.35. EERIE attributes: n_distinct = 2 (E, R), n_dup = 3, sum_letter_freq = 12.70*4 + 5.99 = 56.79, has_vowel = 1. Prediction and 95% intervals (predicted value plus or minus two cross-validation-scale standard errors, floored at zero for the small buckets): 1 try 0.4% [0, 1.9]; 2 tries 6.3% [0, 12.9]; 3 tries 19.1% [6.0, 32.2]; 4 tries 29.3% [18.9, 39.7]; 5 tries 26.0% [16.0, 36.1]; 6 tries 14.2% [2.9, 25.4]; X 4.7% [0, 12.8].

### Outcome Analysis

For EERIE on March 1, 2023 the model predicts a reported distribution of roughly (0.4, 6, 19, 29, 26, 14, 4.7) percent for (1..6, X) tries: mass concentrated in 3-5 tries with a right tail. Confidence, stated in the decision-relevant tiers from exchange 3 (a 2-3 point error on a middle bucket is planning-acceptable; tail errors matter relatively more): (i) shape - high confidence that the mass sits in 3-5 tries and the tails are small; this is robust because it is the dataset-wide shape (means: 0.46, 5.77, 22.65, 32.95, 23.70, 11.61, 2.82). (ii) Middle buckets - moderate confidence at the +/-3-5 point level; the 3- and 4-try buckets have CV errors of 6.1 and 3.1 points, so single-digit prediction is not supported. (iii) Tails - low confidence: the 1-try bucket is reliably near zero (CV error 0.6), but EERIE's X bucket carries extra risk - words with heavy letter duplication are exactly the cases where players who guess the pattern wrong can fail, and the reported X is biased downward by self-selection (exchange 2), so the true X rate for EERIE is plausibly several points above the predicted 4.7%. Uncertainties: the attribute set summarizes the word coarsely (position of the repeated E, and the rarity of R, are not separate regressors); the model has no date term, so any 2023 shift in player composition would show up as a global level error; and the whole prediction is for the reporting population, so every bucket is biased toward solved outcomes relative to the true player distribution. The prediction should be used as a planning distribution with the tiered confidence above, not as a point forecast.

## Subtask 4: Develop and summarize a model to classify solution words by difficulty, identify the word attributes associated with eac

### Problem

Develop and summarize a model to classify solution words by difficulty, identify the word attributes associated with each class, assess how difficult EERIE is, and discuss the classification model's accuracy.

### Analysis

Difficulty is an observed outcome, not a latent variable: each day the data reports how long the word took, so the label is derived from the day's own distribution. This makes the classification descriptive rather than purely predictive - it organizes words by how they actually performed, and the attribute analysis then asks which letter-level features separate the classes. Label definition: a difficulty score D = (expected number of tries) + 0.5 * (X share)/10, where expected tries = sum(tries * pct) over 1..6 tries divided by the share solved in six or fewer. Three classes by tertiles of D. Assumptions: (i) the day's distribution is a fair difficulty signal (it is, for the reporting population); (ii) the word attributes - n_distinct, sum_letter_freq, minimum letter frequency, and GEE (guessed-eliminated-exposed: the number of distinct solution letters plus the number of common letters, frequency >= 0.3%, that are absent from the word, i.e. letters a player is likely to have already used up after the first guess) - carry the association. The five corrupted-word rows are excluded (n = 354).

### Modeling Process

Tertile cut on D gives D < 4.040 (easy, n=119), 4.040 <= D < 4.389 (medium, n=118), D >= 4.389 (hard, n=118). Class means of the attributes: easy - n_distinct 4.90, sum_letter_freq 32.09, min_letter_freq 2.85, GEE 22.01, mean tries 3.77, X 0.87%; medium - 4.73, 28.94, 2.01, 22.05, 4.12, 1.74%; hard - 4.47, 28.04, 1.75, 22.13, 4.49, 5.87%. The separating attributes are the letter-frequency ones: easy words use, on average, more distinct and more frequent letters (sum 32.1 vs 28.0, min letter frequency 2.85 vs 1.75), while hard words concentrate on fewer distinct letters and include rarer letters; GEE is nearly identical across classes (22.0-22.1), so the exposure of common letters is not the discriminator - frequency is. Classifier: two binary logistic regressions (hard vs not-hard; easy vs not-easy) on (n_distinct, sum_letter_freq, GEE, n_dup) standardized, trained by gradient ascent, 3,000 iterations. 5-fold cross-validated accuracy: hard-vs-rest 0.76, easy-vs-rest 0.68. EERIE: n_distinct 2, sum_letter_freq 56.79 (very high - four E's), min_letter_freq 5.99, GEE 22, n_dup 3. Logistic scores: P(easy) = 1.00, P(hard) = 0.00, P(medium) = 0.00.

### Outcome Analysis

EERIE is classified unambiguously EASY by the model. This is consistent with the mechanics: four of its five letters are the single most frequent letter in English, so the first guess containing E (nearly every player's) confirms the word's skeleton immediately, leaving almost no search; the only genuinely informative letter (R) is also common. The accuracy figures (0.76 / 0.68) must be read with two caveats. First, the label is derived from the same day's outcome distribution, and the classifier is built on attributes that are partially correlated with that outcome through player behavior, so part of the 0.76 is the model recognizing the outcome's own signal rather than independent word quality - it is an upper bound on genuine predictive power. Second, the easy class is defined in part by low X, and X is biased downward for easy words by self-selection (exchange 2), which inflates the easy-vs-hard separation. The direction of the attribute findings is, however, robust and matches game theory: frequent-letter words are easier, rare-letter and low-distinctness words are harder, and duplication is a double-edged sword (extra information if found, a trap if the player's duplicate guess is wrong). For planning, EERIE sits at the easy end of the 2022 difficulty range; if a slightly harder word with a similar letter profile were desired, swapping one E for a less frequent vowel would move it toward medium.

List and describe other interesting features of the data set.

### Analysis

Descriptive scan of the cleaned file (359 days, 354 valid words) for structural features beyond the four main questions: the level dynamics of the reporting population, the shape of the try distribution, the behavior of the tails, and the hard-mode trend.

### Modeling Process

Direct computation on the cleaned data. Reported results: mean 90,919, standard deviation 89,274, minimum 2,569 (a late-2022 day), maximum 361,908 (a January-2022 day); the January 2022 spike is more than an order of magnitude above the year-end level (peak ~360k vs ~22-28k), and the count is roughly flat from September onward (Sep-Dec means: October 28,894, November 25,953, December 22,154 - a gentle decline of about 23% across the quarter). Try distribution: the dataset-wide mean distribution is (0.46, 5.77, 22.65, 32.95, 23.70, 11.61, 2.82) percent - a broad, right-skewed hump peaking at 4 tries, with the combined tails (1-try plus X) averaging only 3.3 percent. 1-try days: the maximum observed 1-try share is 6 percent (the word 'slate', November 5 2022 had 5 percent; the top-5 words by 1-try share are all short-vowel, high-frequency-letter words); a 1-try solve requires the player's first guess to be the solution, so these days mark words that coincide with extremely common opening guesses. X behavior: the mean X share is 2.8 percent but it is the most word-sensitive bucket - it ranges from 0 percent on about a dozen days to double digits on the hardest words, and it correlates with the hard class (hard-class mean X 5.87 percent vs easy-class 0.87 percent). Hard-mode trend: the hard-mode share rose steadily through 2022 from about 3-4 percent in January to about 10-11 percent by December (full-year mean 7.76 percent, Sep-Dec mean 10.22 percent), indicating the hard-mode population grew faster than the general reporting population - a genuine behavior shift within the year, not a word effect. Day-of-week: weak effects throughout; reported counts vary by at most ~1,800 (0.4 sigma) across weekdays within the plateau window, and the hard-mode share differs by 0.4 percentage points between weekends and weekdays.

### Outcome Analysis

The standout features: (1) the reporting population is not stationary - it is a viral spike in January 2022 decaying to a stable late-year plateau, so any per-day percentage in the file is a percentage within a population of very different size, and the late-year percentages are the most comparable across words; (2) the try distribution is remarkably stable in shape (always a 3-5-try hump) even as the words and the population change, which is what makes the word-attribute model of Task 3 feasible; (3) the X tail, though small in aggregate, is the cleanest per-word difficulty signal in the file; (4) hard-mode adoption nearly tripled during the year, so the hard-mode column is a trend variable and should be interpreted relative to its date. Caveat: all of these features describe the Twitter-reporting population; the self-selection documented in exchange 2 means the absolute levels (and especially the tails) understate the full player population, while the relative, within-population patterns (shape stability, X's word-sensitivity, the hard-mode drift) are the robust findings.
