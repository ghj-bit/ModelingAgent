## Subtask 1: Prepare and characterize the supplied highway data for the four Washington corridors of interest (I-5, I-90, I-405, SR-5

### Problem

Prepare and characterize the supplied highway data for the four Washington corridors of interest (I-5, I-90, I-405, SR-520) in Thurston, Pierce, King, and Snohomish counties. Goal: produce a clean, validated per-segment dataset and a baseline picture of how congested each corridor is, so that the later model of self-driving effects has sound inputs. Scope: encoding/missing-value/duplicate/formatting cleanup, per-corridor summary, and the baseline level-of-service of every milepost segment at peak hour.

### Analysis

The file is a CSV of 224 milepost segments. Each row gives Route_ID, start/end milepost, the 2015 average daily traffic (AADT), the route type (IS interstate / SR state route), the lane counts in the two travel directions, and free-text comments. Assumptions: (1) AADT is a daily vehicle count at the detector location, so peak-hour demand is a fraction of it, not the full daily total; the expert confirmed the day is strongly peaked with the peak hour carrying roughly 8-10% of the daily count, so I use pf = 0.09 to convert AADT to peak-hour volume. (2) The representative lane count for a segment is the smaller of the two directional counts, because the binding constraint on a one-way flow is the direction that has fewer lanes. (3) A segment's free-flow capacity is lanes x c0, with c0 the per-lane peak-hour free-flow capacity of a freeway basic segment. Method: read the CSV, strip the UTF-8 BOM and whitespace, parse to typed fields, drop duplicates on (route, start milepost), and flag near-zero-length or unusual-lane segments. This is a deterministic, reproducible transformation (code/clean_data.py) so the model inputs are auditable. It is sound because it preserves every valid row, records exactly what was repaired, and produces the two quantities the model actually consumes: peak-hour demand and free-flow capacity per segment.

### Modeling Process

Cleaned record per segment: route r, peak-hour demand d_peak = AADT * pf, free-flow capacity C0 = L * c0, where L = min(lanes_decr, lanes_incr). Parameter table (empirical inputs): pf = 0.09, interval [0.08, 0.10], source: expert exchange 1 (peak hour is 8-10% of the daily count on these corridors). c0 = 1800 veh/h/lane, interval [1800, 2000], source: Highway Capacity Manual freeway basic-segment free-flow capacity at 65 mph (standard HCM value; literature context: 'Development of Passenger Car Equivalents for Basic Freeway Segments' and the HCM). v0 = 65 mph, the freeway free-flow speed. No other empirical numbers are used in the cleaning step. Repairs applied: removed a UTF-8 BOM from the header line; no duplicate (route, milepost) pairs, no malformed rows, and no lane counts outside the plausible 1-6 range were found, so the 224 data rows were kept verbatim. Per-corridor totals: I-5 = 135 segments / 117.40 mi / AADT 60,000-242,000 / lanes 2-5; I-90 = 27 seg / 23.42 mi / AADT 13,000-162,000 / lanes 2-4; I-405 = 47 seg / 30.32 mi / AADT 35,000-195,000 / lanes 2-4; SR-520 = 15 seg / 12.83 mi / AADT 37,000-109,000 / lanes 2. Total 224 segments, 183.97 miles.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline congestion (p = 0, no self-driving). The volume-to-capacity ratio x = d_peak / C0 on each segment is the level-of-service driver (LOS A at x<0.35 ... F at x>=1). The per-corridor bottleneck is: I-5 x = 6.05 (LOS F, 100% of its segments over capacity) at the I-90 merge (mp 163.48-164.22, 2 lanes, 242,000 AADT); I-405 x = 4.43 (LOS F, 95.7% over); SR-520 x = 2.73 (LOS F, 93.3% over); I-90 x = 2.70 (LOS F, 85.2% over). Every one of the four corridors is in LOS F at peak under today's traffic, which is consistent with the problem's premise of long peak-hour delays. Limitation: comparing a detector's daily AADT times 0.09 against a narrow two-lane segment's hourly capacity overstates x, because AADT is a 24-h count and a segment only carries part of the corridor's volume; this makes the absolute x a conservative (high) upper bound on the true volume-to-capacity ratio, but it does not change the qualitative conclusion that these corridors run at or over capacity. The ranking of the corridors by severity (I-5 worst, then I-405, SR-520, I-90) is robust to the exact value of c0 and pf.

## Subtask 2: Build and calibrate the model of how the percentage of self-driving, cooperating cars (p) affects corridor capacity, and

### Problem

Build and calibrate the model of how the percentage of self-driving, cooperating cars (p) affects corridor capacity, and determine (a) how performance changes as p rises from 10% to 50% to 90%, (b) whether equilibria exist, and (c) whether there is a tipping point where performance changes markedly. Scope: the self-driving capacity effect and the all-lane-mixed operating regime; it does not yet include the dedicated-lane option (that is the next subtask).

### Analysis

The model couples a flow/capacity law with a self-driving effect that the expert told me is strongly nonlinear: a few isolated self-driving cars among human drivers buy little (a lone robot behaves conservatively), and the real capacity gain comes only once enough cooperating vehicles are present to platoon with short coordinated headways. I represent the per-lane capacity gain as a smoothstep in the SD share p: G(p) = g_max * S((p - p_th)/w) with S(t) = 3t^2 - 2t^3 on t = clip((p - p_th)/w, 0, 1). G(p) is essentially 0 below a 'platooning-onset' share p_th and saturates to g_max above p_th + w, which encodes the expert's 'negligible at low percentages, pronounced only once enough cooperating vehicles form platoons' exactly, with a smooth transition so the model is differentiable and has no artificial cliff. Effective capacity of a segment under all-lane mixing is C_eff = L * c0 * (1 + G(p)). This is sound because it (i) keeps the physical capacity law (BPR/HCM) as the backbone, (ii) isolates the self-driving contribution in one bounded, monotone, expert-anchored multiplier, and (iii) makes the nonlinearity a tunable, inspectable parameter rather than an assumed step function. The peak-hour volume-to-capacity ratio x(p) = d_peak / C_eff is the operating measure; a stable free-flow equilibrium exists on a segment iff x < 1.

### Modeling Process

Variables: AADT (from data), L (lanes, from data), p in [0.10, 0.90] (SD share), pf = 0.09 (peak-hour share), c0 = 1800 veh/h/lane, p_th = 0.25, w = 0.15, g_max = 0.45, alpha = 4 (BPR exponent, used only for the travel-time index in free flow). Equations: d_peak = AADT * pf; G(p) = g_max * (3t^2 - 2t^3), t = clip((p - p_th)/w, 0, 1); C_eff(p) = L * c0 * (1 + G(p)); x(p) = d_peak / C_eff(p). TTI(p) = 1 / (1 - x^alpha) for x < 1 (free-flow travel-time index). Equilibrium condition: a segment is in a stable free-flow equilibrium iff x(p) < 1; the corridor is in equilibrium iff its bottleneck segment (largest x) satisfies x(p) < 1. Tipping point p* = the smallest p at which the corridor bottleneck crosses x < 1. Parameter table (empirical inputs): pf = 0.09, interval [0.08, 0.10], source: expert exchange 1. p_th = 0.25, interval [0.20, 0.30], source: expert exchange 2 (the gain needs a meaningful cluster, not a scattered few; 0.25 is the midpoint of a 'few tens of percent' onset). w = 0.15, interval [0.10, 0.20], source: expert exchange 2 (width of the onset region). g_max = 0.45, interval [0.30, 0.60], source: bounded above by the platooning result that fully connected platoons can roughly double throughput on urban roads (DOI 10.1016/j.trc.2017.01.023); at 90% SD not all vehicles are platooned and headways are not minimized, so the per-lane share is set to a conservative fraction of that theoretical maximum. c0 = 1800 veh/h/lane, interval [1800, 2000], source: HCM freeway basic-segment capacity. alpha = 4, the standard BPR exponent. Solution procedure: for each corridor and each p in the grid, compute x(p) on every segment, take the bottleneck (max x), and locate p* on a fine 0-1 grid; sweeps over p_th and g_max are run through code/model.py --sweep to bound the conclusions.

### Outcome Analysis

Results across p = 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.50, 0.70, 0.90 (all-lane mixing). Bottleneck x falls as p rises but never reaches free flow: I-5 6.05 -> 6.05 -> 5.42 -> 4.17 -> 4.17 -> 4.17 -> 4.17; I-90 2.70 -> 2.70 -> 2.42 -> 1.86 -> 1.86 -> 1.86 -> 1.86; I-405 4.43 -> 4.43 -> 3.96 -> 3.05 -> 3.05 -> 3.05 -> 3.05; SR-520 2.73 -> 2.73 -> 2.44 -> 1.88 -> 1.88 -> 1.88 -> 1.88. So from 10% to 50% SD the corridors improve modestly (I-90 and SR-520 move from x~2.7 toward x~1.9) but stay in LOS F, and from 50% to 90% there is essentially no further gain because G(p) has already saturated at g_max by p = p_th + w = 0.40. (a) Effect by percentage: 10% and 20% are within the noise floor (the gain is below the expert's ~10% 'day-to-day variation' bar); 30-50% give a real but sub-threshold improvement; 70-90% give the maximum available gain, which for the worst corridors is still not enough to leave LOS F. (b) Equilibria: a free-flow equilibrium does NOT exist at any p in [0.10, 0.90] for the all-lane-mixed regime on any of the four corridors; the corridors remain over-capacity (x >= 1) at every tested p. (c) Tipping point: there is NO tipping point to free flow under all-lane mixing. This is robust: I swept g_max up to its theoretical maximum of 1.0 (full throughput doubling) and p_th down to 0.15, and the bottleneck still never crossed x < 1. Interpretation and bias: the model's central, defensible claim is that on corridors this far over capacity, mixing in self-driving cars raises capacity but cannot by itself restore free-flow operation; the bottleneck is a lane-supply problem, not a headway problem that platooning alone solves. The main source of bias is the x overstatement from the AADT-times-pf conversion (see subtask 1), which, if anything, makes the 'no free-flow equilibrium' conclusion conservative in the sense that a lower true x would make a tipping point more likely, but the magnitude of the overcapacity (x up to 6) is large enough that no reasonable recalibration closes the gap.

## Subtask 3: Decide, under what conditions (if any) lanes should be dedicated to self-driving cars, and identify the tipping point an

### Problem

Decide, under what conditions (if any) lanes should be dedicated to self-driving cars, and identify the tipping point and the policy conditions for a dedicated-lane investment. Scope: the dedicated-lane scenario (a fraction f of lanes set aside for an SD-only express lane), the tradeoff between the lost general-purpose capacity and the express-lane gain, the decision threshold for 'worth building,' and additional policy recommendations. This builds directly on the equilibria/tipping-point result that all-lane mixing alone is insufficient.

### Analysis

Because subtask 2 showed that mixing cannot free-flow these corridors, I test the only lever that physically removes demand from the congested human pool: a dedicated SD-only lane. I set aside a fraction f of the segment's lanes as an express lane. The SD demand p * d_peak runs on the f lanes at full platooning gain (p = 1, so G = g_max), while the human demand (1 - p) * d_peak runs on the remaining (1 - f) lanes with no gain. The corridor's bottleneck is the human-lane pool (the express lane is fast by design), so I measure its x. The decision rule is taken from the expert's decision-relevant threshold: a dedicated lane is worth building if it reduces peak-hour congestion by a substantial amount, roughly 20-30% or more (I use D_thr = 0.25), and is not credited below about 10%, which the expert said is within normal day-to-day variation. This is sound because it grounds the 'worth it' verdict in the decision-maker's own stated bar for a real win rather than in an arbitrary number, and it measures the effect on the peak window (where the delay concentrates), not the daily average. The tradeoff is explicit: dedicating a lane removes general-purpose capacity immediately but only pays off once the SD fleet is large enough to fill and platooning the express lane, so the policy question reduces to 'at what SD share does the express lane start to help rather than hurt.'

### Modeling Process

For a dedicated scenario with lane fraction f: SD demand d_sd = p * d_peak on C_sd = L * f * c0 * (1 + g_max) effective capacity, giving x_sd = d_sd / C_sd; human demand d_h = (1 - p) * d_peak on C_h = L * (1 - f) * c0, giving x_h = d_h / C_h. Corridor bottleneck x_ded = x_h (the human pool). Congestion reduction relative to the all-lane-mixed baseline at the same p: R = (x_mixed - x_ded) / x_mixed. Decision: 'worth' if R >= D_thr = 0.25; 'marginal' if R >= D_no = 0.10; else 'no-credit'. Tipping point (dedicated) = smallest p with x_ded < 1. Parameter table (empirical inputs, in addition to those in subtask 2): D_thr = 0.25, interval [0.20, 0.30], source: expert exchange 3 (a real win is a substantial cut, roughly 20-30% or more in peak-hour delay). D_no = 0.10, interval [0.10, 0.15], source: expert exchange 3 (below about 10% is within normal day-to-day variation). f = 0.25 is the base-case dedicated fraction (swept over 0.25, 0.33, 0.50). Solution procedure: run code/model.py at each (p, f) and read the bottleneck x, the LOS, the reduction R, and the decision; sweep f to characterize how the tipping point and the net-benefit crossover move with the number of dedicated lanes.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (f = 0.25 base case). The dedicated lane is NET-NEGATIVE at low SD share: at p = 0.10-0.40 it raises the human-pool bottleneck (e.g. I-5 x 6.05 -> 7.26 at p = 0.10) because it strips a lane from human traffic before the SD fleet is large enough to use the express lane. It only starts to help at p >= 0.50 (net-gain crossover), and it clears the expert's 'worth building' bar (R >= 25%) only at p >= 0.70, where it cuts the bottleneck by ~42% and, at p = 0.90, by ~81%. Dedicated-lane tipping points (smallest p where the human pool reaches free flow, x < 1): I-90 and SR-520 at p = 0.74, I-405 at p = 0.84, I-5 at p = 0.88. So a free-flow equilibrium DOES exist under a dedicated lane, but only at high SD share. Sensitivity: dedicating more lanes (f = 0.33, 0.50) pushes the tipping point higher (I-90/SR-520 to 0.76-0.82, I-5 to 0.90-0.92) and delays the net-benefit crossover, because each extra dedicated lane removes more general-purpose capacity. Hence a modest f = 0.25 (one lane on a four-lane road) is the efficient choice, not a large reserved block. Conditions for a dedicated lane (answers to the Governor): (1) Do NOT build dedicated lanes now (at the current fleet, p < 10-20%); the effect is below the noise floor and, at 10-40%, the dedicated lane actually worsens human-traffic congestion. (2) Build a dedicated lane only once the cooperating SD share reaches roughly 50% or more on a corridor, and prefer the corridors that are least over capacity and have the most room (I-90 and SR-520 tip first, at p~0.74). (3) Cap the dedicated fraction near one lane (f~0.25); reserving more lanes does not help and delays the payback. (4) On I-5, where the bottleneck is a hard two-lane choke at the I-90 merge (x~6), even a dedicated lane at 90% SD leaves the corridor in LOS D, so the binding constraint there is lane supply: adding a lane or a managed/express-lane program is more effective than a small dedicated carve-out. Other policy changes the model suggests: (i) treat the two-lane I-90-merge segment (mp 163.48-164.22) as the single highest-leverage physical bottleneck and prioritize adding capacity there; (ii) manage peak-hour demand (congestion pricing or transit/AV pooling) rather than relying on fleet share alone, because the all-lane-mixed gain saturates by 40-50% SD; (iii) prioritize AV-coordination standards (platooning, connected headways) on corridors with SD share near the ~25% onset, since that is where the capacity payoff begins. Limitations and bias: the decision hinges on the two expert thresholds (25% and 10%), so if the Governor's true bar were higher (say 40%), the 'worth' share would move toward p~0.8-0.9; and the AADT-based x overstatement means the true tipping points are likely a little lower than computed, i.e. the model is conservative on 'when to build.' The model compares peak-hour x and does not capture safety, emissions, or equity, which a full policy case would add.
