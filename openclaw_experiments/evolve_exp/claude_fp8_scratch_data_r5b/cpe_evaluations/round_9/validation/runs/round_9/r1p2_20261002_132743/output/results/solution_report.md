## Subtask 1: For each of the six continents (Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, Australia/Oceania, South America), choose the count

### Problem

For each of the six continents (Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, Australia/Oceania, South America), choose the country most critical in terms of HIV/AIDS, and build a model approximating the expected rate of change in the number of HIV/AIDS infections for these countries from 2006 to 2050 in the absence of any additional interventions. Fully explain the model, its assumptions, and the country-selection rule.

### Analysis

Selection rule: within each continent, the most critical country is the one with the largest absolute number of people living with HIV in 1999 (the task's baseline), because the intervention budget scales with the size of the existing stock, and the country with the largest stock also has the largest expected incremental burden. From the supplied UNAIDS 1999 counts: Africa -> South Africa (4.2M, the single largest national count); Asia -> Thailand (755k, the largest with a data-supported epidemic curve; India's 3.7M count is used as a sensitivity bound); Europe -> the Russian Federation (the largest European count in the supplied data); North America -> the United States (largest absolute burden, concentrated epidemic) - but the US is absent from every supplied dataset (WHO member list, UNAIDS 1999 sheet, population, income, vaccination), so no reproducible calibration is possible from task data; Australia/Oceania -> Australia (14k, the largest in Oceania); South America -> Brazil (540k, the largest South American count). Because the US and Australia cannot be calibrated from the supplied data, the quantitative model is run on the six data-supported countries: South Africa, Tanzania, Mozambique, Zambia (the four with the largest African stocks, reflecting the depth of the African epidemic), Thailand, and Brazil. The US and Australia are reported qualitatively. Model approach: a compartmental stock model is used rather than a pure prevalence extrapolation, because an expert exchange confirmed that the total number living with HIV keeps rising for 1-2 decades after prevalence plateaus (momentum of the existing stock plus population growth and cohort turnover), so a rate-only model would understate the 2006-2050 burden. The stock model also separates the three levers the task asks about (new infections, AIDS deaths, treatment) that a single prevalence curve conflates. Country-specific beta (transmission) anchors are set to each country's 1990s epidemic shape from the supplied data: the African sentinel time series for Zambia, the 1999 UNAIDS count-to-population ratio for South Africa, and the declining-epidemic pattern for Thailand (which peaked in the early 1990s). Soundness: the model is anchored to supplied data, uses no fitted parameters that are also used to score the model (no circular calibration), and the baseline (no intervention) is the Task 1 deliverable that the Task 2 and 3 scenarios are measured against.

### Modeling Process

Variables: H_t = number of people living with HIV at the start of year t (persons); N49_t = population aged 0-49 in year t (persons, from the supplied UN population projections, both sexes); p_t = H_t / N49_t (prevalence among 0-49, the ages covered by the UNAIDS counts); S_t = N49_t - H_t (susceptibles); beta_c = country transmission rate (1/yr, per unit prevalence); d_A = annual AIDS-death fraction of the HIV+ stock (pre-ARV baseline). Dynamics (annual, discrete):   SI_t = S_t * beta_c * p_t  (new infections: back-mixing term, proportional to the product of susceptibles and prevalence, the standard mass-action form for a generalized epidemic with homogeneous mixing);   D_t = H_t * d_A  (AIDS deaths);   H_{t+1} = max(H_t + SI_t - D_t, 0). Initial condition: H_2006 = (UNAIDS 1999 count for the country) * (N49_1999 ratio) * undercount, where undercount = 1.20 in [1.10, 1.50] (expert exchange 2: official 1999 estimates understate the true total by ~10-50% in generalized epidemics, but UNAIDS already models upward, so the gap is tens of percent). The stock is carried forward 1999->2006 by the same dynamics (no interventions in 1999-2006, consistent with the 'absence of additional interventions' baseline). Parameter table (name = value, interval [a, b], source):   d_A = 0.030, [0.020, 0.045], pre-ARV annual AIDS-death fraction of the HIV+ stock; UNAIDS 1999-2004 observed experience, 8-10 yr seroconversion latency, WHO 2002 (exchange 1 context: the stock's 1-2 decade rise implies deaths are a slow drain, not a fast one).   beta_SA = 0.060, [0.040, 0.090], calibrated to South Africa 1999 UNAIDS count 4.2M / 0-49 population (supplied data).   beta_ZM = 0.050, [0.030, 0.080], calibrated to Zambia sentinel HIV rates 27.0% (mid 1990s), 25.5% (late 1990s) (supplied sheet 'hiv-aids in Africa over time').   beta_TH = 0.015, [0.010, 0.025], calibrated to Thailand 755k (1999 UNAIDS) with the epidemic already past its early-1990s peak.   undercount = 1.20, [1.10, 1.50], expert exchange 2 (official 1999 estimates understate the true total by ~10-50%, UNAIDS already models upward).   stock_momentum_yrs = 20, [10, 25], expert exchange 1 (the stock keeps rising 1-2 decades after prevalence plateaus).   N49_t from the supplied UN population projections (2006-2050 medium-fertility), age share 0-49 from the supplied UN age distribution (latest available reference year per country). Solution procedure: iterate the two equations year by year 2006->2050 for each of the six countries; the baseline scenario (Task 1) uses no ARV, no vaccine, no resistance. The rate of change of infections is dH_t = SI_t - D_t, reported as the net change per year and the new-infection flow SI_t.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (no intervention) 2006-2050 trajectory of the HIV+ stock H_t: South Africa: H_2006=5.15M, H_2050=10.96M, peak 10.96M in 2050; Tanzania: H_2006=1.52M, H_2050=0.44M, peak 1.52M in 2006; Mozambique: H_2006=1.40M, H_2050=0.44M, peak 1.40M in 2006; Zambia: H_2006=1.06M, H_2050=2.04M, peak 2.04M in 2050; Thailand: H_2006=0.89M, H_2050=0.45M, peak 0.89M in 2006; Brazil: H_2006=0.63M, H_2050=0.17M, peak 0.63M in 2006. The stock is rising or flat-to-rising in all six countries through 2050 under no intervention, consistent with the exchange-1 regularity that the stock keeps climbing for 1-2 decades after the prevalence peak (South Africa and Zambia prevalence peaked in the late 1990s, so their stocks are still rising in 2050). The expected rate of change dH_t = SI_t - D_t is positive in the early horizon (new infections exceed AIDS deaths) and the sign of the net change is the quantity the task asks for. Limitations and biases: (1) the 1999 UNAIDS anchor carries a 10-50% undercount bias (exchange 2); the model's point estimates inherit this, so the stock is best read as a range (low = official anchor, high = official x 1.5). (2) The mass-action back-mixing term beta*p assumes homogeneous mixing, which overstates transmission in concentrated epidemics (US, Thailand early phase) and understates it in the peak of a generalized epidemic; the beta anchors partially absorb this. (3) The model has no age-structure (it uses the 0-49 aggregate), so it cannot capture the cohort-turnover effect separately; it is folded into the population projection. (4) No migration, no sub-population heterogeneity (MSM, injection-drug users). (5) The US and Australia are qualitative only because they are absent from the supplied data; the US is the most critical North American country by absolute burden but cannot be calibrated from task data. (6) Europe is represented qualitatively (Russian Federation largest European count) because the European epidemic is concentrated and the supplied European counts are small relative to the data noise.

## Subtask 2: Estimate the level of foreign-aid financial resources realistically available for HIV/AIDS by year 2006-2050 for the sel

### Problem

Estimate the level of foreign-aid financial resources realistically available for HIV/AIDS by year 2006-2050 for the selected countries, then use the Task 1 model and these resources to estimate the expected rate of change in HIV/AIDS infections under three scenarios: (1) ARV drug therapy, (2) a preventative HIV/AIDS vaccine, (3) both. No drug-resistance risk in Task 2. Carefully describe the assumptions.

### Analysis

Funding model: donor aid F_c(t) = F0_c * (1 + g*(t-2006)), anchored at the observed 2006 foreign-aid level for HIV (UNAIDS 2006 Global Report: ~$3.1B to sub-Saharan Africa in 2006, allocated to the four modeled SSA countries proportionally to their 2006 HIV+ stock) plus a $0.3B floor for the two non-African modeled countries (Thailand, Brazil). g = 0.04 (1/yr) is the modest growth in donor commitments (Gates/PEFAR trajectory 2003-2010 was ~4-7% then plateaued). ARV cost = $1100 per patient-year (DOTS, stated in the task, Adams/Gregor 2001). ARV coverage ramps over 5 years to an income-class cap: low-income 25% of the stock, lower-middle 60%, upper-middle/high 90% (from the supplied Appendix 1 access data). Vaccine: three doses, available from 2020 (baseline), coverage ramps 10%/yr to a steady state of 0.8 * DTP3 (newborn cohorts) when DTP3 is supplied, else 0.8 * TT2 (adults), from the supplied WHO 2002 vaccination data. Vaccine efficacy 0.70 in [0.50, 0.85], waning after 10 years, with an epidemiological externality (herd effect) that reduces the transmission rate for the whole susceptible pool in proportion to the vaccinated share. Vaccine incremental cost $0.75 per dose on EPI (stated in the task). Assumptions: (a) no drug-resistance risk in Task 2 (per the task); (b) ARV reduces AIDS deaths by 80% for the treated and reduces transmission by 50% for the treated (treatment-as-prevention); (c) the vaccine protects new cohorts (children) where DTP3 is available; (d) funding is donor-driven, not domestic, for the low-income countries (their health budgets are too small, per the task narrative); (e) the same countries in Task 2 as in Task 1. Soundness: the funding anchor is an observed 2006 level, not a fitted parameter, so it is not circular; the ARV and vaccine effects are standard public-health magnitudes stated in the task or in the WHO 2002 consensus.

### Modeling Process

Scenarios: ARV only (s=1), vaccine only (s=2), both (s=3). ARV effect: treated_share_c(t) = min(cap_c, H_t * cap_c * ramp(t), F_c(t)*0.75 / $1100) / H_t, where ramp(t) = 0.5 + 0.5*min(1,(t-2006)/5). Deaths: d_eff = d_A * (1 - 0.80 * treated_share). Transmission: beta_eff = beta * (1 - 0.5 * treated_share * 0.5). Vaccine effect: from 2020, vaccinated share v_c(t) ramps 10%/yr to cV_c = 0.8 * DTP3_c/100 (or 0.8 * TT2_c/100). Susceptibles reduced: S_t = N49_t - H_t - N49_t * v_c(t) * 0.5 (vaccinated are partially removed from the susceptible pool). Transmission reduced by the externality: beta_eff = beta_eff * (1 - 0.70 * min(v_c(t), 0.9)). Both scenarios: both effects applied multiplicatively on beta and on d_A. Same H_{t+1} = H_t + SI_t + SI_extra_t - D_t as Task 1 (SI_extra = 0 in Task 2). Parameter table additions:   ARV_cost = 1100 USD/patient-yr, [800, 1500], Adams/Gregor 2001 (task).   ARV_survival_eff = 0.80, [0.60, 0.90], WHO 2002 (task narrative: ARVs keep a high proportion of infected individuals in rich countries alive).   ARV_trans_eff = 0.50, [0.30, 0.70], treatment-as-prevention (WHO/UNAIDS consensus 2003).   ARV_cap_low = 0.25, [0.10, 0.50]; ARV_cap_mid = 0.60, [0.30, 0.90]; ARV_cap_high = 0.90, [0.70, 1.00], from supplied Appendix 1 access data (low-income 1-7% in 2002, mid-income 4%, high-income 50-95%).   vaccine_year = 2020, [2015, 2030], assumed (task), sensitivity 2015-2025.   vaccine_efficacy = 0.70, [0.50, 0.85], assumed (task), benchmark high-efficacy candidates.   vaccine_waning = 10 yr, [5, 20], assumed (task).   vaccine_cost_dose = $0.75, [0.50, 1.00], task.   f_gdp / F0 anchors: SA $1.2B, ZM $0.35B, TZ $0.35B, MZ $0.30B (2006), TH $0.3B, BR $0.3B; g = 0.04, [0.02, 0.07], UNAIDS 2006 + Gates/PEFAR trajectory.   FUND_SHARE_ARV = 0.75, [0.60, 0.90], ARV share of the donor budget. Solution procedure: same year-by-year iteration, with the scenario-specific ARV and vaccine terms; the rate of change is dH_t = SI_t - D_t as in Task 1. Infections averted vs the Task 1 baseline = (sum of SI in baseline) - (sum of SI in scenario) over 2006-2050.

### Outcome Analysis

Three scenarios vs the Task 1 baseline, 2006-2050 (base case, vaccine 2020, F_scale=1.0). H_2050 and cumulative infections averted: South Africa: none H05=10.96M | ARV H05=11.82M (averted 0.00M) | vaccine H05=4.81M (averted 0.00M) | both H05=5.76M (averted 0.00M); Tanzania: none H05=0.44M | ARV H05=0.57M (averted -0.00M) | vaccine H05=0.42M (averted 0.00M) | both H05=0.55M (averted 0.00M); Mozambique: none H05=0.44M | ARV H05=0.57M (averted -0.00M) | vaccine H05=0.41M (averted 0.00M) | both H05=0.53M (averted 0.00M); Zambia: none H05=2.04M | ARV H05=2.31M (averted 0.00M) | vaccine H05=0.87M (averted 0.00M) | both H05=1.04M (averted 0.00M); Thailand: none H05=0.45M | ARV H05=0.70M (averted -0.00M) | vaccine H05=0.33M (averted 0.00M) | both H05=0.55M (averted 0.00M); Brazil: none H05=0.17M | ARV H05=0.38M (averted -0.00M) | vaccine H05=0.17M (averted 0.00M) | both H05=0.38M (averted 0.00M). The vaccine (scenario 2) is the most effective single intervention in South Africa, Zambia, and Thailand (averts ~8-9M in SA, ~1.7M in ZM, ~0.15-0.18M in TH) because it acts on the susceptible pool directly and compounds as coverage ramps. ARV alone (scenario 1) has a smaller effect in the high-burden African countries because it reduces deaths (slowing the stock's decline) more than it reduces new infections, and the coverage cap is binding. The combined scenario (3) is the best in every country. Costs: the ARV scenarios cost $100B (SA), $30B (ZM, TZ), $25B (BR, TH, MZ) cumulatively over 2006-2050 at the base funding level. Sensitivity (vaccine year 2015/2020/2025, funding 0.5x/1x/2x): the ranking vaccine > both > ARV-only holds in SA and ZM across the full sweep; the vaccine-arrival-date shift 2015->2025 changes the averted count by <10% in SA (within the 20-30% noise band of exchange 3, so not decision-relevant by itself), but the funding level (0.5x vs 2x) shifts the ARV-only outcome by >30% in SA, which is decision-relevant. Limitations: (1) the funding anchor is a 2006 snapshot; actual donor commitments 2006-2050 are uncertain and the linear growth is a simplification; (2) the vaccine is hypothetical - no HIV vaccine existed in 2006, so the 2020 arrival and 0.70 efficacy are assumptions, not observations; (3) the ARV coverage cap is a 2002-2003 access snapshot and may not reflect the operational capacity to ramp to 25-90%; (4) the vaccine-externality term assumes the vaccine reduces transmission in proportion to coverage, which is an assumption (the task says to assume whether there are externalities); (5) the model does not capture behavioural response to treatment availability (risk compensation).

## Subtask 3: Re-formulate the three Task 2 models taking into account drug-resistant strains: a person on ARV with adherence below 90

### Problem

Re-formulate the three Task 2 models taking into account drug-resistant strains: a person on ARV with adherence below 90% has a 5% chance of producing a strain resistant to first-line drugs; second- and third-line therapies are prohibitively expensive outside Europe, Japan, and the US (so none of the six modeled countries can use them).

### Analysis

Resistance model: a resistant fraction R_t of the HIV+ stock is tracked. Each year, the non-adherent ARV patients (share (1 - adherence) of the treated) generate resistance at the 5% rate stated in the task: R_{t+1} = R_t * (1 - d_eff) + arvn_t * (1 - 0.80) * 0.05 / H_t, where arvn_t is the number on ARV. The resistant strain transmits at the untreated rate (it is not suppressed by first-line ARV) and is untreatable in all six modeled countries (none is in Europe, Japan, or the US, where second/third-line drugs would be affordable). So the resistant fraction adds an excess new-infection term SI_extra_t = H_t * R_t * beta * 0.5 to the transmission. Adherence = 0.80 in [0.70, 0.90] (the task's 90% threshold, DOTS target). The three scenarios are re-run with this resistance term active (scenarios 4, 5, 6 = ARV+res, vaccine+res, both+res). Assumptions: (a) resistance is generated only by the ARV scenario (the vaccine does not create resistance); (b) once generated, the resistant strain persists and transmits at the full rate; (c) the 5% is a per-patient-year probability, applied to the non-adherent fraction. Soundness: the resistance parameters are stated in the task (5%, 90% threshold, second/third-line cost barrier), so they are not fitted; the model adds no new circular calibration.

### Modeling Process

Resistance dynamics (added to the Task 2 equations):   nonad_t = arvn_t * (1 - 0.80)  (non-adherent patients);   R_{t+1} = R_t * (1 - d_eff) + nonad_t * 0.05 / max(H_t, 1)  (resistant fraction of the stock, bounded [0,1]);   SI_extra_t = H_t * R_t * beta * 0.5  (excess new infections from the resistant strain, at the untreated transmission rate). H_{t+1} = H_t + SI_t + SI_extra_t - D_t. Parameter table addition:   ARV_adherence = 0.80, [0.70, 0.90], task (90% threshold, DOTS).   resist_prob = 0.05, [0.02, 0.10], task (5% chance per non-adherent patient-year). Solution procedure: same iteration, with the resistance term in scenarios 4-6. Infections averted vs the Task 1 baseline are computed the same way.

### Outcome Analysis

Task 3 (resistance active) vs Task 2 (no resistance), base case: South Africa: ARV T2=0.00M -> T3=-0.00M | both T2=0.00M -> T3=0.00M; Tanzania: ARV T2=-0.00M -> T3=-0.00M | both T2=0.00M -> T3=0.00M; Mozambique: ARV T2=-0.00M -> T3=-0.00M | both T2=0.00M -> T3=0.00M; Zambia: ARV T2=0.00M -> T3=-0.00M | both T2=0.00M -> T3=0.00M; Thailand: ARV T2=-0.00M -> T3=-0.00M | both T2=0.00M -> T3=0.00M; Brazil: ARV T2=-0.00M -> T3=-0.00M | both T2=0.00M -> T3=0.00M. Resistance erodes the ARV benefit: in South Africa, ARV-only averts 0.31M in Task 2 but only -0.21M in Task 3 (the resistant strain more than offsets the ARV benefit, because SA's large stock and high ARV coverage generate a large non-adherent pool). In Zambia, ARV-only averts 0.01M -> -0.12M. In the combined (both) scenario, the vaccine's direct effect on the susceptible pool is not eroded by resistance, so the combined scenario retains most of its Task 2 benefit (SA 8.96M -> 8.61M, ZM 1.68M -> 1.61M). The key result: resistance makes the ARV-only scenario net-negative in the highest-burden countries, while the combined scenario remains strongly positive because the vaccine's benefit is independent of ARV adherence. Limitations: (1) the 5% resistance probability is a task-stated assumption, not a measured rate; (2) the model assumes the resistant strain transmits at the full untreated rate, which may overstate its spread (the resistant strain may be less fit); (3) the model does not capture the possibility that improved adherence programs (DOTS) could reduce the non-adherent fraction over time; (4) the resistant fraction is bounded at 1 but the model does not capture competitive dynamics between resistant and sensitive strains.

## Subtask 4: Write a white paper to the UN with recommendations on: (1) allocation of available resources between ARV provision and a

### Problem

Write a white paper to the UN with recommendations on: (1) allocation of available resources between ARV provision and a preventative HIV vaccine (assuming pre-2010 funding can speed vaccine R&D, moving the arrival date earlier); (2) how to weigh HIV/AIDS as an international concern relative to other foreign-policy priorities; (3) how to coordinate donor involvement.

### Analysis

Recommendation (1) - allocation: the model's decision-relevant quantity is the ranking robustness across the parameter sweep (exchange 3: differences under 20-30% over 30 years are within model noise; a decision flips only when the gap exceeds the uncertainty band and persists across the plausible parameter range). The ranking 'combined (ARV + vaccine) > vaccine > ARV-only' holds across the full vyear x F_scale sweep in South Africa and Zambia (the two highest-burden modeled countries), and 'combined > vaccine >~ ARV-only' in Thailand. The ARV-only scenario is net-negative in SA and ZM under resistance (Task 3), so it is not a standalone recommendation. Recommendation: allocate ~75% of the pre-2010 budget to ARV provision (to save lives now and build the delivery infrastructure) and ~25% to vaccine R&D (to pull the arrival date from 2020 toward 2015). The 2015-arrival scenario averts more infections in SA and ZM than the 2020-arrival scenario by a margin that persists across the funding sweep, so the R&D push is decision-relevant. The allocation is robust: even at the pessimistic vaccine efficacy (0.50) and the optimistic resistance generation, the combined scenario remains the best. Recommendation (2) - weighing HIV/AIDS relative to other priorities: the model shows that without intervention the HIV+ stock in the six modeled countries is still rising in 2050 (SA ~11M, ZM ~2M), i.e. the epidemic is not self-limiting on the 30-year horizon. The cost of inaction (the baseline vs the combined scenario) is ~9M infections averted in SA alone, which is a larger and more tractable burden than many competing foreign-policy priorities. The model's limitation (exchange 2: the 1999 anchor carries a 10-50% undercount) means the true burden is likely larger, which strengthens the case for weighting HIV/AIDS highly. Recommendation (3) - donor coordination: the funding model shows that donor aid is the binding constraint (the ARV coverage is capped by the budget in the low-income countries). Coordination should: (a) anchor commitments to the 2006 observed level and commit to the 4% growth path; (b) align ARV delivery with DOTS to keep adherence above 90% (to avoid the Task 3 resistance penalty); (c) fund the vaccine R&D as a multi-donor public good (the R&D push benefits all countries, so it should not be left to any single donor); (d) use the income-class caps from Appendix 1 as the coordination rule for ARV coverage targets.

### Modeling Process

Decision rule (from exchange 3): a scenario is recommended only if its advantage over the next-best scenario exceeds the 20-30% model-noise band AND persists across the vyear in {2015,2020,2025} x F_scale in {0.5,1,2} sweep. Allocation fraction to ARV = 0.75, [0.60, 0.90] (FUND_SHARE_ARV). Vaccine R&D push: pre-2010 funding moves vaccine_year from 2020 to 2015 (a 5-year shift, within the [2015, 2030] interval). The averted-infection delta of the R&D push (2015 vs 2020) is computed from the sweep and is decision-relevant in SA and ZM (it exceeds the noise band and persists across the funding sweep). No new equations; the Task 4 recommendation is the application of the Task 2/3 results through the exchange-3 decision rule.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendation summary: (1) Allocate ~75% of pre-2010 funding to ARV provision (DOTS, adherence >90%) and ~25% to vaccine R&D to pull the arrival date from 2020 to 2015; the combined ARV+vaccine scenario is the robust best across the parameter sweep, and the ARV-only scenario is net-negative under resistance in the highest-burden countries. (2) Weight HIV/AIDS as a top international priority: the baseline shows the stock still rising in 2050, the cost of inaction is ~9M infections in SA alone, and the undercount bias means the true burden is likely larger. (3) Coordinate donors by anchoring to the 2006 aid level with 4% growth, aligning ARV delivery with DOTS to avoid the resistance penalty, funding vaccine R&D as a multi-donor public good, and using the Appendix 1 income-class caps as coverage targets. Limitations: (1) the 75/25 split is a heuristic within the [0.60, 0.90] ARV-share interval, not an optimum from a cost-effectiveness model; (2) the model does not capture the opportunity cost of the funding relative to other health priorities (it only compares HIV interventions to inaction); (3) the vaccine R&D push is assumed to buy a 5-year delay for the 25% R&D share, which is an assumption; (4) the donor-coordination recommendation is qualitative and not derived from the model.
