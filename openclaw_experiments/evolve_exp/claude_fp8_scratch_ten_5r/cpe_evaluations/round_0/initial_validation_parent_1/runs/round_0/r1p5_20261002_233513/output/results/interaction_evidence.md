# Expert Interaction Evidence

Ten exchanges, one question each. Question files: `logs/operator_feedback/expert_question_N.md`; replies: `expert_reply_N.json`.

## Exchange 1 — Which renewable matters most for the four states?
**Q:** "Which renewable source matters most for these states: solar, wind, hydro, or geothermal?"
**Reply (summary):** Solar is the most important shared renewable — all four states have very high insolation (AZ, NM, west TX best); CA and TX are the two largest U.S. solar markets. Wind is important in TX (largest U.S. wind state) and CA but weak in AZ/NM. Hydro is historically significant (CA, less in NM/TX) but maxed out and drought-sensitive. Geothermal is real but small and limited to CA (The Geysers), negligible in AZ/TX.
**How it entered the work:** (a) Solar is treated as the anchor growth component for all four states in the projection and in the compact's actions list; (b) hydro is held flat in the no-policy projection (it is maxed out and non-expandable); (c) wind is projected as a TX/CA-dominant component, not a shared one; (d) the 2009 profile discussion is organized around this hierarchy (solar shared > wind TX/CA > hydro CA > geothermal CA-only). Holds over the full 2009-2050 projection window as the basis for component roles.

## Exchange 2 — Which state is driest?
**Q:** "Which of these four states feels the driest: Arizona, New Mexico, or Texas?"
**Reply (summary):** AZ and NM are essentially tied as driest (arid/semi-arid, ~8-15 in/yr); AZ has the largest true-desert share and driest feel; TX is much wetter overall (eastern humid subtropical), only far west TX is truly dry.
**How it entered the work:** (a) Climate is documented in the profiles as a demand driver — AZ and NM carry cooling-dominated, summer-peaking electricity load that reinforces solar's fit; (b) aridity motivates the hydro constraint and makes water-intensive mitigation (large-scale biofuels, hydro) infeasible in AZ/NM, so the compact's actions target solar/wind/storage; (c) TX's wetter climate is noted as a reason its industrial load profile differs. Used in interpretation (I.B) and in the actions (II.B), 2009-2050.

## Exchange 3 — Why rely on coal?
**Q:** "Why do some of these states rely heavily on coal plants?"
**Reply (summary):** Local supply and legacy infrastructure, not preference: TX and NM sit on large cheap coal deposits (lignite, San Juan Basin) with mine-mouth plants; AZ/NM host major coal plants near mines on tribal/rural land with long-term contracts and jobs; TX industry growth drove large baseload demand met cheaply by coal; CA is the outlier — no significant local coal, phased out coal generation via policy and air-quality rules, importing only limited coal power.
**How it entered the work:** (a) Explains the structural 2009 differences: AZ (413 TB Btu coal on total basis), NM (306 TB Btu), TX (1498 TB Btu) vs CA (52 TB Btu); (b) the evolution analysis treats coal as an entrenched baseload stock that decays only by retirement, so the no-policy projection does not assume coal declines faster than demand growth; (c) the compact's coal-transition actions (II.B) are written to address mine-mouth plants and their job linkages, and to treat CA as the de-facto transition reference. Applies to 2009 baseline and the transition path to 2025/2050.

## Exchange 4 — Why can't a desert state build new hydro?
**Q:** "What would stop a desert state from building new hydroelectric dams?"
**Reply (summary):** Water scarcity and lack of dam sites: hydro needs large reliable perennial flow; AZ/NM rivers (Colorado, Rio Grande) are fully allocated (Colorado River Compact) and heavily dammed (Hoover, Glen Canyon), in long-term drought; good sites are taken; remaining flow is legally committed; environmental/legal barriers (species, tribal water rights, compacts); flat terrain lacks head; drought makes output unreliable so new dams are poor investments versus solar. Binding limit is water availability and prior allocation, not construction capability.
**How it entered the work:** (a) Hard constraint in the no-policy projection: hydro component is held flat at its 2009 level for all four states (CA 272,287 B Btu, AZ 62,731, NM 2,645, TX 10,040) for 2025 and 2050; (b) the profile evolution is read accordingly — AZ/NM/TX hydro already declining or flat, CA roughly flat; (c) hydro is excluded from the compact's growth actions; solar/wind/storage carry the new capacity. Applies over the full projection window.

## Exchange 5 — Why did California leave coal first?
**Q:** "Why was California the first of these states to leave coal power?"
**Reply (summary):** Policy and air-quality regulation plus no local coal to defend: CA had no in-state coal reserves (all imported — no mining jobs or mine-mouth economics to protect); strongest environmental/air-quality regime of the four (CARB, strict emissions rules); cheap alternatives available (in-state gas, large hydro, later imports). Phased out coal decades before AZ/NM/TX, which have local coal, mine-mouth plants and entrenched jobs/contracts.
**How it entered the work:** (a) CA's 2009 coal level (52,403 B Btu total basis, already down from 84,160 in 1990) is the empirical basis for the claim that coal can decline well before end-of-life retirements when policy + absence of local coal constituency align; (b) the "best profile" composite uses fossil share as a scored component, which this reply justifies as a meaningful differentiator rather than noise; (c) CA is positioned in the memo as the compact's reference trajectory for coal transition. Applies to the 1960-2009 interpretation and the transition assumptions.

## Exchange 6 — Is wind strong in all four states?
**Q:** "Is wind power strong in all four states, or mainly in Texas?"
**Reply (summary):** Mainly Texas — largest U.S. wind market by installed capacity (west TX, Panhandle). California meaningful but secondary (Altamont, Tehachapi, San Gorgonio; siting-constrained). New Mexico modest (good resource in east, smaller deployment than solar). Arizona weak (limited good sites; solar dominates). Wind is a major renewable only in TX (and secondarily CA), not a shared resource.
**How it entered the work:** (a) In the projection, wind CAGRs are taken from each state's own 2000-2009 record (TX 195,455 B Btu in 2009, CA 56,997, NM 15,096, AZ 288) with no cross-state borrowing; (b) the compact's actions list targets wind expansion primarily for TX and secondarily CA, while solar carries the shared target; (c) the profile discussion states the wind asymmetry explicitly (TX wind ≈ 17.3× AZ wind in 2009). Applies to 2009 baseline and 2025/2050 projections.

## Exchange 7 — Industry vs geography?
**Q:** "Does a state's energy mix mostly reflect its industries, or its geography?"
**Reply (summary):** Both, but geography sets the menu and industry sets the portion: geography is the binding constraint (coal in TX/NM, hydro in CA, sun in all four, wind mainly in TX); industry/economy determines which available option is actually used (TX refining/petrochemicals/manufacturing → large steady baseload favoring coal/gas historically; CA service/tech economy can prioritize cleaner sources); population and climate act through the same channel (cooling in AZ/NM, industry in TX). Geography is the stronger first-order explanation, industry the main modifier.
**How it entered the work:** (a) The evolution model's explanatory framework: the four-state divergence is attributed first to resource endowment (which fixes the feasible mix) and second to economic structure (which fixes the realized mix); (b) demand growth rates are fitted per state (AZ 2.81%/yr, NM 1.63%, TX 1.42%, CA 1.05%/yr) reflecting population/industrial structure rather than one regional rate; (c) the profiles report GSP and population alongside energy to make the industry channel visible. Applies to 1960-2009 interpretation and to the demand-projection method.

## Exchange 8 — Shared target or per-state targets?
**Q:** "Would governors set a shared renewable target or let each state pick its own?"
**Reply (summary):** A shared target with differentiated state contributions: a purely per-state approach defeats the compact's purpose (governors need a common headline goal), but a single identical percentage would be trivial for CA or punitive for AZ/NM; standard practice in real interstate agreements is a common regional target plus state-specific allocations reflecting baseline, resource potential, and cost.
**How it entered the work:** Directly defines the Part II.A goal structure: one regional headline target (15% renewable share of end-use in 2025; 40% in 2050) plus state allocations computed as max(regional target, 1.5 × state no-policy baseline). Because all four states' no-policy baselines (CA 8.7/8.1%, AZ 0/0%, NM 4.2/6.0%, TX 2.2/3.0%) sit below the regional targets, the 1.5× floor does not bind and each state's allocation equals the shared target — the differentiation shows up instead in the physical work each state must do (TX: wind-led; AZ/NM: solar-led catch-up; CA: maintain and deepen), which is stated in the results. The floor ratio 1.5 is a parameter in `model.py` (`--floor_ratio`) and was swept at 1.0/1.25/1.5.

## Exchange 9 — What would hurt the compact most?
**Q:** "What would hurt a four-state energy compact most: price, politics, or grid reliability?"
**Reply (summary):** Politics is the most likely killer: price is a manageable constraint (costs can be shared, phased, offset by federal incentives); grid reliability is a real but solvable technical concern (variable solar/wind, TX's largely isolated ERCOT grid, limited interstate transmission — solvable with storage, transmission, gas backup); politics is binding — four governors and legislatures must agree and keep agreeing across election cycles, with differing party control, fossil-fuel constituencies (TX oil/gas, NM/AZ coal jobs), and compacts needing ratification and sustained funding, making defection or non-renewal the most probable failure mode.
**How it entered the work:** (a) Shapes the goal design: targets are set as a single 2025/2050 pair with a pre-agreed review structure rather than annual renegotiation (removing recurring veto points), which is stated in the Part II discussion; (b) the limitations section identifies political non-renewal as the dominant risk to the goals, with the 15%/40% levels chosen to be ambitious enough to justify the compact yet below levels that would require new interconnection-heavy infrastructure before 2035; (c) grid reliability (especially TX/ERCOT isolation) is carried into the actions list as a distinct workstream rather than assumed solved. Applies to goal design and the 2025/2050 target levels.

## Exchange 10 — What makes the compact durable?
**Q:** "What practical step would make this energy compact most durable across election cycles?"
**Reply (summary):** Make commitments self-executing and economically self-interested: enabling legislation and a ratified interstate compact with fixed multi-year targets (statutory, not reversible executive orders); shared self-funding institutions (regional transmission/storage authority, pooled procurement, common funding pool) whose benefits flow to each state regardless of party; embedded economic constituencies (in-state jobs, manufacturing, utility investment) that make reversal costly; automatic pre-agreed review and ratchet rules instead of annual renegotiation. Durability comes from making compliance cheaper and more profitable than defection.
**How it entered the work:** (a) The Part II.B actions list is organized around these mechanisms: (1) ratify the compact with statutory 2025/2050 targets and an automatic ratchet review every 5 years; (2) create a shared regional transmission and storage authority with pooled procurement (the concrete institutional form); (3) anchor solar/wind build-out in in-state manufacturing and jobs programs (the economic-constituency mechanism); (4) pair the targets with reliability investment (storage, transmission, gas backup) so the goals remain technically credible — the durability logic "compliance cheaper than defection" is stated in the Part II outcome analysis; (b) the memo (Part III) ends on the durability framing. Applies to the 2025/2050 goal structure and actions.
