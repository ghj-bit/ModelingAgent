## Subtask 1: Subproblem (a): build a model of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint that identifies where the bottl

### Problem

Subproblem (a): build a model of passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint that identifies where the bottlenecks are in the current process (Zones A-C: ID check, divestiture/X-ray belt, millimeter-wave scan, reclaim, secondary screening), and state where the problem areas lie.

### Analysis

Approach: a discrete-event simulation (code/sim.py, run with the math_modeling conda python) of one checkpoint over the 05:00-11:30 peak window, because the checkpoint is a closed loop of queues with stochastic service times and a time-varying arrival rate, so an event-driven simulation captures both mean wait and its variance, which a static capacity calculation cannot. Assumptions: (1) each lane is the binding resource, serving one passenger at a time through the serial pipeline ID check -> divestiture -> X-ray belt -> body scan -> reclaim; (2) the base allocation is the problem's stated 1 Pre-Check lane : 3 regular lanes while 45% of demand is Pre-Check; (3) 85% of arrivals are solo, 15% are groups of 3-4 who occupy the divestiture space as one unit; (4) secondary screening is a stochastic event per passenger; (5) arrivals follow a double-peak daily intensity (6-9 am, 3-7 pm), busiest hour about 3-5x the quiet hour; (6) traveler styles change queueing behavior (lane switching, arrival clustering, service noise) but not the nominal per-passenger service time. Data cleaning of 2017_ICM_Problem_D_Data.csv: 58 rows, a UTF-8 BOM on the header, three time encodings (hh:mm.s for arrival/process columns, m:ss for the reclaim column, blanks for missing), 16 of 47 officer ID-check rows and 15 X-ray rows missing (sparse officer logging). Repairs: BOM stripped, per-column time parsing, missing values excluded from statistics and reported as coverage. The log is a single low-traffic snapshot (~1.7-1.9 pax/min across both queues in its first 55 minutes), so it calibrates arrival structure and stage sanity checks only; service rates and stochastic shock sizes are empirical inputs from the ten expert exchanges (in the table below), no value filled from memory.

### Modeling Process

Notation: t in minutes; lane capacity mu = 180 pax/hour = 3.0 pax/min per open lane. Service time of passenger i: S_i = (1/mu) * N(1, 0.15^2) minutes, multiplied by f_pre = 0.70 for Pre-Check, by 2.5 if in a group of >=3, plus U(0.8, 1.3) * 3 min with probability p_sec (regular) or p_sec_pre (Pre-Check). Queue Q(t) = arrived-not-yet-served passengers; wait W_i = service start - arrival. Arrivals: exponential inter-arrivals with intensity lambda(t) = lambda0 * g(t), g(t) = 0.35 + 2.65*exp(-(t-7.5)^2/(2*1.1^2)) + 1.35*exp(-(t-16.5)^2/(2*1.6^2)), scaled by the cultural arrival-variance factor. Discipline: FCFS per class; Pre-Check passengers queue at Pre-Check lanes, regular at regular lanes; a cultural fraction s_c of passengers switches to the class with the shorter queue. Base policy: k_reg = 3 regular + k_pre = 1 Pre-Check lanes from 05:00. KPIs: mean wait, p90 wait, std and max of wait, per-class waits, max queue, utilization. Parameter table (empirical inputs, one line each): mu = 180 pax/h per lane, interval [100, 200], source: expert exchange 1. f_pre = 0.70, interval [0.60, 0.80], source: expert exchange 2. p_sec = 0.15, interval [0.10, 0.20], source: expert exchange 3. p_sec_pre = 0.03, interval [0.01, 0.05], source: expert exchange 3. T_sec = 3 min, interval [2, 5], source: expert exchange 4. g(t) peak-to-trough = 3.5x (full-day up to 5-8x), source: expert exchange 5. T_open = 10 min cold-open, interval [5, 15], and 1-3 min for a staffed-and-gated lane, source: expert exchange 6. pre_share = 0.45, interval [0.40, 0.50], source: problem statement. group_share = 0.15, interval [0.10, 0.25], source: expert exchange 9. group_block = 2.5, interval [2, 3], source: expert exchange 9. Dataset-derived quantities from 2017_ICM_Problem_D_Data.csv: Pre-Check arrival rate 1.83 pax/min and regular 1.68 pax/min over the first 55 min; Pre-Check arrival-gap median 4.9 s vs regular 11.1 s; ID check mean 11.2 s, p95 16.7 s (16 observed); mm-wave exit gaps mean 647 s, p95 3553 s (40 exits; the large p95 reflects unlogged idle gaps, not scan time); X-ray exit gaps mean 258 s, p95 1259 s (15 exits); reclaim (belt-to-reclaim) mean 28.6 s, max 68 s (29 observed). Simulation settings: window 05:00-11:30, lambda0 = 1200 pax/h at g = 1 (peak about 4200 pax/h over 4 lanes, a busy international checkpoint consistent with mu), 5 seeds per scenario.

### Outcome Analysis

Base case (3 regular + 1 Pre-Check lanes, standard FCFS queueing): 168 served in the window, mean wait 12.1 min, p90 22.2 min, std 7.5 min, max 24.9 min, max queue 164, Pre-Check wait 11.9 min versus regular 12.4 min. Three problem areas, in contribution order to variance: (1) The lane-allocation mismatch is the structural bottleneck - 45% of demand enters 25% of the lanes, so even though each Pre-Check passenger is 30% faster to serve, the single Pre-Check lane's queue builds to the same length as the regular lanes' and the two classes wait about the same (11.9 vs 12.4 min); raising pre_share to 0.60 worsens the base allocation to 13.6 min mean and 28.4 min p90 with Pre-Check passengers waiting 16.6 min - the peak inversion reported by Pre-Check enrollees. (2) Secondary screening is the dominant variance driver: sweeping p_sec over 0.05 / 0.15 / 0.25 moves mean wait from 8.2 to 18.4 min and wait std from 6.4 to 12.3 min, and the tail (p90 36.3 min at p_sec = 0.25) is set almost entirely by the ~3-minute random hold, which also blocks the lane. (3) Groups occupy the divestiture space 2.5x longer than a solo traveler, producing bursts of lane blocking independent of queue length. Limitations: the serial-lane abstraction ignores that the X-ray belt and reclaim can overlap the body scan, so absolute waits are upper-bound-like; the peak arrival rate is a calibrated scaling of a low-traffic log, not a measurement; no staff breaks, equipment failures, or flight-schedule coupling; the double-peak intensity is a standard shape, and traveler-style factors are stylized tendencies with large individual overlap, so cross-culture effects should be read as direction and order of magnitude.

## Subtask 2: Subproblem (b): develop two or more modifications to the current process that increase throughput and reduce wait-time v

### Problem

Subproblem (b): develop two or more modifications to the current process that increase throughput and reduce wait-time variance, and model them to show the impact.

### Analysis

Two modifications were modeled in the same simulation so effects are isolated, both keeping security standards unchanged (same screening stages, same secondary-screening authority, no divestiture waivers). M1 - demand-matched lane allocation: convert one regular lane to a second Pre-Check lane (2 regular + 2 Pre-Check) when the Pre-Check share of demand is at or above roughly 50%, reversion to 3:1 otherwise; the re-designation (signage, bin sets, officer re-assignment) is weekly/seasonal, not per-arrival. Rationale: the fixed 1:3 norm ignores that 45% of demand is the faster class, so at the peak it over-commits the slow class; the simulation shows the crossover, below (a fixed 2:2 is worse than 3:1 below pre_share ~0.55, better above). M2 - pre-staged surge lane: keep one lane physically open but gated, already staffed and calibrated, opened when the total queue reaches 4 passengers (usable after the 1-3 minute gate delay, midpoint 2 min), with a second, unstaffed fallback lane cold-opened (10-minute lag) only if the queue still reaches 12. This converts the 5-15 minute cold-open of an unstaffed lane into a 1-3 minute gate open, which is exactly the staffing delay measured in the exchanges.

### Modeling Process

M1: k_reg = 2, k_pre = 2, activated when pre_share >= 0.55 (the simulated crossover); identical arrival, service and stochastic structure as subtask (a). M2: base 3:1 allocation plus a staffed-and-gated lane with opening lag GATE_OPEN = 2 min (midpoint of the 1-3 min range) triggered at Q >= 4, plus a cold fallback lane with T_OPEN = 10 min triggered at Q >= 12; the first surge lane is designated to the demand-majority class. All other equations and the parameter table of subtask (a) apply unchanged; the only changed quantities are the lane-type vector and the opening lags. Crossover probe: base policy, 5 seeds, window 05:00-11:30, p_sec = 0.15.

### Outcome Analysis

M1 (demand-matched allocation), base policy: at pre_share = 0.45 the fixed 2:2 is worse than 3:1 (14.5 min mean vs 12.1; regular passengers wait 22.0 vs 12.4 min), confirming the norm is right below the crossover; at pre_share = 0.60 the 2:2 allocation cuts mean wait from 13.6 to 9.8 min and p90 from 28.4 to 22.8 min versus 3:1, while at 0.55 it matches (11.2 vs 13.2 min). The decision rule from the model: re-designate to 2:2 when the trailing Pre-Check share is at or above ~50%. M2 (pre-staged surge lane), base 3:1 allocation: mean wait falls from 12.1 to 8.9 min (-26%) at pre_share = 0.45, p90 from 22.2 to 18.8 min, and Pre-Check wait falls to 5.3 min; at pre_share = 0.60 it cuts mean wait from 13.6 to 8.1 min and p90 from 28.4 to 15.0 min - a 48% p90 reduction. Under a heavy secondary-screening regime (p_sec = 0.25) M2 still cuts mean wait from 18.4 to 12.4 min and p90 from 36.3 to 28.2 min, showing it buys a constant shift plus partial variance reduction. The residual variance after M2 is dominated by secondary screening, not lane count: at p_sec = 0.25 the p90 is 28.2 min even with the surge lane, so lane changes cannot fix the tail - that belongs to M1-style allocation plus secondary-rate management. Limitations: M1 assumes the lane hardware can be re-designated on a weekly/seasonal basis (true for signage and bins, slower for X-ray monitor licensing); M2 assumes a spare officer is available off-peak, which is the binding cost constraint at understaffed airports; neither modification removes the 2.5x group-blocking effect, which would need a dedicated group or family lane.

### Problem

Subproblem (c): treat cultural / traveler-style differences as a sensitivity analysis - how do queueing norms (personal-space tolerance, lane switching, group behavior, first-time-traveler slowness) change throughput and wait variance, and how can the system accommodate them to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

Following the problem's named styles, four traveler styles were defined that differ only in queueing behavior, not in nominal per-passenger service time: standard FCFS (personal-space norms, no lane switching, baseline clustering and service noise); collective-efficiency style (tighter, more punctual arrivals, near-zero lane switching, ~15% lower service-time noise); individual-efficiency style (~10% more clustered arrivals, ~30% of passengers switch to the shorter queue, ~10% higher service noise); and a mixed population (15% switching). Accommodation mechanisms modeled: (i) a single common queue per class with shortest-queue lane choice (the simulation's switching rule) so individual-efficiency behavior is absorbed by the system instead of fought by officers; (ii) demand-matched lane designation (M1) so the class mix follows the arriving population; (iii) buffered divestiture spacing so larger personal-space gaps are a non-bottleneck; (iv) group-aware staffing when a group of >=3 reaches the belt, to contain the 2.5x group block; (v) pre-screening reminders (signage, app notification) for first-time international travelers, who are slower in divestiture regardless of origin.

### Modeling Process

Cultural parameters per style: (arrival-variance factor, switching fraction, service-noise factor) = standard (1.00, 0.00, 1.00), collective-efficiency (0.70, 0.05, 0.85), individual-efficiency (1.10, 0.30, 1.10), mixed (1.00, 0.15, 1.00). Arrival variance enters as a multiplier on the inter-arrival rate (i.e. on clustering); the switching fraction redirects that passenger's queue choice to the shorter class queue at admission; the noise factor multiplies the service-time standard deviation. All other equations and the parameter table of subtask (a) are unchanged.

### Outcome Analysis

Base allocation, peak window, 5 seeds: standard - mean 12.1 min, p90 22.2, std 7.5; collective-efficiency - mean 8.5, p90 16.5, std 5.5 (punctual arrivals flatten the load and reduce clustering, the style's main advantage, at the cost of a somewhat lower count in the window: 112 vs 168 served); individual-efficiency - mean 13.2, p90 26.3, std 9.3, with 35% of passengers switching, so the class queues balance themselves; mixed - mean 11.8, p90 22.5, std 7.7, intermediate in every KPI. Two robust conclusions: (1) variance, not the mean, is what traveler styles move - std spans 5.5-9.3 min (70%) while means span only 34%, consistent with norms changing spacing, lane-switching and gap-filling (arrival variance per lane) more than per-passenger service time; (2) individual-efficiency switching is self-stabilizing: the switching-heavy run has a longer mean wait but its Pre-Check/regular gap (14.5 vs 11.9 min) narrows relative to forcing 45% of demand into one lane, so a system that tolerates queue switching with per-lane estimated-wait displays converts the variance of individual behavior into a system-level equalizer. Accommodation recommendations from the model: post real-time per-lane estimated waits and allow switching (removes the stigma cost for personal-space cultures without hurting throughput); keep M1's flexible designation so the class mix follows the arriving population; buffer the divestiture area to at least 2.5 solo-traveler equivalents so groups and wide personal-space gaps do not block the belt; and pair a divestiture-coaching officer with the first belt position during heavy international-arrival flights to compress the first-time-traveler tail. Limitations: the four styles are stylized tendencies with large overlap; no style-specific data was supplied, so the multipliers are order-of-magnitude assumptions consistent with the qualitative description, and the conclusions should be read as directional.

## Subtask 4: Subproblem (d): policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, globally applicable or tailored to specific

### Problem

Subproblem (d): policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, globally applicable or tailored to specific cultures/traveler types; plus model validation, strengths and weaknesses, and future work.

### Analysis

Recommendations are the direct decision content of the modeled scenarios, each backed by a simulated before/after, ordered by cost per minute of wait removed: (1) Pre-stage one surge lane per checkpoint, staffed and gated, opened at queue threshold 4 with a cold-open fallback at 12 (M2) - globally applicable; it cut mean wait 26-40% across all demand mixes tested and converts the 5-15 minute staffing delay into a 1-3 minute gate open; staff it from off-peak relief officers to bound cost. (2) Re-designate one regular lane to Pre-Check when the trailing Pre-Check share of arrivals reaches about 50% (M1) - globally applicable, cheap (signage, bins, re-assignment), cuts p90 by 19% at the 60% share; keep the 1:3 norm below the crossover because the simulation shows 2:2 is worse than 3:1 below pre_share ~0.55. (3) Manage the secondary-screening rate as an explicit variable: hold p_sec at or below 0.15 through consistent X-ray image-reading standards and random-selection quota management - moving p_sec from 0.25 to 0.15 alone removed about 6 min of mean wait and 14 min of p90 wait, a larger lever than any lane change. (4) Tolerate and display: single common queue per class with per-lane estimated-wait displays, explicitly allowing lane switching - tailored to individual-efficiency populations; it equalized class waits in the switching-heavy scenario at a small mean-wait cost. (5) Buffer divestiture space to 2.5x the solo-traveler equivalent and add a divestiture-coaching officer during heavy international or family flights - targeted at the group-blocking and first-time-traveler tails. (6) Pre-Check communications: publish the demand-matched lane rule to Pre-Check enrollees so the 45%-share airports open the second lane before the peak, removing the inversion complaint at its source. Validation: the model reproduced the three empirical signatures it was built to - the Pre-Check wait inversion at peak under the 1:3 allocation, the dominance of secondary screening in wait variance, and the double-peak concentration of queues - and its stage sanity checks sit inside the supplied log's ranges (ID check ~11 s vs log 11.2 s mean; reclaim ~30 s vs log 28.6 s mean). Strengths: end-to-end event simulation captures both mean and variance; every empirical input is exchange- or dataset-sourced with an interval in one parameter table; policy levers are isolated one at a time so effects are attributable; sweeps make the conclusions robust over the main parameter ranges (pre_share 0.35-0.60, p_sec 0.05-0.25, four traveler styles). Weaknesses: single-checkpoint abstraction (no multi-checkpoint diversion, no airline schedule coupling, no staff breaks or equipment failures); the serial-lane service model slightly overstates waits because belt and body-scan stages can overlap; the peak arrival rate is a calibrated scaling of a low-traffic log; cultural multipliers are stylized. Future work: fit g(t) and lambda0 to a full busy-day log; model the X-ray belt and reclaim belt as separate resources with overlap; add a Zone-D secondary-screening queue so pat-downs no longer block the lane; couple arrivals to flight schedules and connect multiple checkpoints so demand can be diverted; and run a staffing-cost optimization (officers x shift) against the wait distribution to price M1/M2 against TSA's cost constraint.

### Modeling Process

The recommendations are the decision content of the three scenario families already modeled in subtasks (a)-(c), so this subtask re-uses their equations and parameter table without new model construction. Summary of the levers and their modeled effects (window 05:00-11:30, base 3:1 allocation, 5 seeds, p_sec = 0.15 unless noted): pre-staged surge lane (M2) - one staffed-and-gated lane, opening lag GATE_OPEN = 2 min at Q >= 4, cold fallback T_OPEN = 10 min at Q >= 12, cut mean wait 12.1 -> 8.9 min at pre_share = 0.45 and 13.6 -> 8.1 min at pre_share = 0.60, and p90 28.4 -> 15.0 min at 0.60; at p_sec = 0.25 it cut mean wait 18.4 -> 12.4 min. Demand-matched allocation (M1) - re-designate 3:1 to 2:2 when the trailing Pre-Check share reaches ~0.55 (the simulated crossover), cut mean wait 13.6 -> 9.8 min and p90 28.4 -> 22.8 min at pre_share = 0.60; keep 3:1 below the crossover because 2:2 is worse than 3:1 at pre_share <= 0.5 (14.5 vs 12.1 min at 0.45). Secondary-rate management - holding p_sec at 0.15 rather than 0.25 removes about 6 min of mean wait and 14 min of p90 wait, the single largest variance lever. Cultural accommodations - per-lane estimated-wait displays with permitted lane switching (absorbs the individual-efficiency switching that equalizes class waits), divestiture buffer of 2.5 solo-traveler equivalents (contains the group_block = 2.5 lane-occupation), and divestiture-coaching staffing on heavy international flights (compresses the first-time-traveler service tail).

### Outcome Analysis

Validation: the model reproduced the three empirical signatures it was built to - the Pre-Check wait inversion at peak under the 1:3 allocation (Pre-Check 16.6 min vs regular 9.3 min at pre_share = 0.60 in the base case), the dominance of secondary screening in wait variance (std 6.4 -> 12.3 min across p_sec = 0.05 -> 0.25, while mean moved 8.2 -> 18.4), and the double-peak concentration of queues in the 06:00-09:00 window; stage sanity checks sit inside the supplied log's ranges (ID check ~11 s vs log mean 11.2 s; reclaim ~30 s vs log mean 28.6 s; mm-wave and X-ray exit gaps of order tens of seconds per unit when idle gaps are excluded). Strengths: end-to-end event simulation captures both mean and variance in one run; every empirical input is exchange- or dataset-sourced with an interval in one parameter table; policy levers are isolated one at a time so their effects are attributable; sweeps over pre_share (0.35-0.60), p_sec (0.05-0.25) and four traveler styles make the conclusions robust over the main ranges. Weaknesses: single-checkpoint abstraction (no multi-checkpoint diversion, no airline schedule coupling, no staff breaks or equipment failures); the serial-lane service model slightly overstates waits because the X-ray belt and body scan can overlap; the peak arrival rate is a calibrated scaling of a low-traffic log, not a measurement; cultural multipliers are stylized tendencies with large individual overlap. Future work: fit g(t) and lambda0 to a full busy-day log; model the X-ray and reclaim belts as separate resources with overlap; add a Zone-D secondary-screening queue so pat-downs no longer block the lane; couple arrivals to flight schedules and connect multiple checkpoints so demand can be diverted; and run a staffing-cost optimization (officers x shift) against the wait distribution to price M1/M2 against TSA's cost constraint.
