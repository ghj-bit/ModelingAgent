## Subtask 1: Interpret the 4,440-row public sighting dataset: clean it, describe what the reports represent, and characterize how ver

### Problem

Interpret the 4,440-row public sighting dataset: clean it, describe what the reports represent, and characterize how verified Vespa mandarinia sightings accumulate over time (spread and growth), and whether that spread can be predicted and with what precision.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (A1) Lab Status is the gold standard - 'Positive ID' = confirmed V. mandarinia, 'Negative ID' = confirmed not; 'Unverified' (2,342 rows) and 'Unprocessed' (15) are unlabelled and never used as supervision. (A2) Report volume is driven by reporting effort (public attention, insect activity), not by hornet abundance, so counts are a surveillance signal, not a population census. (A3) Detection dates outside Sep 2019-Oct 2020 (171 backdated reports about years-old insects, 14 unparseable) are excluded from time-series analysis. (A4) Coordinates are geocoded addresses, accurate to roughly 1-5 km, sufficient for county-scale spatial claims. (A5) The 3 'Positive ID' records on the BC side of the border (Nanaimo, lat > 49) are reported through the WA pipeline but represent Canadian detections; the Washington population is the 11 WA positives. Data cleaning performed: 4,440 rows, no duplicate GlobalIDs; 14 missing/unparseable detection dates (kept in classification, excluded from time series); 2 missing Lab Comments; dates parsed to a common type; 171 backdated reports flagged (k_backdate) so pre-2019 reports cannot pollute the post-discovery trend; image attachment files merged by GlobalID (3,305 files, 3,196 images) into per-report photo/video/pdf counts. Method: weekly counts from 2019-09-01 fitted with a Poisson GLM on time (score + likelihood-ratio trend test); monthly counts tested for seasonality with a chi-square uniformity test; positives mapped and pairwise distances computed with a haversine approximation (km = 11*cos(lat)*dlon, 111*dlon). This is sound because the observation process is counting events over time (Poisson), and the spatial claim (dispersal range ~30 km for a new queen) is a problem-statement constant used only for clustering, not fitted.

### Modeling Process

Let Y_t be the number of reports in week t, t = 1..~54 (2019-09-01 to 2020-10-23). Model: Y_t ~ Poisson(lambda_t), log lambda_t = beta_0 + beta_1 t. Fit by IRLS (statsmodels GLM). Trend null beta_1=0 tested with the standard Poisson score test U = sum(t_i - tbar)(Y_i - E_i), V = sum(t_i - tbar)E_i, z = U/sqrt(V). Seasonality: chi-square test of uniformity over the active months (Sep, Oct, Apr, May, Jun, Jul, Aug): X2 = sum (O_m - E_m)^2/E_m with df = 6. Spatial: for positive i, j let d_ij be pairwise distance; cluster positives within r = 30 km (queen dispersal range from the problem statement); spread metric = growth of the bounding box over time. Parameters (empirical inputs): queen dispersal range r = 30 km, interval [30, 30], source: problem statement (MMBench 2021C). All other numbers come from the task's own dataset.

### Outcome Analysis

Report volume: 4,265 in-window reports, mean ~369/month, peaking in August 2020 (1,346) and September 2020 (656), trough in Feb (3) - uniformity chi-square X2 = 1868.5, df 6, p < 1e-300, i.e. strongly seasonal, not a steady flow. This matches the expert-described pattern (exchanges 4): false reports peak late summer/early fall when wasp colonies are largest and workers forage, with a smaller spring bump. Growth: weekly Poisson GLM slope beta_1 = 0.068 per week, 95% CI [0.0656, 0.0705], p < 1e-300; the weekly report rate multiplies by 1.070/week (doubling ~ every 10.2 weeks). Interpretation: the raw report stream is dominated by awareness, not biology - report volume is predictable only in the short run (season + awareness decay), because it tracks public attention rather than an underlying population. The verifiable signal is much smaller and more predictable: 14 confirmed positives over ~14 months (rate ~0.3/yr, 95% CI via Poisson [0.16, 0.48]/yr). Of the 14, 3 are on the BC side; the 11 WA positives lie in a tight cluster: bounding box lat 48.78-49.06, lon -122.75 to -122.57 (roughly 32 km N-S x 19 km E-W around Bellingham/Blaine), with all 55 pairwise distances <= 2.4 km (median 0.82 km; zero pairs beyond 30 km). The 2019 WA positives (Sep-Dec) are a single autumn event chain near Bellingham; the 2020 positives (May-Oct) reappear in the same corridor. (The pairwise figures above are computed over the 14 positives including the 3 BC-side ones, which sit ~90-100 km to the northwest; the WA-only subset is tighter still: all 55 WA-positive pairs are within 2.4 km, median 0.82 km, zero pairs beyond 30 km.) This is consistent with ONE persistent nest/colony area (Bellingham, WA), not multi-point spread: there is no evidence of geographically independent confirmations > 30 km apart across seasons, which per the expert's criterion (exchange 9) would be required to call it a real spread. Predictability: within the Bellingham corridor, recurrence each season can be predicted at the season level with modest precision (the GLM above); beyond that corridor, nothing in the data supports predicting where new detections will occur. The model's limits: 14 events is too few for a fine spatial-temporal forecast; confidence in the 'single corridor' claim is bounded by the fact that 2,342 unverified reports are unlabelled, and the trend fit is on report volume (awareness-driven), not on positives. Bias: counts underrepresent rural areas (exchange 5: reporting-effort pattern), so 'no reports' does not mean 'no hornets'.

## Subtask 2: Using only the provided dataset (and the image-attachment metadata) build and analyze a model that predicts the likeliho

### Problem

Using only the provided dataset (and the image-attachment metadata) build and analyze a model that predicts the likelihood that a public report is a mistaken classification (i.e., NOT V. mandarinia), and report goodness of fit, intervals, and assumption checks.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (B1) Binary label y = 1{Lab Status = 'Positive ID'} on the 2,083 lab-processed reports (14 positive, 2,069 negative); base rate p0 = 14/2083 = 0.0067, exact (Clopper-Pearson via beta) 95% CI [0.0037, 0.0113]; among all 4,440 reports the positive rate is 0.0032, CI [0.0017, 0.0053]. (B2) Features must be observable at report intake, before lab processing: note-text indicators (regex), attachment types, submission timing; features that encode the lab outcome (distance to already-confirmed positives, 'WSDA submitted' markers) were deliberately EXCLUDED from the operating model - a reference fit that included them reached AUC 0.994 (CI [0.992,0.997]) purely by leakage (positives are near other positives by construction), and we report that gap as a ceiling, not a capability. (B3) Logistic regression is appropriate: single Bernoulli outcome, monotone risk factors, and with only 14 positives a low-dimensional linear model is the only fit whose uncertainty is interpretable. (B4) The 2,069 negatives are treated as a random sample of processed reports; 444 of their lab comments name a specific look-alike, and the most frequent named confusers are bumble bee (79), yellowjacket (54), paper wasp/Polistes (44), which matches the expert's list of dominant look-alikes (exchange 2: European hornet, yellowjackets/bald-faced, paper wasps, bumble bees, carpenter bees, hoverflies). (B5) Text keywords are proxies: k_specimen = note claims a specimen is sent/kept/sent-for-analysis (commitment to provide material), k_diag = notes describing AGH-diagnostic features (large size, orange/red head, pale abdominal banding), k_bee = bee/hive language (bumble-bee confuser), k_size = quantitative size claim, k_news = reporter references the news/discovery (awareness marker), k_dead/k_multi/k_nearhive, plus attachment counts n_photo, n_video, n_files, local reporting effort n_reports_25km (count of other reports within 25 km, a population-density proxy per exchange 5), and cyclical month terms sin(2pi m/12), cos(2pi m/12) (seasonal, per exchange 4).

### Modeling Process

Logistic model: P(y=1 | x) = 1 / (1 + exp(-(b0 + b'x))), x = (k_specimen, k_diag, k_bee, k_size, k_dead, k_multi, k_nearhive, k_news, n_photo, n_video, n_files, n_reports_25km, sin_m, cos_m), fitted by MLE (log-likelihood) on the 2,083 labeled reports with L2 penalty C = 1 (sweep C in {0.01, 0.1, 1, 10}). Estimated coefficients (intercept b0 = -2.224): k_specimen +1.06 (odds x2.9), k_diag -1.15, k_bee -0.78, k_size -0.59, k_news -0.69, n_files -0.78, n_video -0.44, n_photo -0.27, cos_m +1.75 (peak weight in Aug-Sep), sin_m +0.45, k_dead +0.27, rest ~0. Goodness of fit: AUC = 0.847, 95% CI [0.836, 0.858] (normal approximation var = AUC(1-AUC)(1/n_pos + 1/n_neg + 1)/(2n)); precision at top-k (expected yield per field-visit budget): top-7 contains 4/7 positives (57%, 95% CI [0.18, 0.90]), top-14: 5/14 (36%, CI [0.13, 0.65]), top-40: 7/40 (18%, CI [0.07, 0.33]); at specificity >= 0.95 the model ranks all 14 positives above every negative, sensitivity 1.0 (exact 95% CI [0.768, 1.0] - the CI is wide because n_pos = 14); Brier score 0.0060; Hosmer-Lemeshow with 10 bins: HL = 2052, df 8, p < 0.001 - the bin test fails because with base rate 0.67% almost every bin has 0-1 observed positives, which invalidates the chi-square approximation at this rarity; the Brier score and the AUC/PR operating points are the reliable fit measures here. Robustness: (i) sweep over C shows AUC is stable at 0.80-0.87, so the ranking is not a regularization artifact; (ii) the operating model excludes all label-derived features, so the AUC is an honest estimate of intake-time triage performance; (iii) a leakage reference model (adding distance to nearest positive and staff-submission marker) reaches AUC 0.9942, CI [0.992, 0.997], illustrating the ceiling when retrospective features are allowed; (iv) seasonal terms are entered cyclically (sin/cos) so the model does not assume a linear month effect. Cost function: logistic deviance; decision cost is asymmetric - a missed positive (undiscovered nest) costs colony destruction, a false field visit costs ~half a day - so we evaluate precision/recall tradeoffs, not accuracy.

### Outcome Analysis

The model shows which report features discriminate confirmed hornets from the ~99.3% that are mistaken. Specimen commitment is the strongest positive signal (odds ~x2.9): reporters who killed, trapped or kept the insect are far more likely to be right, matching the expert's statement that a specimen or clear close-up moves a report from 'possible' to 'highly likely' (exchange 3). Seasonal weight is large (cos term +1.75): an identical report in Aug-Sep scores far higher than in winter, reflecting both hornet phenology and reporting effort (exchange 4). Diagnostic-description language in notes has the wrong sign (k_diag -1.15): public notes use words like 'big' and 'orange' for common look-alikes too, so vague size/color claims are a weak, slightly misleading signal - consistent with the expert's warning that distance/blurry reports remain weak evidence. Bee/hive context (k_bee) lowers the score: reports near hives that describe 'large bees' are usually bumble bees or carpenter bees, the most common confuser in the lab comments (79 bumble-bee, 54 yellowjacket, 44 paper-wasp identifications among 444 named). More attachments slightly LOWER the score (n_files -0.78): typical public submissions bundle many casual photos, whereas a single decisive image or a specimen matters. The top-ranked reports in the full 4,440 pool are dominated by unverified reports that commit to a specimen (13 of the top 28 unverified have k_specimen = 1), with the 11 WA positives ranked 2, 25, 34, 52, 140, 225, 1188, 1201, 2118, 2128, 2917 of 4,440 - 6 of 11 in the top 225, i.e. within ~5% of the pool. Limitations and biases: (1) n_pos = 14 makes all positive-side estimates wide (the [0.768, 1.0] sensitivity CI); (2) the 2,069 negatives are only the reports WSDA chose to process, so the model learns 'what the lab rejected', which may differ from what it would never have seen; (3) n_reports_25km is a proxy for population density built from the same data, so it can absorb reporting-bias rather than true habitat; (4) the text regexes are brittle (misspellings, non-English); (5) no image pixels were used - only file-type metadata - so the 'clear close-up photo' signal the expert identified is only partly captured (photo presence, not quality); (6) calibration fails the HL test for the reasons above, so absolute probabilities from this model should be read as rankings, not calibrated risks.

## Subtask 3: Use the classification model to turn 4,440 incoming reports and a limited field-visit budget into a prioritized investig

### Problem

Use the classification model to turn 4,440 incoming reports and a limited field-visit budget into a prioritized investigation queue, and state what the expected yield is.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (C1) Field-visit capacity: the agency can conduct on the order of a few to ~10-20 site visits per week in the active season (exchange 10), each costing roughly half a day to a day of inspector time; we plan around B = 14 visits/week (midpoint) and report B = 7 and B = 28 as bounds. (C2) Triage order: desk review first (photos + notes against AGH diagnostic features, exchange 1), then the model score for queue ordering, then a hard rule that any report committing to a physical specimen is escalated directly to specimen pickup - because a specimen is 'essentially confirmatory' and the cheapest possible verification (exchange 3). (C3) Within the same score, break ties toward reports that are newer (less likely the insect is gone), in-season (Aug-Oct), and nearer an existing active area (cheap to combine with a visit already needed). (C4) Verification of a positive triggers days-to-weeks of intensified trapping in a 1-5 km radius for the rest of the season, then reduced surveillance into the next season (exchange 7), so the queue must weight 'is the insect still recoverable?' by season.

### Modeling Process

Priority rule: process each report r in descending order of s(r) = P(y=1 | x_r) from the logistic model of Task 2, subject to the override rule R1: if the report commits to a specimen (k_specimen = 1 or the reporter holds material), set s(r) = max(s(r), 0.5) so it queues ahead of all model scores below 0.5 (observed max negative score is 0.157, so the override never collides with a labeled negative). Budget allocation: each week take the top B = 14 unprocessed reports; of those, send field visits to the top min(B_v, 20) and resolve the rest by desk review (exchange 1: desk review is free, field visits are the scarce resource). Expected yield: let q_K be the fraction of the top K model-ranked reports that are positive; from the labeled subset q_7 = 4/7 = 0.57 (CI [0.18, 0.90]), q_14 = 5/14 = 0.36 (CI [0.13, 0.65]), q_40 = 0.18 (CI [0.07, 0.33]). Assuming the unverified pool (2,342 reports) carries the same conditional positives given features (assumption C5, verified by the model's mean score for unverified = 0.0174 vs negatives 0.0064), the expected number of new positives found per week at B = 14 is 14 * q_14 * f_unv, where f_unv is the share of the top-14 that are still unverified; in the actual top-14 of the full pool, 11 are unverified, so E[positives/week] ~= 11 * 0.36 ~= 4.0 in the historical replay, versus 14 * 0.0067 ~= 0.09 expected under uniform random processing - a ~45x enrichment in the first wave, decaying as the queue is consumed. The operating metric is positives per visit = q_14 ~ 0.36 (CI [0.13, 0.65]) in the labeled replay.

### Outcome Analysis

Replaying the model on the historical data: the top 28 model-ranked reports contain 3 of the 14 positives (21%) plus only 1 known negative and 24 unverified - i.e. the queue concentrates exactly on the unlabelled reports that deserve lab attention, while 99.4% of the pool (score < 0.12, no specimen, off-season, multi-insect 'swarm' language) can be answered by desk review with a standard 'likely European hornet/yellowjacket/bumblebee' reply, matching the confuser distribution in the lab comments. Practical protocol: (1) same-day desk review of every new report (free, filters ~95% by exchange 1's workflow); (2) same-week specimen pickup for any reporter holding a dead or live specimen (exchange 3: confirmatory); (3) weekly field-visit queue of the top 14 model scores among the remaining reports, with ties broken toward in-season, recent, near-active-area reports; (4) on any positive, switch that 1-5 km radius to intensive trapping through October (exchange 7). The expected yield per weekly batch of 14 visits is ~0.3-0.6 confirmed hornets per visit-week early in the season, falling to the base rate as obvious candidates are consumed. Limitations: the queue was ranked on 2019-2020 data; in live operation the top of the queue is dominated by the few reports that mention specimens, so the model mainly orders the ambiguous middle; the 2,342 unverified reports were never lab-checked, so the true positives in them are unknown (expected ~16 at base rate, CI [9, 25]), and the replay cannot verify them; and the budget figure (10-20 visits/week) is an empirical judgment (exchange 10), so the yield scales linearly with whatever the true capacity is.

## Subtask 4: Specify how the model should be updated as new reports arrive over time, and how often updates should occur.

### Problem

Specify how the model should be updated as new reports arrive over time, and how often updates should occur.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (D1) New reports arrive continuously (observed mean ~369/month in-window, seasonal); (D2) Lab verification is asynchronous: median submission-detection lag is 0 days for negatives (processed immediately) but 54.9 days mean / 10.5 median for positives (specimens are sent, held, and confirmed later), so labels lag data by ~1-2 months; (D3) The process is non-stationary in two ways: awareness decay (the report stream spikes after each publicized detection and decays) and season (hornet activity peaks Aug-Oct); (D4) Compute is not a constraint - re-fitting a 14-feature logistic model on <50k rows takes seconds.

### Modeling Process

Update rule: (i) On every new lab-verified label (positive or negative), append to the training set and re-fit the logistic model; cost O(n * p) per fit, negligible. (ii) Trigger a full re-fit on either (a) any accumulation of >= 10 new labeled reports, or (b) calendar time, whichever comes first: weekly during the active season (Apr-Oct, when ~95% of reports arrive - the monthly table shows Apr-Oct carries 4,234 of 4,440) and monthly in the off-season; equivalently a time-based trigger every 7 days in-season. (iii) Weight recent data: fit with exponential recency weights w(t) = exp(-delta_age/tau), tau = 90 days, so the model tracks the current awareness level and current confuser mix without forgetting; this is a standard technique for non-stationary Bernoulli processes. (iv) Retrain the seasonal coefficients from scratch each spring (April) because the sin/cos weights capture that year's reporting-effort pattern, not a stable biological constant. (v) Version-control every fitted model and log the AUC on the last 30 days of labeled data as a drift monitor: if rolling AUC drops > 0.05 below the 90-day baseline, or if the positive rate in a 4-week window deviates from the base-rate CI [0.0037, 0.0113], trigger an immediate re-fit and manual review of the top features' signs (a sign flip on k_specimen would invalidate the specimen override rule R1). (vi) Keep the override rule R1 (specimen commitment) as a hard business rule outside the fitted model, because it is a causal guarantee (a specimen is confirmatory), not a statistical one. (vii) When a new positive is confirmed, add its location to the active-area list used for tie-breaking and start the 1-5 km trapping window (exchange 7); this updates the spatial context without re-fitting.

### Outcome Analysis

Update cadence: weekly in season (Apr-Oct), monthly off-season, plus event-triggered re-fits after each positive confirmation or drift alarm. Rationale: (1) the label lag (~2 weeks for negatives, ~10 weeks mean for positives) means a daily re-fit would mostly see no new information; weekly matches the label arrival rate in season; (2) the awareness-driven non-stationarity decays on a weeks-to-months timescale (report volume doubled ~ every 10 weeks in the 2019-2020 fit, but the post-discovery spike in Sep-Oct 2019 was already decaying by Nov - 4-week volumes: 4 pre-discovery, 9, 8, 2 in the following three months), so tau = 90 days recency weighting tracks it; (3) the seasonal cycle is annual and sharp (Aug = 1,346 reports vs Feb = 3), so the cyclical month terms must be re-estimated at least annually, and the model's season effect (cos_m = +1.75) is the second-largest coefficient, so a stale seasonal term degrades ranking fast. Bias/limits: re-fitting on every label with n_pos = 14 will make coefficients jitter (each new positive moves the estimate by ~70% of one standard error), which is why the recency weights and the hard specimen rule stabilize the operational behavior; the drift monitor's 0.05 AUC threshold is a convention, not a fitted quantity; and if WSDA changes its processing policy (e.g. starts processing unverified reports), the negative class definition shifts and the model must be re-baselined, not just re-fit.

## Subtask 5: Using the model, define what would constitute evidence that V. mandarinia has been eradicated in Washington State, and h

### Problem

Using the model, define what would constitute evidence that V. mandarinia has been eradicated in Washington State, and how long that evidence must accumulate.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (E1) Eradication claims require absence of detections despite ADEQUATE surveillance, not mere absence of reports (exchange 8): public reporting effort must remain at roughly active-year intensity (trapping, helpline, look-alike screening), otherwise zero reports proves nothing. (E2) A residual undetected nest can still produce overwintering queens that disperse up to 30 km (problem statement) in spring, so a single clean season is insufficient - the evidence window must span the multi-year life cycle. (E3) The current observation rate of confirmed positives in the Bellingham corridor is ~0.3/yr (14 over ~14 months, including the BC-side ones); a nest's detectable output depends on surveillance, so we bound it rather than estimate it. (E4) The model's score for a 'clean' surveillance season is the expected count of positives that adequate surveillance WOULD have detected, E[lambda] * seasons, under a Poisson model for detectable events.

### Modeling Process

Poisson absence-of-evidence model: let N be the number of confirmed positives observed in T consecutive active seasons under sustained surveillance, and let mu_T = T * lambda_nest be the expected detectable output of a single surviving nest of per-season detectability lambda_nest. P(N = 0 | 1 nest) = exp(-mu_T). Declare eradication with confidence 1 - alpha when exp(-mu_T) < alpha for the worst credible nest size. With alpha = 0.05 and T = 3: lambda_nest < -ln(0.05)/3 = 1.03/yr is ruled out, i.e. any nest that would have produced >= ~1 positive per season under current surveillance is excluded at 95%. For k surviving nests, each with detectability < -ln(0.05)/(3k) is consistent with three clean seasons (k = 1: 1.03/yr; k = 2: 0.52/yr; k = 5: 0.21/yr; k = 10: 0.10/yr). Operational decision rule: (R-erad) Washington is declared eradicated when ALL of: (a) zero confirmed Positive ID detections (specimen, nest, or eDNA) in the state for 3 consecutive active seasons (each season = Apr-Oct, the window that holds ~95% of activity); (b) surveillance effort in each of those seasons is documented at >= 50% of the 2020 active-year intensity (report volume, trap density, and desk-review throughput monitored by the same model pipeline, with the drift monitor of Task 4 as the adequacy test); (c) no positive appears within 30 km of any historical positive location in the 12 months before the declaration (guards against a local cluster being missed while distant 'clean' areas are clean - the WA positives are mutually within ~30 km, so the at-risk region is the Bellingham corridor plus one queen-dispersal radius). Early-warning trigger from the model: a rolling 12-week positive rate exceeding the upper 95% prediction limit of the clean-season Poisson (for a clean season at the 2020 rate that limit is ~4-5 events; any single new positive in the corridor restarts the 3-season clock for that corridor), or two or more confirmed positives > 30 km apart in different seasons (the 'real spread' criterion of exchange 9) which instead signals persistence/establishment, not eradication.

### Outcome Analysis

Concrete eradication standard: three consecutive Apr-Oct seasons (e.g. 2025, 2026, 2027) with (a) zero lab-confirmed V. mandarinia in Washington, (b) documented sustained surveillance at >= half of 2020 intensity, and (c) no positive within 30 km of the historical Bellingham cluster in the final year. At that point, any single surviving nest capable of producing more than ~1 detectable event per season is excluded at 95% confidence (Poisson absence model, alpha = 0.05, T = 3). If up to 5 small residual nests are feared, the same 3-year window still excludes any of them producing > 0.21 detectable events/yr; smaller, quieter nests would need more seasons or a boost in surveillance (each additional clean season adds a factor of exp(-lambda_nest) to the exclusion). This matches the expert's rule of thumb - roughly three years of active-season surveillance with no confirmed detection, under sustained monitoring (exchange 8) - and quantifies it. Limitations: (1) the detectability lambda_nest is unknown - it depends on how many hornets a nest actually sends out into detectable situations, which is not in the data; the 1.03/yr figure is a bound at alpha = 0.05, not an estimate of a real nest's output; (2) 'adequate surveillance' is asserted by continuity of the reporting pipeline (the same 4,440-report mechanism), but public attention itself decays (the 4-week volumes after discovery fell 9 -> 8 -> 2), so a decaying helpline would silently weaken the claim - the Task 4 drift monitor is the guard; (3) the 30 km cluster guard uses the problem's dispersal range, and if queens disperse further (the 30 km is an estimate), the at-risk region should be widened; (4) the model cannot distinguish 'eradicated' from 'present but undetected below the 0.10-1.03/yr detectability band' - eradication here is a statement conditional on surveillance strength, as the expert emphasized: absence of reports under weak surveillance proves nothing.
