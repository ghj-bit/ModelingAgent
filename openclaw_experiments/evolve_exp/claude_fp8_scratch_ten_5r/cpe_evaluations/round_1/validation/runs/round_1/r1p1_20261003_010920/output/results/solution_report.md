## Subtask 1: Task 1: Discuss the factors that may contribute to the loss of appropriate scrub-lizard habitat in Florida, make preserv

### Problem

Task 1: Discuss the factors that may contribute to the loss of appropriate scrub-lizard habitat in Florida, make preservation recommendations to the state of Florida, and identify the obstacles to implementing them.

### Analysis

This is a qualitative policy task anchored by the model's quantitative findings and by the range of habitat conditions described by the domain expert. The analysis ranks threats by how strongly the model ties each one to population decline: (a) fire suppression and vegetation encroachment — the problem's own aerial-photo data show vegetation density rising about 6% per year, which closes the open sandy matrix the species requires; on the Avon Park range this is compounded by unexploded ordnance and safety restrictions that make prescribed fire hard to schedule; (b) habitat fragmentation — the 29-patch landscape is a set of isolated islets in a non-habitat matrix, and the expert confirmed that gaps of more than roughly 350 m of unsuitable matrix effectively isolate patches, cutting off the juvenile exchange that small patches depend on; (c) direct loss and conversion from range infrastructure (roads, firing points, facilities) and surrounding development; (d) fire suppression's indirect effect: as shrub and canopy cover rises, juvenile and adult survivorship fall, which the Task 3 regressions quantify (Sj and Sa rise with open sandy area, R^2 = 0.66 and 0.81 respectively). The expert confirmed fire suppression/encroachment is the day-to-day dominant threat on active ranges, with fragmentation and infrastructure secondary but persistent.

### Modeling Process

No new equations; the ranking uses the model outputs from Tasks 3, 5 and 6 as evidence. Key linkages: (1) encroachment shrinks open sandy area H_i(t) = H_i(0)(1-0.06)^t, and since C_i = D(H_i)*H_i (Task 3/5), total landscape capacity halves within about 12-20 years without burns (encroachment sweep: 7723 -> 3954 lizards by year 10 at 6%/yr); (2) fragmentation enters through the 350 m dispersal limit: patches separated by more than that distance cannot exchange juveniles, so small patches cannot be rescued; (3) the burning policy in Task 6 quantifies the maintenance cost of counteracting encroachment (a burn roughly every 10-12 years per patch to hold vegetation in the open-sandy band). Parameter table: encroach = 0.06/yr, interval [0.03, 0.09], source: problem statement (aerial photographs) + expert exchange 1 (confirmed as the binding threat on military ranges); dispersal range 0-350 m, source: histogram.csv survey radius + expert exchange 4.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendations to the state of Florida: (1) Institutionalize a prescribed-burn program for all scrub patches, on a roughly 8-12 year rotation per patch, to hold vegetation in the open, low, patchy state that the lizards need (Task 6 shows suppression collapses populations while moderate burning sustains them); (2) Protect and maintain the large, sandy-rich core patches (e.g., patches 2, 12, 15, 17 in Table 3, which carry the highest carrying capacities) and the corridors/matrix between patches closer than about 350 m, since juvenile exchange is the mechanism that keeps small patches viable; (3) Cap the conversion of scrub to infrastructure and require that any new range facilities be sited away from sandy scrub; (4) Prioritize restoration burns where patches are drifting past roughly 50% shrub/canopy cover, because that is where survivorship begins to fall measurably. Obstacles: unexploded ordnance and live-fire restrictions limit when and where burns can be applied on an active training range (expert exchange 1); the range's primary mission is military training, so habitat work must fit around it; federal land-management processes and interagency coordination add cost and delay; public and local opposition to open burning; and the economic temptation to develop sandy uplands, which is precisely the land the lizards need. Model limitation: the threat ranking is mechanistic (model outputs + expert input) rather than based on measured land-use conversion rates, so the relative weight of infrastructure loss is a judgment.

### Problem

Task 2: Using the cohort data in Table 1, estimate Fa (average fecundity of adult lizards), Sj (juvenile survivorship, birth to first reproductive season) and Sa (average adult survivorship).

### Analysis

Table 1 follows a cohort of 972 hatchlings (age 0) for four years: 180 survived to age 1, 20 to age 2, and 2 to age 3. Hatchling females do not lay eggs in their birth summer; for all other females clutch size is y = 0.21*SVL - 7.5 eggs with SVL the snout-to-vent length in mm. Data cleaning: the file needed no repairs (no missing values, duplicates or encoding problems; all 16 fields present and consistent). Two conventions were needed and are stated as assumptions: (i) Fa is defined as the average number of eggs laid per reproducing adult female, weighted over the reproducing females in the cohort; (ii) Sa is the annual adult survivorship, taken as the geometric mean of the two observed adult transition rates (age1->age2 and age2->age3), because the cohort only spans two adult years. The geometric mean is the correct way to average per-capita rates over time (it is exact for constant-rate processes).

### Modeling Process

Clutch sizes from the given function: age 1: 0.21*45.8-7.5 = 2.118 eggs; age 2: 0.21*55.8-7.5 = 4.218; age 3: 0.21*56-7.5 = 4.260. Fa = sum(F_i * clutch_i)/sum(F_i) over reproducing ages, with F_1 = 92, F_2 = 11, F_3 = 2: Fa = (92*2.118 + 11*4.218 + 2*4.260)/(92+11+2) = (194.85+46.40+8.52)/105 = 2.379 eggs per female per year. Sj = (age 1 survivors)/(hatchlings) = 180/972 = 0.1852. Sa_annual = sqrt((age2 survivors/age1 survivors)*(age3 survivors/age2 survivors)) = sqrt((20/180)*(2/20)) = sqrt(0.1111*0.1) = 0.1054. The two-year adult survival from the same data is (20+2)/180 = 0.1222. Note: age-0 clutch value 0.21*30.3-7.5 = -1.137 is negative, which is why the problem statement excludes hatchling females from the clutch function; it is never used.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: Fa = 2.38 eggs/female/year; Sj = 0.185 (18.5% of hatchlings reach their first reproductive season); Sa = 0.105 per year (10.5%/yr adult survivorship; 12.2% over the two observed adult years). Interpretation: with a net reproductive rate R = Fa*Sj = 0.44 eggs that survive to adulthood per adult female, the cohort is far below replacement in isolation — a single-closed-patch population would decline without immigration or higher quality habitat. This is internally consistent with Table 2, where even the best patches have Fa*Sj of only about 1.2-2.1 with higher survivorship, and with the expert's description that this is a space/shelter-limited species in a fire-maintained habitat. Limitations and biases: only 105 reproducing females contributed to Fa, and the last two ages contribute only 13 of them, so Fa's upper ages are weakly supported (weight is dominated by the 92 age-1 females); the cohort followed four years, so Sa is estimated from only two adult transitions and assumes those rates are representative of the long-run adult schedule; survivorship estimates assume the cohort is a closed, non-migrating sample (the 10% juvenile emigration stated in the problem would make true survival slightly higher than the capture-based ratios). The age-1 clutch (2.1 eggs) is small, so Fa is sensitive to the SVL-clutch function at small sizes; if the function underestimates early clutches, Fa would be higher.

### Problem

Task 3: Using Table 2 (8 patches with vital rates, patch size and open sandy habitat), develop functions that estimate Fa, Sj and Sa for any patch, and a function that estimates C, the carrying capacity of scrub lizards for a given patch.

### Analysis

Table 2 gives Fa, Sj, Sa and observed density for 8 patches of very different sizes (8.5-278 ha) and open sandy areas (1.7-84 ha). The problem's own conjecture is that the vital rates depend on patch size and the amount of open sandy area, and the expert confirmed that the operative resource is open sandy ground (basking, nesting, foraging) rather than total patch area. Method: ordinary least squares linear regressions of each vital rate on (i) open sandy habitat H (primary predictor, following the problem's conjecture and the expert's guidance) and (ii) total patch size S (sensitivity check). Linearity was chosen because with only 8 data points a simple monotone form is the most defensible, and all four regressions are strong (R^2 = 0.66-0.84 for H). Carrying capacity is built as C_i = D(H_i) * H_i, i.e., the density on open sandy habitat multiplied by the open sandy area: the expert confirmed capacity is a space/shelter limit set by open sandy area (order of magnitude 10-50 lizards per ha of open sand), and the Table 2 density column is the empirical anchor. Data cleaning: Table 2 was normalized (the patch label 'C' is a case typo for 'c'); no missing values or duplicates; all units consistent (ha, lizards/ha).

### Modeling Process

Fits of y = a + b*H on the 8 patches (H in ha): Fa(H) = 5.737 + 0.0709*H (R^2 = 0.770); Sj(H) = 0.1338 + 0.000761*H (R^2 = 0.662); Sa(H) = 0.0720 + 0.001080*H (R^2 = 0.811); D(H) = 50.238 + 0.6927*H (R^2 = 0.837). Sensitivity fits on total size S: Fa(S) = 5.915 + 0.0223*S (R^2 = 0.738); Sj(S) = 0.1361 + 0.000236*S (R^2 = 0.615); Sa(S) = 0.0754 + 0.000331*S (R^2 = 0.738); D(S) = 51.18 + 0.2269*S (R^2 = 0.871). Carrying capacity function: C(H) = D(H)*H = (50.238 + 0.6927*H)*H = 50.238*H + 0.6927*H^2 lizards. A two-variable fit (Fa, Sj, Sa on both S and H) was also computed; the H coefficients carry the signal and the S coefficients are small and in places negative, so the single-variable H functions are used as the estimators and the S fits are reported as sensitivity. Example evaluations: a patch with 10 ha open sand: Fa = 6.45, Sj = 0.141, Sa = 0.083, C = 672 lizards; a patch with 50 ha: Fa = 9.28, Sj = 0.172, Sa = 0.126, C = 3593 lizards. Parameter table: all coefficients are derived from Table 2 (task dataset), no external parameters. Empirical validation band from expert exchanges 3 and 5: D ~ 10-50 lizards/ha of open sand; the fitted D(H) spans 50-64/ha over the Table 3 landscape, at the upper edge of that band (the density is itself an increasing function of sandy area in the data).

### Outcome Analysis

The functions say: more open sandy habitat means higher fecundity (bigger females, more eggs), higher juvenile and adult survival (more basking/nesting ground, more prey, less exposure), and higher density, so carrying capacity grows faster than linearly in sandy area. The strong fit for Sa (R^2 = 0.81) and D (R^2 = 0.84) supports the expert's mechanism that shelter/space in open sand is the limiting factor; Sj fits worst (R^2 = 0.66), meaning juvenile survival has substantial patch-to-patch noise beyond sandy area (predation, fire history, microhabitat). Biases/limitations: 8 points is a small sample, so coefficients have wide confidence intervals; the linear-in-H form will over-predict if the true relationship saturates at very large patches (no Table 2 patch exceeds 84 ha of sand, so extrapolation above that is untested); the regression uses all 8 patches including the small, low-density ones, which pull the intercepts up; and C(H) inherits the density model's bias — if real densities cap below the regression line for large sandy areas, C is overestimated for the big patches. The total-size sensitivity check shows total area alone is a slightly better predictor of density (R^2 = 0.871 vs 0.837) than sandy area, a tension the expert resolved in favor of open sand as the mechanistic resource; both should be reported when interpreting results.

### Problem

Task 4: From the migration histogram (juvenile lizards marked, released and recaptured up to 6 months later, surveys out to 350 m from release sites), estimate the probability that a lizard survives the migration between any two patches i and j.

### Analysis

The histogram gives the distribution of distances at which marked juveniles were recaptured: 42% at 50 m, 25% at 100 m, 18% at 150 m, 12% at 200 m, 2% at 250 m, 0% at 300 m, 1% at 350 m (these sum to 1.0, so the file needed no repair). Two modeling questions had to be resolved: (i) what the recapture distribution means — an expert exchange confirmed that dispersing juveniles usually survive the trip itself, and that the recapture data measure movement plus short-term survival, not establishment; and (ii) how to turn a distance distribution into a survival probability for a trip between patches — the expert confirmed the 350 m survey radius is the outer limit of detectable (and biologically realistic) movement, with tens to a few hundred meters typical and gaps beyond ~350 m of unsuitable matrix effectively isolating patches. Method: (a) the empirical cumulative distribution of recaptured distances is used directly as the in-domain survival-to-distance function on [0, 350] m (a juvenile that reaches distance x has not died en route, and 42% of recaptures are already within 50 m); (b) an exponential decay e^{-lambda*d} fitted to the distribution estimates the tail, giving a decay constant lambda = 0.01/m (grid fit over the 7 distance classes).

### Modeling Process

Let F(x) = P(recaptured within x m) be the empirical CDF: F(50)=0.42, F(100)=0.67, F(150)=0.85, F(200)=0.97, F(250)=0.99, F(300)=0.99, F(350)=1.00. Survival to a patch at distance d (0 <= d <= 350 m): P_surv(d) = F(d), i.e., 0.42 at 50 m, 0.67 at 100 m, 0.85 at 150 m, 0.97 at 200 m, 0.99 at 250 m, 1.00 at 300-350 m. Exponential model: f(d) ~ e^{-lambda d} with lambda = 0.01/m, so P_surv(350) = 1 - e^{-0.01*350} = 0.970 and P_surv(500) = 1 - e^{-5} = 0.993. Weighted mean recapture distance: 105.5 m; median 50 m. For the metapopulation (Task 5), where the map distances between the 29 patches are not provided, the per-trip survival used is 0.97 (the value at the 350 m boundary, the maximum credible inter-patch gap); trips across more than ~350 m of matrix are treated as not occurring (patches effectively isolated), per the expert's isolation threshold.

### Outcome Analysis

The migration survival probability is high — about 0.97-1.0 for any two patches within the detectable movement range (up to 350 m) — so the histogram supports the interpretation that dying en route is rare; the cost of migration is the loss of the ~10% of juveniles that attempt it when they fail to establish, not mortality in transit. The distribution is strongly right-skewed (most movers travel 50-150 m), which the exponential tail captures: beyond 250 m, fewer than 3% of movers go farther, so patch pairs separated by more than about 250-350 m exchange almost no juveniles. Limitations: the 0% at 300 m and 1% at 350 m are almost certainly detection artifacts (a marked lizard 300-350 m away was simply not found), so the true tail may be slightly heavier; the survey was circular (350 m radius), so 'distance moved' is a radial distance and inter-patch trip distances can differ from it; recapture also conflates survival with detection probability, which declines with distance, so the estimated survival is a lower bound at long distances (real survival is at least as high). With no map coordinates, the exact i-to-j distances cannot be computed; the model uses the boundary value 0.97 for any connected pair and zero exchange beyond 350 m of matrix.

### Problem

Task 5: Develop a model to estimate the overall population size of scrub lizards for the 29-patch landscape of Table 3 (Avon Park Air Force Range), and determine which patches are suitable for occupation and which would not support a viable population.

### Analysis

Table 3 gives patch size and open sandy habitat for 29 patches (total 518 ha, of which 137.2 ha is open sand; data needed no cleaning). The model combines the Task 3 functions with the Task 4 migration term. Assumptions, each tied to the data or an expert exchange: (1) densities and carrying capacity are functions of open sandy area (expert: space/shelter in open sand sets the ceiling); (2) each patch's population is at or near its carrying capacity at equilibrium (the density column in Table 2 is an observed equilibrium); (3) 10% of each patch's juvenile cohort emigrates each year (problem statement; expert: ontogenetic, first-year only, and on net beneficial), adults do not move (problem); (4) emigrants survive the trip with probability 0.97 and are redistributed in proportion to the receiving patches' carrying capacities (Task 4); (5) without map coordinates, all patch pairs within the matrix are treated as connected; pairs separated by more than ~350 m of unsuitable matrix would exchange no juveniles (expert isolation threshold) — this is the model's main structural uncertainty. Viability rule: a patch supports a viable population if its carrying capacity is at least ~10 lizards and its intrinsic growth factor is at or above 1, i.e., lambda = Sa + Fa*Sj >= 1 (adults persist and each adult female is replaced).

### Modeling Process

For each patch i with open sandy area H_i: Fa_i = 5.737 + 0.0709*H_i; Sj_i = 0.1338 + 0.000761*H_i; Sa_i = 0.0720 + 0.001080*H_i; D_i = 50.238 + 0.6927*H_i (lizards/ha of open sand); C_i = D_i * H_i (carrying capacity). Intrinsic growth factor lambda_i = Sa_i + Fa_i * Sj_i. Migration: emigrants from i = 0.10 * Fa_i * Sj_i * C_i (10% of the juvenile cohort output); arrivals to j = 0.97 * (total emigrants) * C_j / (sum of C). Net per-patch change = (lambda_i - 1)*C_i + arrivals_i - emigrants_i. Overall landscape population = sum of C_i. Results (year 0): total carrying capacity = 7723 lizards (range 7 to 1216 per patch; top contributors: patch 12 = 1216, patch 2 = 697, patch 15 = 657, patch 17 = 619). lambda_i >= 1 for 4 patches: 2 (1.025), 12 (1.146), 15 (1.016), 17 (1.006). Suitable patches (C_i >= 10 and lambda_i >= 1): 2, 12, 15, 17. All other 25 patches have lambda_i < 1 (intrinsic decline) and would persist only through juvenile immigration; the smallest (patch 18: C = 6.5, sand ratio 0.08) cannot sustain even a small population. Sand-ratio (H/S) check per the expert's suitability rule (>=50% good, <20-30% marginal): patches 24 (0.49), 25 (0.83), 26 (0.93), 27 (0.61), 28 (0.64), 7 (0.79), 5 (0.51) are open-sand rich; patches 18 (0.08), 3 (0.17), 20 (0.18), 14 (0.18), 23 (0.20), 17 (0.23), 19 (0.23) are marginal on sand fraction but several are large enough that their absolute sandy area still supports hundreds of lizards. Sensitivity (encroachment sweep): without management, open sand shrinks by 6%/yr and total capacity falls 7723 -> 3954 (10 yr) -> 2070 (20 yr) -> 1098 (30 yr), with the number of self-sustaining patches dropping from 4 to 0 by year 10 at 6%/yr (1 patch left at year 10 at 3%/yr).

### Outcome Analysis

The landscape supports roughly 7,700 scrub lizards at equilibrium, concentrated in the four largest, sandy-rich patches (2, 12, 15, 17), which are the only patches that replace themselves without immigration. The remaining 25 patches are leaky: their intrinsic growth is negative (lambda 0.84-0.97), so they are maintained as a metapopulation by the 10% juvenile exchange from the core patches — this is exactly the 'source-sink' structure the expert described (immigration is net beneficial; small patches depend on it). Patches that would not support a viable population: all 25 non-core patches if isolated; specifically the tiny ones (18, 3, 21, 26, 25, 22, 16, 4, 24, 1, 19, 20, 14, 23, 13, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 5, 7, 27, 28, 29) fail the lambda >= 1 test, and the six with C_i < 10 lizards (18, 3, 21, 26, 25, 4) fail even the minimum-size test. The metapopulation total is robust to the redistribution assumption (arrivals roughly mirror emigrations in total, so the landscape sum is close to sum C_i regardless of routing), but per-patch numbers are not. Limitations: (i) no map coordinates were provided, so inter-patch distances and the 350 m isolation rule cannot be applied per pair — the connected-matrix assumption overstates connectivity for distant small patches; (ii) the model is at equilibrium and ignores stochasticity, predation pulses and fire; (iii) vital-rate functions extrapolate beyond the 84 ha sandy maximum of Table 2 only slightly (max landscape sandy area is 19.2 ha, well inside range); (iv) if migration survival is lower than 0.97 for distant pairs, sink patches decline faster and the core-sink structure sharpens; (v) the 7723 figure assumes every patch is occupied at carrying capacity, which is an upper bound — realistically some marginal patches are unoccupied.

## Subtask 6: Task 6: Vegetation density increases about 6% per year within the Florida scrub. Make a recommendation on a policy for c

### Problem

Task 6: Vegetation density increases about 6% per year within the Florida scrub. Make a recommendation on a policy for controlled burning.

### Analysis

The policy question is: how often and under what rules should prescribed fire be applied to hold the scrub in the state the lizards need? The expert's account of fire effects shapes the model: a burn causes a short-term population dip (direct mortality, lost cover) followed by recovery over about 1-3 years, often to higher densities than the pre-burn overgrown condition; the real danger is suppression (no burns, vegetation closes in) or burning so often there is no recovery time. The quantitative core: vegetation cover grows by 6%/yr, so to keep cover inside a healthy band [c_low, c_high] (open, low, patchy vegetation) a burn is needed every T = ln(c_high/c_low)/ln(1.06) years. The band is chosen from the expert's description of healthy scrub: open sandy ground dominant, low patchy shrub, full sun — which corresponds to the lower part of the cover range; the upper bound is where shrub cover starts shading out the sand and depressing the vital rates measured in Table 2 (the Task 3 regressions show Sj and Sa rising with open sand, so the policy is to keep open sand high).

### Modeling Process

Cover dynamics with a burn reset: c_{t+1} = min(c_t*1.06, 1.0); when c_{t+1} >= c_high, burn: c_{t+1} = c_low and the population takes a one-time dip factor of 0.7 (30% transient mortality, the expert's 'drops immediately'). In unburned years the population growth factor is lambda = 1 + 0.3*(q-1) with habitat quality q = 1.0 for cover <= 0.75 falling to 0.3 at closed canopy, plus a +0.10 recovery boost while cover <= 0.5 (reopening, prey return, recolonization). Burn interval for a band: T(c_low, c_high) = ln(c_high/c_low)/ln(1.06). Computed intervals (encroachment 6%/yr): band 0.4->0.7: 9.6 yr; 0.3->0.6: 11.9 yr; 0.3->0.7: 14.5 yr; 0.2->0.6: 18.9 yr; 0.2->0.5: 15.7 yr. 200-year comparison (starting cover 0.3): no burns: cover reaches closed canopy in ~13-14 years and the population declines to near zero; burns on the 0.3->0.6 band (every ~11.9 yr): the population dips ~30% at each burn but grows strongly between them (recovery boost), for a net long-term increase; tighter bands (0.4->0.7) give shorter intervals and higher average density but burn-year dips every ~10 years. The expert's 1-3 year recovery window is honored: with ~12-year intervals there is always >10 unburned years of recovery between dips, and the model's recovery boost applies for the whole healthy-cover phase.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendation: a scheduled prescribed-burn policy on a patch rotation of roughly 10-12 years (burn when shrub/canopy cover reaches about 60-70% of the healthy maximum, resetting to about 30%), implemented as follows: (1) Burn each patch on a fixed rotation, never more often than once every ~8 years, to leave the 1-3 year recovery window the expert described (plus buffer); (2) prioritize burns on patches whose open sandy fraction is drifting below about 50%, since those are where juvenile and adult survivorship are measurably dropping (Task 3 regressions); (3) sequence burns so that at any time a majority of the landscape — especially the four core source patches (2, 12, 15, 17) — is in the 1-3-year post-burn high-quality window, because that is when juvenile output and recolonization are strongest and sink patches are best supplied (Task 5 metapopulation result); (4) pair burns with the ~350 m connectivity rule: burn corridors and small patches adjacent (within ~350 m) to a just-burned source patch so that the post-burn influx of juveniles lands in suitable open habitat; (5) make suppression the explicit failure case: the model shows that at 6%/yr encroachment, no-burn management halves the landscape's carrying capacity within a decade and eliminates every self-sustaining patch within 10-20 years. Obstacles (from Task 1): unexploded ordnance and live-fire restrictions on the range constrain burn timing and locations, weather windows (wind, rain) limit the annual burn season, and the rotation must be long enough that military training can schedule around it. Limitations: the dip factor (30%) and recovery boost (+0.10) are order-of-magnitude values from the expert's description, not measured post-fire counts; the 6%/yr encroachment rate is from aerial photos of unburned scrub and may vary by patch type; the model is deterministic and single-patch, so it shows the mean policy effect but not the risk of a bad burn season; and the optimal interval is robust (any band holding cover in roughly the 30-70% range gives a viable long-term population), so the exact band choice matters less than ensuring burns actually happen.
