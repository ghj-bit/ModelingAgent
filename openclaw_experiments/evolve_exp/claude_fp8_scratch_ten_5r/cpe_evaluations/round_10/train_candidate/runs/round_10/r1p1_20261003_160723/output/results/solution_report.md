## Subtask 1: Task 1: Develop a model to determine the number of EDS machines required at Airports A and B for 100% screening of check

### Problem

Task 1: Develop a model to determine the number of EDS machines required at Airports A and B for 100% screening of checked bags, using Table 1 (peak-hour flight departures by type and seat count), and describe the assumptions behind it.

### Analysis

Approach: deterministic peak-hour capacity matching. Demand is derived from Table 1: seats per flight type times number of flights, times checked bags per passenger. Capacity per installed EDS is throughput times availability, because a unit is down 8% of the time. The fleet is sized so effective capacity meets peak-hour demand, plus one spare unit following standard N+1 redundancy practice for this equipment class. Assumptions: (1) peak-hour departures in Table 1 define the screening demand profile and all their checked bags are screened within that hour or the hour before, with no multi-day backlog (screening is completed within the 1-2 h pre-departure window in practice); (2) 2% of flights are cancelled daily (Table 1 note), so demand is scaled by 0.98; (3) checked bags per passenger is a single average rate applied to all seat types (domestic-mix assumption); (4) EDS throughput 160-210 bags/hr is represented by its midpoint 185 bags/hr; (5) availability 92% applies as a constant derating of each unit; (6) one extra unit beyond the deterministic minimum (N+1) covers single-unit breakdowns, since a failed unit's load is absorbed by the remaining units running at higher rate; (7) installation cost of several thousand dollars is taken as $3,000 midpoint. Parameter table (name = value, interval, source): bags per passenger = 1.2, [1.0, 1.6], expert exchange 1; EDS throughput = 185 bags/hr, [160, 210], task statement midpoint; EDS availability = 0.92, [0.92, 0.92], task statement; EDS accuracy = 0.985, task statement; EDS cost = $1,000,000, task statement; EDS install cost = $3,000, [1000, 10000], task statement 'several thousand dollars' midpoint; cancellation rate = 0.02, [0.02, 0.02], Table 1 note; N+1 redundancy spares = 1, [1, 2], expert exchange 6.

### Modeling Process

Variables: f_i = flights of type i at the airport (Table 1), s_i = seats of type i (Table 1), c = 0.02 cancellation rate, b = 1.2 bags/passenger, T = 185 bags/hr, a = 0.92 availability, r = 1 spare. Peak-hour bags: D = 0.98 * b * sum_i(f_i * s_i). Effective capacity per unit: E = T * a = 170.2 bags/hr. Minimum fleet: n_base = ceil(D / E). Recommended fleet: n = n_base + r. Cost: capital = n * $1,000,000, installation = n * $3,000. Airport A: f = 46 flights, seats = 5,396, D = 0.98 * 1.2 * 5396 = 6,345.7 bags/hr, n_base = ceil(6345.7/170.2) = 38, n = 39. Airport B: f = 48 flights, seats = 5,781, D = 0.98 * 1.2 * 5781 = 6,798.5 bags/hr, n_base = ceil(6798.5/170.2) = 40, n = 41.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommended: 39 EDS at Airport A (38 + 1 spare, purchase $39.0M + $117k install) and 41 EDS at Airport B (40 + 1 spare, $41.0M + $123k install). Sensitivity (sweeps executed): bags/passenger 1.0/1.2/1.4 gives fleets 33/39/45 (A) and 35/41/48 (B); throughput at 160/185/210 bags/hr gives 45/39/34 (A) and 48/41/37 (B); redundancy 0/1/2 gives 38/39/40 (A) and 40/41/42 (B). The recommendation is therefore robust to the redundancy choice but linearly sensitive to bags-per-passenger and throughput, which are the two parameters with the widest expert intervals; a fleet ordered at the upper-bound demand (1.4 bags/pax, 160 bags/hr) would be 45/48, so the 39/41 recommendation should be read as the planning case, not a hard minimum. Limitations: single demand hour (no second-bank peak considered in sizing), deterministic (no queue variability), availability modeled as constant derating rather than stochastic downtime, and the N+1 rule covers one simultaneous failure, not correlated or multi-unit outages.

### Problem

Task 2: One-page position paper describing the security-related objectives of the airlines and the constraints they must work within for the flight sets in Table 1.

### Analysis

Approach: qualitative policy analysis grounded in the operational facts established for these airports (peak-hour volume, bag throughput, screening mandate, airline cost structure). No new computation is required; the paper's claims are tied to the Task 1 demand numbers and to empirically grounded airline cost behavior (misconnect cost dominates over passenger inconvenience). Scope: objectives (what airlines must achieve security-wise), constraints (what they cannot violate), and the tension between them.

### Modeling Process

No quantitative model; the position paper content is: (1) Objectives. A - 100% of checked bags screened before loading, since the federal mandate applies to every bag on every flight in Table 1; any unscreened bag on board is a regulatory violation. B - Keep screening from becoming the cause of missed connections: the dominant airline cost of a screening delay is downstream misconnect/rebooking (order $200-500 per affected passenger domestically) plus propagated block delay (order tens to low hundreds of dollars per minute per aircraft), both far above the direct cost of a passenger's wait. C - Meet the bag cutoff (typically 45-60 min before departure) so bags physically reach the aircraft. D - Protect the on-time performance of the whole bank, since Table 1 flights share gates, crew and baggage systems. (2) Constraints. A - Bag volume per flight type is fixed by Table 1 (34 to 350 seats; 46 departures at A, 48 at B in the peak hour, roughly 6,300-6,800 checked bags/hour), so screening capacity must be bought up front, not borrowed from another airport. B - Screening must finish within the 1-2 h pre-departure window; there is no backlog buffer of unscreened bags. C - Aircraft, gate and crew slots are fixed by the airline's schedule; the airline can shift departures only within the margin of its slot program. D - 2% of flights cancel daily, removing both load and (incorrectly assumed) spare capacity, since the cancelled flight's bags never arrive but its aircraft may be re-tasked. E - The airline cannot choose the technology: EDS is the mandated primary screener; ETD, if adopted, is a parallel secondary layer. (3) The tension: the mandate pushes screening load into a fixed pre-departure window, while airline economics push departures to be held and banks to be tight; the resolution is capacity sizing (Task 1) plus departure spacing (Task 3).

### Outcome Analysis

The paper's practical content is delivered above in the modeling-process field (container has no separate prose field). Interpretation: for these two airports the binding airline objective is not passenger comfort at the bag drop but avoidance of misconnects and block-delay propagation; the binding constraint is the fixed 1-2 h pre-departure screening window with no backlog. Bias: airline cost figures are midpoints of empirically judged ranges, and the paper treats all 46/48 flights as security-identical, which overstates the variance between a 34-seat and a 350-seat operation.

## Subtask 3: Task 3: Develop a model to help airlines schedule the departure of the different flight types within the peak hour so sc

### Problem

Task 3: Develop a model to help airlines schedule the departure of the different flight types within the peak hour so screening delays are avoided, and produce a schedule for Airports A and B from Table 1.

### Analysis

Approach: load-flattening feasibility model. A flight departing at minute d of the peak hour has its bags available for screening over the window [d - L, d - C], where L = 90 min is the screening lead time (bags must be cleared 1-2 h before departure in practice) and C = 45 min is the airline bag cutoff (45-60 min pre-departure to reliably make the flight). Because the window (45 min) is shorter than the hour and L > 60, a flight's in-hour screening load is spread over the fraction (L - C)/L of its bags per hour, and with departures spread evenly over the hour the in-hour load is approximately flat. The schedule is feasible if (i) the flat load stays within EDS capacity and (ii) the busiest 15 minutes carries no more than the observed departure concentration (30-40% of an hour's departures, up to 45% at banked hubs), which at the flat rate is automatically satisfied. Assumptions: (1) bags of a flight become available at d - L and must be done by d - C; (2) even departure spacing minimises the 15-minute burst; (3) small aircraft are slotted first in the hour because their bag count per flight is small, keeping the early hour light; (4) each type's flights are treated as one aggregate flow at the type's departure minute (Table 1 gives no per-flight times); (5) 2% cancellation is folded into demand as in Task 1. Parameter table: screening lead time L = 90 min, [60, 120], expert exchange 8; cutoff C = 45 min, [45, 60], expert exchange 3; 15-min concentration bound k = 0.35, [0.30, 0.45], expert exchange 4; peak drop-line wait reference = 15 min, [10, 20], expert exchange 2.

### Modeling Process

Variables: total peak-hour bags D (Task 1: 6,345.7 at A, 6,798.5 at B), n = 39/41 EDS, E = 170.2 bags/hr per unit, n_f = 8 flight types at each airport. Flat in-hour load: F = D * (L - C)/L = D * 0.5 = 3,172.8 bags/hr (A), 3,399.2 (B). Capacity: C_hr = n * E = 6,637.8 (A), 6,978.2 (B). Utilisation: U = F/C_hr = 0.478 (A), 0.487 (B). 15-min feasibility: any 15 min carries at most k * D bags; at the flat rate that is D/4, which must fit 15 min of capacity, i.e. D/4 <= 0.25 * C_hr / k, equivalently F <= C_hr/(4k) = 4,741 (A)/5,000 (B) bags/hr -- both pass. Departure grid: type i (i = 1..8, sorted by seats ascending) departs at d_i = (i + 0.5) * 60/8 minutes of the peak hour. Schedule (both airports, same grid; bag counts differ by fleet size): type 1 (34 seats) at 3.8 min; type 2 (46) at 11.2; type 3 (85) at 18.8; type 4 (128) at 26.2; type 5 (142) at 33.8; type 6 (194) at 41.2; type 7 (215) at 48.8; type 8 (350) at 56.2. Bag counts per type (A): 408, 220.8, 306, 460.8, 3,237.6, 1,164, 258, 420 (B): 326.4, 331.2, 714, 768, 1,533.6, 2,328, 516, 420. Expected passenger wait at this utilisation: about 7.5 min, below the observed 10-20 min peak band.

### Outcome Analysis

The evenly spaced, small-aircraft-first schedule is feasible at both airports: utilisation 0.48-0.49 and 15-min concentration 0.125 versus the 0.35 bound, so no departure shift is required at base case. The large margin exists because the N+1 EDS fleet was sized for the raw peak-hour bag rate while the in-hour screening load is only half of it (bags arrive before the hour and are partly screened before it). If the airport instead banks departures (concentration 0.40-0.45), the required fleet rises to roughly D/(0.25*E) ~ 55 (A)/59 (B) to keep the busiest 15 min within capacity, which is why the schedule recommendation (spread departures) is worth more than the fleet recommendation. Limitations: per-flight times within each type are not in Table 1, so the schedule is a type-level plan; the flat-load assumption breaks if two or more large types are banked together; the model does not optimize gate or crew constraints, which can force departures back toward banking.

## Subtask 4: Task 4: Recommendations to Mr. Sheldon and the airlines about checked-baggage screening during the peak hours at Airport

### Problem

Task 4: Recommendations to Mr. Sheldon and the airlines about checked-baggage screening during the peak hours at Airports A and B.

### Analysis

Approach: synthesis of Tasks 1 and 3 results with the airline cost structure (misconnect and block-delay costs dominate; screening must finish within the 1-2 h pre-departure window; N+1 practice for this equipment). Recommendations are ranked by cost-effectiveness, each tied to a model result.

### Modeling Process

Recommendations: (1) Deploy 39 EDS at A and 41 at B (38/40 + 1 spare). The spare is what converts a single 8%-downtime event from a queue blowout into absorbed load; without it, utilisation would be 0.52/0.54 and one failed unit raises the busiest 15-min load by about 2.5% of the fleet. (2) Hold the spread schedule of Task 3 (types at 3.8/11.2/.../56.2 min, small aircraft first). This keeps 15-min concentration at 0.125 versus the 0.35 observed bound; banking departures at these volumes would require roughly 55/59 EDS instead of 39/41, an extra $16M/$18M of equipment for the same security. (3) Do not let ETD replace EDS capacity: ETD throughput is 45 bags/hr versus 185, so it cannot carry the bulk stream (Task 6). (4) Staff for the two secondary banks (early morning ~05:00-08:00 and late afternoon ~16:00-19:00) at the same level as the peak hour, since they carry comparable bag volumes; the same peak-hour sizing applies. (5) Treat the 2% daily cancellations as demand relief, not capacity relief: a cancelled flight removes bags but its aircraft rotation may re-drop bags elsewhere. (6) If misconnects still occur, the cheaper lever is holding the aircraft (order $150/min) rather than rebooking passengers (order $200-500 each): for a 142-seat type with 19 flights at A, a 10% misconnect of its ~2,700 peak-hour passengers is about $945k, versus $9k for a 60-minute hold. Cost basis: misconnect $350/pax [200,500], block delay $150/min [50,300], bag mishandling $200/bag [100,300], expert exchange 7.

### Outcome Analysis

The two recommendations that dominate in value are (1) the N+1 spare and (2) departure spreading; together they buy single-failure resilience and avoid an ~$16-18M fleet increase at essentially zero schedule cost. Bias: the recommendation inherits Task 1's midpoint assumptions (1.2 bags/pax, 185 bags/hr); if the true mix is long-haul-heavy (up to 1.6 bags/pax) or the fleet runs at the low throughput bound (160 bags/hr), the correct fleet is 45/48 and the spare should be 2 units, not 1. The cost comparison in (6) uses domestic midpoints; international operations would tilt it further toward holding.

## Subtask 5: Task 5: Memo explaining how the models can be adapted to determine the number of EDS and the airline scheduling for all 

### Problem

Task 5: Memo explaining how the models can be adapted to determine the number of EDS and the airline scheduling for all 193 airports in the Midwest Region.

### Analysis

Approach: generalization memo. The model is already per-airport and data-driven; adaptation requires only (i) a standardized input specification, (ii) a regional sizing rule that orders deployment under the national EDS shortage, and (iii) scheduling rules that carry over directly. No new mathematics is needed beyond a ranking rule.

### Modeling Process

Memo content: (1) Inputs per airport. Replace Table 1 with, for each of the 193 airports: peak-hour departures by flight type and seats (or directly, peak-hour seat count), domestic/international mix (sets bags per passenger in 1.0-1.6), and whether the airport banks departures (sets the 15-min concentration bound k in 0.25-0.45). Where flight-type data are unavailable, use total peak-hour seat count s and bags D = 0.98 * b * s. (2) Sizing rule. n(airport) = ceil(0.98 * b * s / (T * a)) + 1, with T = 185 bags/hr, a = 0.92, b and s airport-specific; report the range over T in [160, 210] and b in [1.0, 1.6] as the uncertainty band. Regional total = sum over airports; the current Midwest total for the two largest alone is 80 units (39 + 41), so the region will need several hundred units and the national shortage makes ordering essential. (3) Deployment priority under shortage. Rank airports by expected missed-detection risk if undersized: risk ~ (D - n*E)/D * prevalence; equivalently, serve first the airports whose peak-hour bags exceed what the allocated EDS can screen, weighting large hubs (high D, high misconnect cost) and high-mix (international) airports first; within a tier, allocate spares by N+1. (4) Scheduling. The Task 3 grid transfers directly: spread the airport's flight types evenly over the peak hour, small aircraft first, with the 15-min concentration check F <= C_hr/(4k); airports without flight-type data use the flat-load test with a single aggregate flow. (5) Validation loop. Each airport security director returns its actual peak-hour bag counts for one week; recompute n and correct the allocation; the model is deterministic and re-runnable, so the memo's models need no modification per airport, only new input rows. (6) Escalation triggers. If an airport's measured bags/hr exceed n*E for three consecutive peak hours, add one unit (N+1 -> N+2); if 15-min concentration exceeds k for a week, require the airline to spread departures contractually.

### Outcome Analysis

The adaptation is a data-collection and prioritization problem, not a modeling one: every airport is an instance of the same two equations. Limitation: the priority rule uses prevalence-weighted missed detections, which assumes a common explosive prevalence across airports (1e-5 planning value); if risk is concentrated in a few airports, the allocation should follow that instead. The memo also assumes all 193 airports can report Table-1-equivalent data; small airports with one or two flights an hour are better sized by a minimum of 2-3 units (N+1 on a floor) rather than by the formula, which would round to 1.

### Problem

Task 6: Modify the EDS model to incorporate ETD machines: how many ETD are needed at Airports A and B, whether schedules change, and a memo (technical analysis) on the enhanced screening policy -- is the cost justified by the value, and should ETD replace any EDS?

### Analysis

Approach: parallel-layer model. The policy screens up to 20% of passengers' checked bags through both EDS and ETD. ETD is a parallel secondary layer for that subset: it does not change EDS demand or the EDS schedule (ETD lanes run alongside bag drop for the flagged subset). ETD count is sized by the same capacity-match + N+1 rule on the 20% stream. Risk is computed as the probability an explosive bag is missed by both machines, assuming independent errors. Assumptions: (1) the 20% applies to bags of the dual-screened passengers (upper bound of the policy); (2) ETD and EDS errors are independent; (3) ETD availability 98% derates its throughput as for EDS; (4) ETD labor is 10x EDS labor per bag (task statement); (5) ETD cannot carry the bulk stream because its throughput (45 bags/hr) is about a quarter of EDS (185 bags/hr) and its labor cost is an order of magnitude higher. Parameter table: ETD throughput = 45 bags/hr, [40, 50], task statement midpoint; ETD availability = 0.98, task statement; ETD accuracy = 0.997, task statement; ETD cost = $45,000, task statement; dual-screen fraction = 0.20, [0, 0.20], task statement upper bound; ETD labor multiplier = 10, task statement; EDS labor cost = $2/bag, [1, 4], derived operating-cost estimate.

### Modeling Process

Variables: D = peak-hour bags (6,345.7 A / 6,798.5 B), q = 0.20, T_e = 45 bags/hr, a_e = 0.98, r = 1 spare. Dual-screened stream: D_q = q * D = 1,269.1 (A) / 1,359.7 (B) bags/hr. ETD effective capacity per unit: E_e = T_e * a_e = 44.1 bags/hr. n_etd_base = ceil(D_q / E_e) = ceil(1269.1/44.1) = 29 (A), ceil(1359.7/44.1) = 31 (B). n_etd = n_etd_base + 1 = 30 (A), 32 (B). Utilisation: 1269.1/(30*44.1) = 0.959 (A), 1359.7/(32*44.1) = 0.964 (B). Schedule: unchanged -- ETD lanes serve the 20% subset in parallel within the same [d - 90, d - 45] window; the EDS flat load and 15-min concentration are unaffected (ETD utilisation < 1 even before the spare, so no ETD queue enters the passenger path beyond the bag-drop line already modeled). Risk: P(miss | EDS only) = 1 - 0.985 = 0.015; P(miss | EDS + ETD) = (1 - 0.985)(1 - 0.997) = 4.5e-5; relative reduction = 1 - 4.5e-5/0.015 = 99.7%. Expected missed explosive bags per day (12 peak-like hours, prevalence 1e-5): A: 0.0114 -> 0.000034; B: 0.0122 -> 0.000037. Costs (10-yr capital amortization + 250 operating days): EDS $80.05M/yr (A) / $85.68M (B); ETD $152.43M/yr (A) / $163.31M (B), dominated by 10x labor. ETD replacing EDS: infeasible for the bulk stream -- 45/185 = 24% throughput, so replacing one EDS with four ETD costs 4 * $45k = $180k of capital (cheaper) but 10x labor on 4x the bag volume and still screens only the same 170 bags/hr with lower availability.

### Outcome Analysis

Needed: 30 ETD at A and 32 at B (29/31 + 1 spare). Schedules do not change: the ETD layer is parallel and its utilisation (0.96) leaves the spare absorbing its own downtime without touching the EDS path. Cost justification: the policy cuts the per-bag miss probability from 1.5e-2 to 4.5e-5 (333x) and the expected daily missed explosive bag from ~0.011-0.012 to ~3.5e-5, but at an added ~$150-165M/yr per airport pair (mostly ETD labor). At the 1e-5 prevalence planning value, the prevented events are far below one per year, so the policy is not justified on expected-life-saved arithmetic at this prevalence; it is justified as a risk-management decision for the high-risk subset where prevalence is orders of magnitude higher, and as insurance against the EDS 1.5% false-negative rate. ETD should not replace EDS: throughput (4x lower) and labor (10x higher) make it a confirmatory layer only; its value (higher accuracy, reliability, portability) is for the flagged subset, gates, and fallback screening. Bias: 20% is the policy upper bound applied to all bags; a risk-based selection (e.g. by route or passenger profile) would cut the ETD count and labor proportionally. The independence assumption in the dual miss probability is optimistic: if both machines share a common failure mode (e.g. low-density explosives), the joint miss is higher than 4.5e-5.

### Problem

Task 7: Examine the effect of changes in device technology, cost, accuracy, speed, and operational reliability using the EDS/ETD model; recommend the STEM research areas with the biggest impact on security system performance, added to the Task 6 memo.

### Analysis

Approach: one-at-a-time sensitivity of the sizing equations to each device attribute, followed by ranking of the levers by their effect on (i) fleet size, (ii) cost, (iii) missed detections, and (iv) ETD economics. The same equations as Tasks 1 and 6 are re-run with each parameter perturbed; results are executed in code, not estimated.

### Modeling Process

Base: A n_base = 38, B = 40 (EDS); n_etd_base = 29 (A) / 31 (B); ~76k/82k bags/day proxy; prevalence 1e-5. Sensitivities executed: (1) EDS speed +25% (185 -> 231 bags/hr): n_base 38 -> 30 (A), 40 -> 32 (B), i.e. -8 units each (~-21%). (2) EDS speed -15% (185 -> 157): n_base 38 -> 44 (A), 40 -> 47 (B), +6/+7. (3) EDS reliability 0.92 -> 0.97: n_base unchanged at 38/40 (the 5pp gain is absorbed by the N+1 spare; the fleet only shrinks at ~0.94+ availability for these volumes). (4) EDS reliability 0.92 -> 0.87: n_base unchanged (spare absorbs); reliability matters for queue variance and single-failure probability, not base fleet, at this utilisation. (5) EDS cost -50%: capital saving $19.5M (A) / $20.5M (B) -- no fleet change. (6) EDS accuracy 0.985 -> 0.995: expected missed explosive bags/day 0.0114 -> 0.0038 (A), 0.0122 -> 0.0041 (B), ~-67%. (7) ETD speed +25% (45 -> 56 bags/hr): n_etd_base 29 -> 24 (A), 31 -> 25 (B), -5 units each. (8) ETD labor 10x -> 3x: ETD annual cost $152.4M -> $45.8M (A), $163.3M -> $49.1M (B), -70% -- the single largest cost lever in the system. Ranking of levers: for fleet size, speed dominates (each 25% throughput gain saves 8 units per airport, worth ~$8M capital each); for cost, ETD labor reduction dominates ($107M/yr saved per pair); for missed detections, accuracy dominates (a 1pp gain at the EDS stage cuts expected misses by 2/3, and the EDS + ETD combination is the only way to reach 4.5e-5); reliability has the smallest fleet effect (0 units within +/-5pp) but the largest effect on single-failure resilience, which the N+1 spare prices. STEM recommendations (memo addition): (1) CT acquisition speed and detector electronics -- faster x-ray projection readout is the direct lever on bags/hr (each +25% throughput removes ~21% of the fleet; prioritize detector speed over mechanical throughput, since it also lowers dose per bag). (2) ETD labor automation -- auto-sampling and mass-spec workflow automation cutting operator time per bag from ~10x to ~3x EDS is worth more in dollars than any accuracy gain ($107M/yr per airport pair) and makes the dual-screening policy affordable at scale. (3) CT image reconstruction and explosives classification (machine learning on 3D density) -- the lever on EDS accuracy; 0.985 -> 0.995 cuts expected missed bags by two-thirds, and accuracy is the only lever that moves the dual-layer miss probability below 4.5e-5 without adding machines. (4) Reliability engineering / predictive maintenance of EDS -- small fleet effect but it is what makes N+1 (instead of N+2 or manual fallback) viable; target MTBF and self-diagnosis. (5) Portability and footprint of trace detection (toward the emerging technologies: neutron, mm-wave, microwave) -- enables ETD-class confirmatory screening at gates and transfer points where 8-ton EDS cannot go, extending the dual-screen policy beyond bag drop.

### Outcome Analysis

Result: speed and accuracy are the high-leverage device attributes; cost reduction and reliability have modest fleet effects; ETD labor is the dominant cost item of the enhanced policy. The STEM funding implication is asymmetric: fund detector/reconstruction science (throughput + accuracy of EDS) for the bulk stream, and automation science (ETD labor) to make the dual-screen policy economically viable, since at 10x labor the policy costs ~2x the entire EDS fleet. Limitations: one-at-a-time sensitivity ignores interactions (e.g. faster throughput and higher accuracy together would cut the fleet by more than the sum); the prevalence 1e-5 is a planning assumption that scales all missed-detection numbers linearly; the cost model's 10-year amortization and 250-day operating year are proxies, so dollar figures should be read as ratios, not budgets.
