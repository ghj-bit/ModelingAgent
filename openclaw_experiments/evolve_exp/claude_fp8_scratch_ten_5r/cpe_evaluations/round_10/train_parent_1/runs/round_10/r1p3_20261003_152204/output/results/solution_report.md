## Subtask 1: Subproblem 1: develop a model that captures the flow of play point by point, identify which player is performing better 

### Problem

Subproblem 1: develop a model that captures the flow of play point by point, identify which player is performing better at any moment and by how much, and support it with a match-flow visualization. Applied primarily to the Alcaraz-Djokovic final (match 2023-wimbledon-1701) and validated on the other 30 featured Wimbledon 2023 men's matches. The tennis-specific requirement that the server's advantage be factored in is an explicit part of the scope.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) point outcomes are the elementary unit of play, and a player's 'performance at a time' is his realized point output relative to what the match situation would predict; (b) the dominant structure of tennis point play is the serve advantage plus score-situation leverage (break points, tiebreak points) - confirmed as the first-order explanation of match swings in expert exchange 1, so raw points/games would misattribute the serve advantage to 'form'; (c) short-term form changes are transient: an expert-estimated self-correction window of about 5-15 points or one to two games (exchange 2), so the flow must be smoothed on roughly that scale; (d) first-serve quality is not a free control - forcing it raises double-fault and weak-second-serve risk (exchange 3) - so it enters only as an observed rate, never as a decision variable. Approach: estimate point-win probabilities from the data, conditioned on (server, first/second serve, score state, break-point pressure); convert each realized point into a signed 'surprise' relative to that expectation; smooth with an exponential moving average whose half-life is set from the exchange-2 recovery window. This is sound because the conditioning removes the structural serve advantage (which would otherwise masquerade as momentum) while preserving genuine deviations in play, and the EMA gives a well-defined, reproducible measure of who is better at each point and by how much.

### Modeling Process

Data: 7,284 point rows, 31 matches (Wimbledon 2023 men's, after 2nd round). Cleaning: no full or key duplicates; no server-alternation violations (0/1157 game transitions); game/set victor flags consistent with point victors (0 mismatches); elapsed time monotone; missing only in descriptive columns (speed_mph 752, return_depth 1309, serve_width/depth 54), which the model does not use. Score encoding: '0,15,30,40,AD' in normal games, raw integers in tiebreaks; mapped to state s in {1 leads, 2 leads, even}; pressure p in {none, BP on p1 serve, BP on p2 serve}. Point model: p1e_i = P(player1 wins point i | server, serve_no, s, p), a 2x2x3x3 table estimated on all 31 matches with cells of n>=20 kept and rare cells pooled to the global rate. Observed rates: server wins 74.9% of first-serve points and 52.4% of second-serve points; on break points the receiver wins 26.1% (vs first serve) and 48.5% (vs second serve); leading the score raises the leader's point share (e.g. server at 40-0 or similar states: ~0.75-0.78). Flow: surprise s_i = y_i - p1e_i with y_i in {0,1} the realized p1 point; flow F_t = EMA of s_i with half-life 10 points (chosen inside the 5-15 point self-correction window, exchange 2). F_t > 0: player 1 is performing better than the situation predicts, |F_t| = how much (in expected-point share). A companion serve-battle series A_t = EMA of the serving-side surplus over the same half-life gives who is winning the serve/return contest. Game level: P(player1 takes game | state at game start, momentum shift) by exact dynamic programming over the in-game score states using the same point table; game edge g_t = 2*P1(game) - 1; slow flow G_t = EMA of g_t with half-life 3 games. Visualization (described, not rendered as image): plot F_t and -F_t stacked across the 334 points of 1701 with set boundaries and swing markers from G_t crossing zero with a 0.08 deadband - the per-point table is in results/flow_1701.csv and the per-game table in the game-model run.

### Outcome Analysis

On 1701 (Alcaraz = player 1): F1 stays deeply negative through set 1 (mean -0.156, min -0.273) - the model quantifies Djokovic's 6-1 dominance as Djokovic outperforming his own serve-conditional expectation by roughly 15 percentage points of expected point share; the flow recovers to near zero in set 2 (mean -0.018) and swings positive in set 3 (mean +0.112, max +0.244) as Alcaraz takes 6-1; it oscillates around zero in set 4 (mean -0.001) with the slow-flow G crossing back to Djokovic-favorable after game 4 (G=0.107 at set-4 game 4, i.e. after Alcaraz's game, Djokovic takes over 6-3), and ends set 5 with a final positive push (G crosses to +0.089 at game 10) for the 6-4 close. Five slow-flow swing events are detected (set 3 games 2/4/6, set 4 game 7, set 5 game 10), matching the five narrative direction changes. The metric answers 'how much better' in units of expected point share: e.g. at the set-1 trough Alcaraz is playing ~0.27 points below his expectation per point, which over a 40-point set is ~11 points - a concrete magnitude. Limitations: (1) the point table is estimated on the same 31 matches (in-sample conditioning), so per-match flow magnitudes are slightly optimistic; (2) the 10-point half-life smooths out sub-game blips, so the 'who is better right now' answer changes slowly by design; (3) tiebreak games use numeric score encoding mapped to lead states, losing the 40-AD nuance there; (4) no player-level strength prior - a weak player performing well and a great player performing normally can show similar F values, which is acceptable for within-match flow but not for cross-player comparison.

## Subtask 2: Subproblem 2: assess the coach's claim that swings in play and runs of success are random, using the flow model and metr

### Problem

Subproblem 2: assess the coach's claim that swings in play and runs of success are random, using the flow model and metric from subproblem 1.

### Analysis

The coach's null hypothesis is that point-by-point play has no memory beyond the match structure: runs of success for either player are what an i.i.d.-like point process (with the fixed serve advantage) would produce. The sound test design separates three levels where 'random' means different things: (a) the raw server-wins sequence z (1 if the server takes the point) - under pure i.i.d. with the observed server-win rate p0, its runs should match the Wald-Wolfowitz distribution; (b) the surprise-based flow F_t - a random process would produce a flow with no autocorrelation and runs of flow-sign with the i.i.d. baseline; (c) the magnitude of streaks - longest consecutive server-won / receiver-won streaks compared to the binomial expectation. Because tennis scoring gives the server a large fixed advantage (74.9%/52.4%), the raw z sequence is structurally biased and must be tested against its own p0, not 0.5; the surprise series removes that structure by construction, so its autocorrelation is the genuine 'momentum vs random' signal. This is sound because each level tests a different component of the claim and they triangulate: if swings were purely random, the surprise flow would be a near-white sequence.

### Modeling Process

For each of the 31 matches (n = 122..337 points): (a) R = Wald-Wolfowitz runs count on z, z-score under H0: mu = 2*n1*n2/n + 1, var = 2*n1*n2*(2*n1*n2 - n)/(n^2*(n-1)); (b) longest streaks of server-wins and receiver-wins vs binomial expectation ln(n)/ln(1/p0); (c) runs test on the sign of F_t (half-life 10). Pooled across matches for the summary statistic. All computations in code/momentum.py --random, results in logs/momentum_random_all.log.

### Outcome Analysis

Result: the coach is partially right and partially wrong, and the answer differs by level. (1) At the raw point level the process is close to memoryless: pooled runs z-score = -0.15 (mean over 31 matches), i.e. the server-wins sequence has statistically indistinguishable runs from an independent sequence at its own rate - no anti-clustering, no point-to-point contagion detectable at this level. (2) But the longest streaks exceed the i.i.d. baseline: observed max server streaks 8-19 vs expectation 3-9 (e.g. 17-19 in matches 1301, 1407, 1501), because the serve advantage and short games create structured runs even without momentum. (3) Decisively, the surprise flow F_t is strongly non-random: its runs z-score is -7.1 to -14.6 in every one of the 31 matches (pooled mean -10.8), i.e. the flow shows heavy negative runs-statistics (persistent sign stretches far beyond i.i.d. at the 0.001 level). Interpretation: point-by-point, play is nearly random once the serve and score situation are accounted for (the coach is right that individual points carry little memory), but the 'swings' people see are not random - the performance deviation itself is persistent over many points, and that persistence is what makes a swing look dramatic. Runs of success at the scoreboard level are therefore a mixture: their occurrence timing is largely random (breaks land when the stochastic point process allows), but their duration and the underlying form shifts are not. Caveats: with ~300 points per match and an EMA in the statistic, the runs test on F is a conservative read of persistence; the streak comparison uses an approximation for the expected maximum run; only one tournament/season of data is available, so 'random vs momentum' here is a within-Wimbledon-2023 statement.

## Subtask 3: Subproblem 3: build a model that predicts when the flow of play is about to swing, identify the most related factors, gi

### Problem

Subproblem 3: build a model that predicts when the flow of play is about to swing, identify the most related factors, give advice for a player facing a different opponent, and test on other matches with an honest read on performance, needed future factors, and generalizability (women's matches, other tournaments/surfaces, other sports such as table tennis).

### Analysis

Prediction target: define a swing as a sustained change in the flow metric - F moving by at least delta = 0.20 over a W = 15-point window (deadband chosen between the expert's ~5 pp 'noise floor' and ~10 pp 'real struggle' threshold on the first-serve scale, exchange 8; W inside the 5-15 point swing-recovery window, exchange 2). A swing toward player 1 (+1) or player 2 (-1) is the label at the point 15 steps before it completes, using only past information. Features must be things a coach could actually see or track live, and per expert exchange 6 the candidate causal indicators are: the returner winning points on the server's first serve (first-serve % dropping), and the break-point context (high-leverage points, exchange 1). Model: logistic regression on standardized features, trained on the final (1701) which contains the clearest, most dramatic swings, tested on all 29 other matches (leave-one-out on the flagship). This is sound as a first model because it is interpretable for the coaching memo, uses only observable quantities, and the train/test split avoids inflating the accuracy on the match everyone asks about. Honest caveat carried through: with only 14 swing-labeled points in the training match, the coefficient estimates are indicative of factor importance, not precise probabilities. Empirical parameter table (calibrated inputs with provenance): (1) HL_FLOW = 10 points, interval [5, 15], source: expert exchange 2 (slump self-correction window 5-15 points / 1-2 games); (2) HL_GAME = 3 games, interval [1, 5], source: expert exchange 2 (a game or two) applied at game scale; (3) SWING_DELTA = 0.20 in F units over W=15 points, interval [0.15, 0.25], source: expert exchange 8 (struggle threshold ~10 pp first-serve drop; noise floor ~5 pp) mapped onto the flow scale, sweep-validated in logs/momentum_sweep_delta.log and logs/momentum_sweep_W.log; (4) G_DEADBAND = 0.08 game-edge units, interval [0.05, 0.10], source: same exchange 8 band (between 5 pp noise and 10 pp real); (5) FS_STRUGGLE = 0.10 (first-serve % drop below own match norm that flags real struggle), interval [0.05, 0.15], source: expert exchange 8; (6) FS_DIP_5SET = 0.053 mean, interval [0.02, 0.08] normal, source: expert exchange 5 (3-8 pp late-match dip), empirically confirmed on the dataset (5-setter mean dip 5.3 pp, signal_test.py); (7) SLUMP_PERSIST_BOUND = 'at most ~1/5 of slumps persist beyond 2-3 service games', interval [0.02, 0.20], source: expert exchange 4, empirically 2% in this dataset (signal_test.py); (8) TT_SERVER_EDGE = 0.55, interval [0.53, 0.57], source: expert exchange 10 (elite table-tennis server point share), used only in the generalizability argument; (9) PERSIST_BOUNCEBACK = 0.11 within 10 first serves, interval [0, 0.5], source: dataset measurement (131 struggle stretches, 15 bounced back, signal_test.py), consistent with expert exchange 7. All other numbers in this task are computed from the task's own dataset (Wimbledon_featured_matches.csv) as stated in each field.

### Modeling Process

Features at point t (all computed from points < t): F1_lag (current flow, EMA half-life 10); per player: fs_K20 and fs_K40 (trailing 20/40 first serves: fraction that went in), fsdrop_K20/K40 (that player's match-norm first-serve % minus trailing value - the exchange-6/8 'server struggling' indicator, norm ~10 pp), bp_faced1_K20/bp_faced2_K20 (break points faced in last 20 points), p1_serve (who is serving), p1_leads/p2_leads (score state), press1/press2 (current break point), pts_elapsed (fatigue proxy, exchange 5). Label y_t in {-1,0,+1}: swing toward p1/p2 in (t, t+15] if |F_{t+15} - F_t| >= 0.20. Logistic regression (max_iter 2000) on y != 0 points. Coefficients (standardized) at W=15, delta=0.20: bp_faced1_K20 +0.62, bp_faced2_K20 -0.57, F1_lag -0.55, fs1_K40 -0.51 / fsdrop1_K40 +0.51, p1_serve +0.42, p1_leads -0.19. (At delta=0.15 the break-point and flow features dominate even more: bp_faced2 -1.23, F1_lag -1.10, bp_faced1 +0.46.) Evaluation at W=15/delta=0.20 over the 29 holdout matches: n=202 swing-labeled points, accuracy 0.861 vs majority-class base rate 0.72 (p1 swings 0.913, p2 swings 0.818); at W=25: n=362, accuracy 0.856 (p1 0.847, p2 0.865); at W=10: n=99, accuracy 0.717. Sweep logs: logs/momentum_sweep_delta.log, logs/momentum_sweep_W.log.

### Outcome Analysis

What predicts swings: (1) break-point context is the single strongest factor - facing more break points in the last 20 points predicts a swing away from the player facing them (coefficients +0.62/-0.57), consistent with exchange 1's 'high-leverage points' mechanism: the flow turns when leverage points start going the other way; (2) current flow F1_lag with a negative coefficient - the model learns partial reversion: when F1 is already high, an upward swing label is less likely, i.e. extreme flow tends to fade rather than continue, matching the expert's 'transient by default' view (exchanges 2, 7); (3) first-serve drop (fsdrop_K40, fs1_K40) is a genuine but secondary signal - the exchange-6 indicator 'returner winning on first serves' shows up with the expected sign (a server's falling first-serve % predicts a swing toward his opponent), supporting the coach-facing story even though its coefficient is smaller than the break-point terms. Performance and its limits: 86% accuracy on holdout swings is well above the base rate, but (i) the training set is small (14 labels), (ii) accuracy here means 'predicting the direction of a swing that is about to happen within 15 points', not forecasting swings from a calm state - most labeled points sit near an already-moving flow, so part of the skill is trend continuation within the swing's own 15-point definition, and (iii) per-match accuracy ranges 0.33-1.00; the model is weakest on short matches with few swings (n=1-10 there). Factors to add in future models: live first-serve % and double-fault count streamed per game (currently approximated over 20-40 point windows), unforced-error clusters (exchange 3 links them to serve-mechanics breakdown), set/game context (tiebreak proximity), player fatigue beyond a linear elapsed-points proxy (exchange 5), and player-pair priors from head-to-head data. Advice for a player facing a different opponent: the factors are opponent-agnostic (break-point leverage, first-serve % relative to one's own match norm, flow reversion), so the same live dashboard transfers; calibrate the deadband to the opponent's serve strength - against a big server the flow swings more through break points (weight the bp features), against a weaker server swings show up earlier in first-serve % (weight fsdrop), and keep the transient-by-default rule: only treat a swing as structural once it has survived about two service games (expert bound: at most ~1 in 5 slumps persist that long; data: 2%). Generalizability: (a) women's matches - same serve/return mechanism with a weaker serve advantage (expert exchange 9): the surprise-based flow transfers directly by re-estimating the point table on women's data, but the baseline is choppier (more breaks), so widen the swing deadband to avoid over-reading swings; (b) other tournaments - the table must be re-estimated per data sample; (c) court surfaces - faster surfaces increase the serve advantage (more first-serve holds, fewer breaks), which strengthens the serve-conditioning and shortens the break-point-driven swings; slower/clay reduces it, moving the system toward the women's-match regime; (d) other sports - table tennis is the boundary case (expert exchange 10): server wins only ~53-57% of points and there are no service games, so the game/hold component must be dropped entirely and only the surprise-EMA flow survives, on a shorter horizon (service alternates every 2 points) and with rally/spin/error features replacing serve features; sports with strong serve/hold structure (tennis, badminton, volleyball rally scoring with server advantage) accept the full model, sports with near-50/50 starts (table tennis, table-tennis-like) accept only the flow core.

## Subtask 4: Subproblem 4 (report/memo content): summarize findings into advice for coaches on the role of 'momentum' and on preparin

### Problem

Subproblem 4 (report/memo content): summarize findings into advice for coaches on the role of 'momentum' and on preparing players to respond to flow-affecting events during a match. This task carries the synthesis: what 'momentum' is in measurable terms, what is random and what is not, and the concrete live-response playbook implied by the models.

### Analysis

The memo must separate three claims that are usually conflated: (1) 'momentum is a real physical force' - rejected by the model; (2) 'swings are pure random noise' - the coach's claim, rejected at the flow level and accepted at the single-point level; (3) 'momentum is persistent performance deviation driven by serve/return quality and leverage points' - the supported, measurable definition. The advice section follows directly from the tested signals (first-serve % drop vs own norm, break-point leverage, flow reversion, slump persistence bound), so every coaching recommendation is a statement about a measured mechanism rather than a generic platitude. Scope: one-to-two page memo content, expressed here in machine-readable form.

### Modeling Process

Synthesis of the three models into a decision rule for live use: define player's flow F (EMA half-life ~10 points of point-surprises); define a 'struggle flag' when a server's trailing first-serve % falls ~10 pp or more below his own match norm (expert threshold, exchange 8); define a 'structural swing flag' when a flow sign persists across ~2 service games (expert persistence bound, exchange 4; data: 2% of slumps persist). Coach-facing rules: R1 (trust the serve data, not the scoreboard) - a run of games without a first-serve % change is scoreboard noise (exchange 6: if the server is still holding easily, the turn is noise); R2 (act on the 10 pp line) - below ~5 pp first-serve % dip: normal, no change; at 10 pp: expect the opponent's break chances to rise, shift return aggression up; R3 (treat swings as transient by default) - prepare to respond within a game or two, do not re-plan the whole match until the sign survives two service games; R4 (leverage points are where swings are decided) - break-point conversion is the dominant swing factor in the prediction model, so preparation time should go into break-point and tiebreak-point routines; R5 (late-match expectation) - expect a 3-8 pp first-serve dip in the final set of a five-setter (exchange 5, data: mean 5.3 pp); plan energy and serve selection for it; a much larger late drop signals a physical problem, not just fatigue.

### Outcome Analysis

What momentum is, in this model: a persistent (half-life ~10 points) deviation of a player's realized point output from what his serve/score situation predicts, expressed in expected point share (e.g. '-0.27' means playing 27 percentage points of expected points below situation - roughly 10-11 points per set). What is random: individual points conditional on serve and score (pooled runs z = -0.15) - the coach is right that point-to-point there is no detectable contagion, and that when you 'lose momentum' on a random stretch, it usually fades within the same game. What is not random: the persistence of the deviation itself (flow runs z = -10.8 pooled, significant in all 31 matches), the ~2-3 game duration of slumps, and the fact that swings cluster at leverage points. Bias and limitations of the whole analysis: single tournament/season (Wimbledon 2023, grass, men's), in-sample point tables, small swing-training set (14 labels), no biomechanical or video data, and the flow metric's smoothing means it lags the true state by a few points. The model's strongest, most defensible claims are the mechanism-level ones (serve advantage 74.9%/52.4%, break-point leverage as the top swing factor, transient-by-default with a 2-service-game persistence test), and those are the ones the coaching advice rests on.
