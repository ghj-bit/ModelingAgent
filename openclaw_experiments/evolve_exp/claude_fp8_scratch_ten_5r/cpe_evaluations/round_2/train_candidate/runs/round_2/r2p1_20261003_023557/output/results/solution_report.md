### Problem

Sub-problem 1: identify, describe, and support with mathematical evidence the meaningful quantitative and qualitative patterns, relationships, measures and parameters within and between star ratings, reviews, and helpfulness ratings across the three product data sets (hair dryers, microwaves, baby pacifiers), so that Sunshine Company can inform its launch strategy and design choices.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (i) each row is one review of one product by one customer (verified: 1.001 reviews per customer-product pair); (ii) star_rating is the customer's satisfaction on the 1-5 scale; (iii) helpfulness votes measure perceived usefulness to other shoppers, not accuracy (expert exchange 2); (iv) review text is in English and the lexicon-based sentiment proxy is adequate for these categories; (v) the data end at 2015-08-31, a marketplace snapshot, so 'recent' means the last 12 months of the snapshot. Method: clean and normalize the three TSVs, then (a) compare star-rating distributions across products with point-biserial and AUC screening of candidate measures, (b) model review volume as log-linear in time, (c) regress each review's stars on the preceding stars of the same product, and (d) profile sentiment words and defect phrases by star level. This is sound because every pattern is either a descriptive statistic over the full data or a fitted regression with its goodness-of-fit reported, so each claim carries its own evidence.

### Modeling Process

Data: n(hair_dryer)=11470, n(microwave)=1615, n(pacifier)=18939; 473/55/5432 products; 30560 customers; dates 2002-03-02 to 2015-08-31. Parameter table (one row per empirical parameter): review_propensity_rate = 0.03-0.05 (fraction of buyers who write a review), interval [0.01, 0.10], source: expert exchange 1 (empirical range for Amazon-style categories), used to interpret review volume as purchases x propensity; recency_half_life = 180 days, interval [180, 365] days, source: expert exchange 8 (6-12 months suggested; 180 days chosen as the conservative end), used in the recency-weighted reputation R = sum_i 0.5^((t_max-t_i)/180) s_i / sum_i 0.5^((t_max-t_i)/180); seasonal_threshold = 0.15 (fractional Nov-Dec volume lift counted as seasonal), interval [0.10, 0.30], source: expert exchange 6 ('tens of percent' lift), used in the seasonality test; vine_inflation = 4.39 - 4.19 = 0.20 stars, interval from the data, source: task dataset (Vine vs non-Vine pooled mean star, results/pooled.json), used to explain early-rating inflation (exchange 10); verified_gap = 4.27 - 3.76 = 0.51 stars, source: task dataset (verified vs unverified pooled mean star). Models: (1) star distribution per product (counts and shares); (2) log-linear volume model log V(t) = a + b t fitted by OLS per category; (3) prior-rating regression s_i = alpha + beta * ctx_i + gamma_product + eps, where ctx_i is the mean of the preceding 5 stars on the same product_parent, fit by OLS with product fixed effects (pooled, 26064 reviews); (4) per-star sentiment profiles: pos(s) = E[number of distinct positive-lexicon words | star = s], neg(s) = E[number of distinct negative-lexicon words | star = s], net(s) = pos(s) - neg(s); (5) defect rate d(s) = P(review matches any of 6 defect-phrase patterns | star = s); (6) helpfulness ratio h = helpful_votes / total_votes for voted reviews.

### Outcome Analysis

Patterns within and between the datasets: (1) Star distributions differ sharply. Pooled share of 5-star = 62.6% (dryer 58.4%, pacifier 66.8%, microwave 41.3%); the microwave category is the least well-rated: mean star 3.45 overall vs 4.12 (dryer) and 4.31 (pacifier), and 31.8% of microwave reviews are 1-2 star vs 14.6% (dryer) and 11.3% (pacifier). Microwave reviews are also far more negative in text: net sentiment at 1 star is -0.88 (dryer -0.53, pacifier -0.10). (2) Review volume grows ~10x from 2011 to 2015 for every category (dryer 634 -> 3143, microwave 67 -> 549, pacifier 1005 -> 5796); pooled log-linear fit per category gives b = 0.042 (dryer, R^2 = 0.956), 0.045 (microwave, R^2 = 0.920), 0.057 (pacifier, R^2 = 0.929) per month. This is marketplace-wide review growth (more listings, more reviewers), not product-specific demand: it is nearly the same slope in three unrelated categories, so it must be netted out before reading any product-specific volume signal (exchange 1). (3) Star ratings are weakly self-reinforcing. Pooled OLS: a review's stars = 0.315 times the mean of the preceding 5 stars on the same product (p ~ 1e-201, R^2 = 0.065); within category, after controlling each product's running mean, beta = 0.072-0.089 (dryer p = 0.0015, pacifier p = 0.0004, microwave n.s.). A small positive feedback loop exists (shared complaints among buyers, exchange 4), but it explains <7% of the variance - a product's own quality dominates. (4) Sentiment words track star level monotonically in all three categories. Positive word count rises 0.80 -> 2.03 (pacifier) and 0.87 -> 2.18 (dryer) from 1 to 5 star; negative word count falls 1.39 -> 0.24 (dryer), 1.77 -> 0.30 (microwave), 0.90 -> 0.15 (pacifier). A 1-star review is 9.7x (pacifier), 12.4x (dryer) or 16.7x (microwave) more likely than a 5-star review to contain a negative-lexicon word (Fisher exact, p < 1e-80 in every case). (5) Defect phrases are concentrated in low stars and monotone: defect rate 0.51/0.38/0.18/0.08/0.06 (dryer), 0.49/0.46/0.23/0.10/0.06 (microwave), 0.26/0.21/0.11/0.07/0.03 (pacifier) for stars 1-5. Top defect themes in 1-2 star reviews are the same everywhere: returns, breakage, safety wording, 'stopped working'. (6) Helpfulness: 33.5% of reviews have any votes; among voted reviews the mean helpful ratio is 0.684; helpfulness correlates with quality only weakly and inconsistently (point-biserial -0.20 to +0.47), so it is a popularity signal, not a quality signal (exchange 2). (7) Verification and Vine: 84.8% of reviews are verified purchases and average 4.27 stars vs 3.76 for unverified (+0.51); Vine reviews (1.0% of the data) average 4.39 vs 4.19 for non-Vine (+0.20), the early-rating inflation channel (exchange 10). Limitations: the lexicon is category-agnostic, so 'design/comfort' or 'noise level' keywords mean different things per product; review propensity is not directly observable (no purchase counts), so the 10x volume growth is an upper bound on true demand growth; the 2015-08-31 snapshot means 'trend' conclusions describe the 2011-2015 window only.

## Subtask 2: Marketing question 1: identify data measures based on ratings and reviews that are most informative for Sunshine Company

### Problem

Marketing question 1: identify data measures based on ratings and reviews that are most informative for Sunshine Company to track once the three products are on sale.

### Analysis

A tracking measure is 'informative' if it (a) separates high-reputation products from low-reputation ones and (b) does so consistently across all three categories, since one dashboard must serve all three launches. Screening method: for each product, aggregate candidate measures per product (minimum 30 reviews per product so early, unstable averages are not used - exchange 10), form a quality flag (top tertile of mean star), and compute the point-biserial correlation and AUC of each measure against that flag. Text measures come from two lexicons: positive and negative quality descriptors (exchange 3), and six defect-phrase patterns (returned/return, broke/broken, stopped working, does not work, safety, misleading listing - exchange 9). Candidate measures: 5-star share, 1-2-star share, share of reviews with any positive word, mean net sentiment (pos-neg hits), defect rate, share of reviews that received any helpful votes, mean helpful ratio, share of reviews mentioning a design feature, and mean star.

### Modeling Process

Per product, for product p with reviews i: S5_p = (1/n_p) sum_i [s_i = 5]; S12_p = (1/n_p) sum_i [s_i <= 2]; PosP_p = (1/n_p) sum_i [pos_i > 0]; Net_p = (1/n_p) sum_i (pos_i - neg_i); Def_p = (1/n_p) sum_i [defect_i]; Voted_p = (1/n_p) sum_i [tv_i > 0]; HR_p = mean over voted reviews of hv_i/tv_i; Feat_p = (1/n_p) sum_i [feat_i > 0]; M_p = (1/n_p) sum_i s_i. Quality flag Q_p = 1[M_p >= quantile_2/3(M)]. Screening statistic: point-biserial r(Q_p, measure_p) and AUC. Empirical inputs: the 1-2-star-share and defect patterns are the exchange-5/9 failure channels; the lexicon sizes are fixed (47 positive, 44 negative descriptors). Products with >=30 reviews: 92 (dryer), 14 (microwave), 92 (pacifier).

### Outcome Analysis

Recommended tracking set (in order of information): (1) Mean star rating - AUC 1.000 by construction and the baseline of every screen; point-biserial 0.62-0.69. (2) 5-star share S5 - point-biserial 0.67-0.76, AUC 0.98-0.99 in all three categories; the single best pure-rating quality measure. (3) 1-2-star share S12 - point-biserial -0.57 to -0.61, AUC 0.98-0.99 in reverse direction; the recommended early-warning channel (exchange 5: the low-star tail, not the average, predicts returns and reputation decay). (4) Mean net sentiment Net - point-biserial 0.36-0.49, AUC 0.71-0.81; the best text-based measure and the one that adds information beyond the stars (it captures wording intensity the star level alone does not). (5) Defect rate Def - point-biserial -0.27 to -0.59, AUC 0.80-0.91 in reverse direction for dryer/microwave; the diagnostic channel (exchange 9): it tells Sunshine which design feature to fix, not just that something is wrong. Rejected: positive-word share (AUC 0.67-0.86, useful but subsumed by Net), helpfulness measures (Voted_p, HR_p - inconsistent signs, point-biserial -0.31 to +0.47, AUC 0.29-0.82; a popularity signal per exchange 2), and feature-mention rate Feat_p (negative point-biserial in two categories - feature words appear in comparative complaints, so it is not a quality indicator). Limitations: the 14-product microwave screen is thin; the point-biserial values are screening statistics, not forecasts; a product needs ~30 reviews before any of these is stable (exchange 10), so in the first weeks the dashboard should report 'insufficient evidence' rather than a number.

## Subtask 3: Marketing question 2: identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns within each data set that might suggest a pr

### Problem

Marketing question 2: identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns within each data set that might suggest a product's reputation is increasing or decreasing in the online marketplace.

### Analysis

Reputation is a moving target: the measure must separate (a) marketplace-wide volume growth from product-specific signals, (b) satisfaction changes from demand changes, and (c) a real drift from a small-sample wobble. Assumptions from the expert exchanges: volume = purchases x a stable low propensity (exchange 1), so volume alone is never a satisfaction indicator; recency weighting with half-life 180 days defines 'current reputation' (exchange 8); a Nov-Dec volume lift of at least 15% over the other months would count as seasonal (exchange 6); the failure-worsening signature is a rising 1-star share (exchange 5, 7). Method: monthly aggregation (n, mean star, 1-star share, 1-2-star share); OLS slopes of log volume, mean star and 24-month 1-star share; a seasonality test at the 15% threshold; a recency-weighted mean; and a 12-month rolling reputation table over the last 8 quarters. The 30-review floor and the 12-month window keep small-sample noise out (exchange 10).

### Modeling Process

Let V_t, M_t, F1_t, F12_t be the monthly review count, mean star, 1-star share and 1-2-star share. Volume model: log V_t = a + b t + eps (OLS). Satisfaction model: M_t = a' + b' t + eps (OLS). Worsening test: F1_t on the last 24 months, slope c. Seasonality: lift = mean(V_t : t in {Nov,Dec}) / mean(V_t : other months) - 1; seasonal if lift >= 0.15. Current reputation: R = sum_i w_i s_i / sum_i w_i with w_i = 0.5^((t_max - t_i)/180). Rolling reputation: 12-month trailing windows M_q, F1_q, F12_q by quarter. Decision rules: DECREASING reputation if c > 0 (rising 1-star share) or b' < 0 over a sustained window; INCREASING if b' > 0 and F12_q falling; FLAT otherwise. Volume change is reported separately and never read as a satisfaction change (exchange 1).

### Outcome Analysis

Hair dryer: volume log-slope +0.0416/month (R^2 = 0.956), i.e. roughly +5%/month of marketplace growth; mean star +0.0050/month (R^2 = 0.28); 24-month 1-star share slope +0.0005/month (essentially flat); seasonal lift -7.6% (below the 15% threshold - no seasonal component). Current reputation: recency-weighted 4.205, last-6-month 4.221, last-90-day 4.158 with 8.7% one-star. Rolling quarters 2013Q4 to 2015Q3: mean star 4.18 -> 4.24, 1-star share 6.7% -> 7.6%, 1-2-star 12.3% -> 11.7%. Verdict: reputation stable-to-slightly improving; the rising volume is marketplace growth, not a satisfaction signal (exchange 1). Microwave: volume log-slope +0.0453/month (R^2 = 0.920); mean star -0.0007/month overall (R^2 = -0.02, no trend) BUT the yearly means tell a clear improving story: 2.58 (2011) -> 2.93 (2012) -> 3.21 (2013) -> 3.51 (2014) -> 3.77 (2015); 24-month 1-star share slope -0.0081/month (falling); seasonal lift -24.5% (none). Rolling quarters: mean star 3.21 -> 3.70, 1-star share 31.2% -> 19.2%, 1-2-star 39.3% -> 24.4%. Verdict: reputation INCREASING - the one category in the data with a sustained multi-year improvement, though it remains the weakest of the three (mean 3.77, 19% one-star in the last 90 days). Pacifier: volume log-slope +0.0573/month (R^2 = 0.929); mean star +0.0056/month (R^2 = 0.30); 24-month 1-star share slope -0.0002/month (flat); seasonal lift +13.7% (below threshold). Rolling quarters: mean star 4.30 -> 4.35, 1-star share 5.7% -> 6.0%, 1-2-star 10.8% -> 10.5%. Verdict: reputation stable and strong. Cross-product lesson for Sunshine: the correct reading of a volume drop or rise is first to net out the category-wide ~5%/month growth; only a deviation from that trend, combined with a moving 1-star share, is a reputation signal (exchange 1, 5, 7). Limitations: monthly means in the microwave's early years rest on <30 reviews and are noisy; the log-linear volume fit is a category-level average, so a single product's volume can deviate; the 2015-08-31 endpoint means the 'current' window is Apr-Aug 2015.

### Problem

Marketing question 3: determine combinations of text-based measure(s) and ratings-based measures that best indicate a potentially successful or failing product.

### Analysis

Products are labeled at product level (not review level) because success/failure is a property of the product's reputation. Labels: SUCCESS = mean star >= 4.2, FAILING = mean star < 3.4, products in between are ambiguous and excluded; a minimum of 30 reviews per product keeps the label stable (exchange 10). Features: mean star (rating channel), share of reviews with a positive word, mean net sentiment, defect rate (text channels - exchanges 3 and 9), and design-feature mention rate. Model: logistic regression with the label as response; a measure is kept in the recommended combination if it is significant or if it raises the AUC beyond the best single measure. The exchange-7 failure signature (growing 1-star share, repeated specific defects) is encoded in the features as the 1-2-star share and the defect rate.

### Modeling Process

For product p: y_p = 1[M_p >= 4.2] - 1[M_p < 3.4] (0 otherwise). Logistic model: P(y_p = 1) = expit(beta0 + beta1 M_p + beta2 PosP_p + beta3 Net_p + beta4 Def_p + beta5 Feat_p). Because the label is a deterministic function of M_p, the model separates perfectly (AUC = 1.0) by construction; the informative output is therefore the single-measure AUC table (which text measures separate success from failure on their own) and the profile contrast between success and failing products. Labeled products (>=30 reviews): 45 (dryer: 41 success, 4 failing), 7 (microwave: 4/3), 67 (pacifier: 65/2).

### Outcome Analysis

Best combination: mean star + 1-2-star share + defect rate + net sentiment. Single-measure AUCs (success vs failing): mean star 1.000 (by label construction); net sentiment 0.743-1.000; positive-word share 0.770-1.000; defect rate 0.081-0.800 in reverse direction (i.e. high defect rate marks failing products); feature mention 0.239-0.333. The profile contrast is the actionable result: SUCCESS products average 4.43-4.55 stars, defect rate 0.034-0.100, net sentiment +1.24 to +1.63, 86-87% of reviews contain a positive word. FAILING products average 2.37-3.13 stars, defect rate 0.158-0.341 (3-10x higher), net sentiment +0.09 to +0.69 (near neutral - the complaints are specific and negative rather than diffuse), 67-81% positive-word rate. The decisive combination is therefore: a mean star below ~3.5 together with a defect rate above ~0.15 and a net sentiment near zero identifies a failing product; a mean star above 4.2 with defect rate below 0.10 and net sentiment above 1.2 identifies a successful one. The 1-2-star share is the early-warning companion: it rises before the mean falls (exchange 5, 7). Top defect themes in the failing profile match exchange 9's 'repeated specific defect': returns, breakage, safety wording, stopped working (counts in 1-2-star reviews: dryer 269/233/181/146, microwave 86/68/63/61, pacifier 181/162/99/82). Limitations: the failing class is small (2-4 products per category), so the profile differences are real but the classifier cannot be calibrated for rare failure modes; the 4.2/3.4 label cut is a judgment choice - a 4.0/3.5 cut gives the same qualitative separation; the model is descriptive (which features co-occur with success/failure), not causal (removing a defect theme does not automatically raise the rating).

### Problem

Marketing question 4: do specific star ratings incite more reviews? For example, are customers more likely to write some type of review after seeing a series of low star ratings?

### Analysis

The question confounds two possible mechanisms, and the expert exchange resolved which one to test (exchange 4): (a) an onlooker effect - seeing low ratings makes browsers write reviews (predicted to be weak: review propensity is a few percent of buyers and browsing deters purchases); (b) a shared-complaint effect - existing buyers with a bad experience are validated by the visible low ratings and post. The testable consequence of (b) is that the recent star level of a product shifts the star level of its next reviews; the testable consequence of (a) would be a change in the review count after low ratings, which cannot be measured here because the data contain no non-reviewing buyers. Design: for each review, ctx = mean of the preceding 5 stars on the same product_parent; regress the review's own star on ctx and on the product's running mean (the baseline expectation absent any context effect); pooled version adds product fixed effects. The review-text check asks whether post-low-rating reviews are more defect-laden (the shared-complaint signature).

### Modeling Process

For review i on product p at time t_i: ctx_i = (1/5) sum_{j in P5(i)} s_j where P5(i) is the preceding 5 reviews of p; base_i = (1/i) sum_{j < i} s_j (running mean). Model 1 (within product): s_i = alpha + beta * ctx_i + gamma * base_i + eps. Model 2 (pooled): s_i = alpha + beta * ctx_i + sum_k delta_k 1[product = k] + eps. Effect size of interest is beta; the delta-R^2 of adding ctx to the baseline measures how much of the variation the recent context explains beyond the product's own history. n: 10864 (dryer), 1518 (microwave), 12088 (pacifier) contextualized reviews; 26064 pooled.

### Outcome Analysis

Yes, weakly, and through the shared-complaint channel, not an onlooker stampede. Pooled: beta = 0.315 stars per star of recent context (p ~ 1e-201), R^2 = 0.065. Within category, after controlling each product's running mean: beta = 0.0724 (dryer, p = 0.0015), 0.0884 (microwave, p = 0.197, n.s. at n = 1518), 0.0886 (pacifier, p = 0.0004). A product whose last 5 reviews averaged 1 star writes its next review ~0.08 stars lower on average than a product whose last 5 averaged 2 stars - a real but small pull. Delta-R^2 is 0.0008-0.0010: the recent context adds almost nothing beyond the product's own running mean, meaning the 'incitement' is not a new phenomenon but the slow accumulation of a shared problem. The review count is essentially invariant to context (in this data every review has a body; the with-body propensity is 0.9998-1.000 in all context bins), consistent with exchange 4: seeing low ratings does not make browsers write; it makes already-dissatisfied buyers write, which is exactly the 0.315-star pooled pull concentrated in the low-star tail. Practical reading for Sunshine: a cluster of low ratings is both a symptom and a mild amplifier of an existing defect - it will not create a review wave, but it will make the next cohort of dissatisfied buyers a little more likely to post, and the defect themes in that cohort will be the same specific complaints (exchange 7, 9). Limitations: the preceding-5 context is a proxy for what a buyer actually sees (Amazon shows sorted/mixed reviews); the microwave result is underpowered (1518 contextualized reviews); no counterfactual exists for the onlooker mechanism, so its absence is inferred, not measured.

### Problem

Marketing question 5 plus deliverable 3: determine whether specific quality descriptors in review text ('enthusiastic', 'disappointed', and others) are strongly associated with rating levels, and write the one-to-two page letter to the Marketing Director summarizing the analysis with the most confidently recommended result and its justification.

### Analysis

Quality descriptors were operationalized as two lexicons (47 positive descriptors covering enthusiastic wording - love, great, excellent, perfect, recommend...; 44 negative descriptors covering disappointed wording - disappointed, poor, broken, waste, returned...) plus the six defect-phrase patterns, following exchange 3 (strong positive wording is a real but noisy enthusiasm signal, concentrated in 4-5 star reviews) and exchange 9 (specific defect complaints are the sharper signal). The association test per product: the descriptor count profile by star level (monotonicity), a Fisher exact test of negative-word presence at 1 vs 5 star, and the defect-rate gradient. The letter's most confident recommendation rests on the result with the largest, most consistent evidence across all three independent categories: the ratings/defect/sentiment triple that separates successful from failing products (Task 4), because it is the measure set Sunshine will actually track after launch and it is the one with the strongest and most uniform statistical support (odds ratios 9.7-16.7, defect gradients monotone in all three categories, profile gaps of 3-10x in defect rate).

### Modeling Process

For star level s in {1,...,5}: pos(s) = E[|tokens(reviews at s) intersect POS|], neg(s) = E[|tokens intersect NEG|], net(s) = pos(s) - neg(s), d(s) = P(defect | s). Association test: Fisher exact 2x2 of negative-word presence vs star in {1,5}: odds ratio OR_s = [P(neg | 1) / P(no neg | 1)] / [P(neg | 5) / P(no neg | 5)]. Monotonicity check: pos(1) < pos(2) < ... < pos(5) and neg(1) > neg(2) > ... > neg(5) and d(1) > d(2) > ... > d(5). Letter structure: (1) what was analyzed, (2) the five answers, (3) the recommended tracking set with thresholds, (4) the design-feature list from 4-5 star reviews, (5) the most confident recommendation with its justification, (6) limitations.

### Outcome Analysis

Strong, monotone association in all three categories. Positive descriptors: mean count 0.80 -> 2.03 (pacifier), 0.87 -> 2.18 (dryer), 0.90 -> 2.17 (microwave) from 1 to 5 star; share of reviews containing a positive word 0.50-0.56 at 1 star rising to 0.86-0.92 at 5 star. Negative descriptors: 0.90 -> 0.15 (pacifier), 1.39 -> 0.24 (dryer), 1.77 -> 0.30 (microwave). Fisher exact odds ratios for negative-word presence at 1 vs 5 star: 9.75 (pacifier, p ~ 1e-265), 12.37 (dryer, p ~ 1e-270), 16.68 (microwave, p ~ 1e-88). Defect gradient: 0.26 -> 0.03 (pacifier), 0.51 -> 0.06 (dryer), 0.49 -> 0.06 (microwave). So 'disappointed' wording and defect complaints are strongly concentrated in 1-2 star reviews and 'enthusiastic' wording in 4-5 star reviews - the association is one of the most robust in the data. Letter to the Marketing Director (summary): We analyzed 32,024 Amazon reviews of 5,960 products across the three categories, March 2002 to August 2015. Five answers: (1) Track four numbers: mean star, 1-2-star share, defect rate, and net review sentiment; helpfulness votes are a popularity signal and should not drive decisions. (2) Reputation trends: net out the category-wide ~5%/month review-volume growth first; a product is declining only when its 1-star share rises or its mean star falls over 12+ months - the microwave category is currently improving (2.58 in 2011 to 3.77 in 2015 mean star) while hair dryers (4.23) and pacifiers (4.35) are stable. (3) A product is on a failing trajectory when its mean star is below ~3.5 together with a defect rate above ~15% and near-neutral sentiment; successful products sit above 4.2 stars with defect rate under 10% and positive net sentiment. (4) Low ratings do not create a review stampede; they mildly validate existing disgruntled buyers (next review ~0.08 stars lower per star of recent context), so a low-rating cluster is a symptom to fix, not a traffic event to fear. (5) Review wording tracks ratings strongly: 1-star reviews are 10-17 times more likely than 5-star reviews to contain disappointed-type wording. Most confident recommendation: build the post-launch dashboard around the mean-star / 1-2-star-share / defect-rate triple, reviewed monthly from the 30th review onward. Justification: this is the only measure set that is monotone, significant and directionally consistent across all three independent categories (defect-rate gradient 0.26-0.51 at 1 star to 0.03-0.06 at 5 star; 1-vs-5-star negative-word odds ratios 9.7-16.7, all p < 1e-80; success-vs-failure defect-rate gap 3-10x), and it directly answers the two things a marketing director needs: when to worry (rising 1-2-star share) and what to fix (the recurring defect theme - returns, breakage, safety wording, stopped working). Design features to prioritize, from the 4-5 star reviews: ease of use (mentioned 1544-2237 times per category), size/portability, value for money, and for the dryer specifically drying speed. Limitations to note in the letter: the data end August 2015; early-product ratings are inflated by early adopters and Vine reviews (Vine 4.39 vs 4.19 overall) and typically drift down over the first months, so first-month averages should not be trusted; review counts are not purchase counts, so volume signals are only proxies for demand.
