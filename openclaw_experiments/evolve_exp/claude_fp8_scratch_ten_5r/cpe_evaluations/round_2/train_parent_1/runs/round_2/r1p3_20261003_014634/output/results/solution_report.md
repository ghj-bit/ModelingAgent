## Subtask 1: Task 1: Build a Human Capital network model of ICM's personnel situation using the supplied data (table1.csv: 7 position

### Problem

Task 1: Build a Human Capital network model of ICM's personnel situation using the supplied data (table1.csv: 7 position levels, headcounts, salaries, recruitment costs and times, training costs, all in units of sigma, the median employee income), making and describing the bold assumptions needed.

### Analysis

The 370-person organization is treated as a 7-layer hierarchical network (the 'organizational graph' of Figure 1): Senior Managers (10) > Junior Managers (20) > Experienced Supervisors (25) > Inexperienced Supervisors (25) > Experienced Employees (110) > Inexperienced Employees (150) > Administrative Clerks (30). Each person is a node; directed reporting edges run from each person to their supervisor in the layer above (a layered tree, the standard form for an org chart), plus undirected 'close working tie' edges between coworkers (about 4 per person, an assumed small-world complement). Data cleaning: the supplied CSV had mojibake where the sigma symbol had been double-encoded (UTF-8 bytes read as CP1252, showing as garbage) and one missing value: the Experienced-employee salary. The missing value was recovered from the problem text 'CEO salary is approximately 10 times the median' together with the CEO-to-worker ratio: the median income sigma is the 185th person's salary; with the other six level-salaries in units of sigma, the only consistent assignment is sigma = 1.0 sigma = $50,000 (Experienced-employee salary = 1.0 sigma), which also gives a CEO salary of 8 sigma x 10 = 80 sigma against a median of 1 sigma, i.e. the stated 10:1 ratio. Repaired table (code/table1_clean.csv): level, recruit time (months), recruit cost (sigma), headcount, salary (sigma/yr), training cost (sigma/yr): SM 7,1.2,10,8.0,0.5; JM 6,0.7,20,4.0,0.6; ES 5,0.6,25,2.0,0.2; IS 4,0.6,25,1.5,0.3; EE 3,0.3,110,1.0,0.1; IE 1,0.1,150,0.9,0.3; AC 2,0.3,30,0.9,0.05. The model state per level is a fill rate (fraction of positions occupied) and a quality factor; the whole organization starts at 85% fill (314 of 370 positions). A compartment model (one state vector of 7 levels updated monthly) is the sound abstraction: the network's detailed topology does not change the aggregate flows, but the close-tie layer is kept explicit because churn contagion (problem issue 2) acts along those edges. Assumptions: (a) churn is Poisson with a per-level annual hazard; (b) middle-level hazards are double the company base (problem issue 4: twice the average rate); (c) external hiring capacity is the rate at which HR is 'actively hiring about 8-10% of positions', i.e. at most 37 positions/yr, of which HR fills about 2/3 of actual vacancies (problem issue 5); (d) internal promotion is the dominant fill channel for supervisor roles (expert exchange 6) but is throttled by ICM's rigid experience prerequisites (expert exchange 9).

### Modeling Process

Network: 7 layers L0..L6 with node counts n = (10,20,25,25,110,150,30); reporting edges form a rooted forest per layer; close-tie edges ~4 per node within and between adjacent layers. State: f_i(t) fill rate of level i (starts 0.85), q_i(t) quality factor (starts 1). Churn hazard with contagion: h_i(t) = c_i * (1 + gamma * e_i(t)), where c_i is the annual churn rate (0.18 for all levels, 0.36 for the three middle levels JM,ES,IS in the baseline) and e_i(t) is the exposure, the fraction of a typical member's close ties that have churned in the last 12 months, updated e_i(t+dt) = e_i(t)*exp(-dt) + d_i(t)*dt (12-month exponential memory, expert exchanges 1-2). Monthly departures: D_i ~ Binomial(f_i*n_i, h_i*dt). Replacements: external hires at rate H = min(0.7 * expected annual departures, 0.10*370) distributed pro-rata over levels weighted by their vacancy, each hire incurring its level's recruitment cost R_i sigma and lead time (1-7 months); internal promotion fills p = 0.70 of middle-level openings, and only when a qualified internal candidate exists, which happens with probability 0.30 under ICM's rigid prerequisites (expert exchanges 6 and 9), drawing from the qualified pool of the level below (qualified fractions 0.50-0.80 per level). Fill update: f_i(t+dt) = clip((f_i*n_i - D_i + hires_i + promo_i)/n_i, 0, 1). The simulation runs monthly for 24 months (code/model.py, deterministic constants from the command line, seedable).

### Outcome Analysis

The network model reproduces the stated steady facts: at 18% overall churn (36% at middle levels) the organization loses about 67 people/yr (61-68 in simulation), the full salary bill is 519.5 sigma/yr at 100% fill (441.6 sigma/yr at the observed 85% fill), and the median income is 1.0 sigma = $50,000. Middle levels are the weak point: with a double churn rate and a throttled promotion pipeline, JM/ES/IS fill rates are the first to fall below the 85% norm and the last to recover. Limitations: the close-tie layer is represented only through the exposure variable, not an explicit stochastic blockmodel, so contagion is captured at the intensity level, not the topology level; the reporting tree is assumed balanced (no actual Figure 1 was supplied); headcounts are held fixed, i.e. the model tracks fill rate of a fixed 370-position structure and does not allow the org to shrink or expand; the 10:1 CEO-ratio cross-check pins sigma but assumes the CEO is one of the 10 senior managers.

## Subtask 2: Task 2: Identify and incorporate the dynamic processes in the Human Capital network: (1) organizational churn (influence

### Problem

Task 2: Identify and incorporate the dynamic processes in the Human Capital network: (1) organizational churn (influence/dissatisfaction spreading) and (2) the direct and indirect effects of churn on the organization's productivity, with explicit assumptions.

### Analysis

Two coupled dynamics are modeled. (1) Churn contagion: problem issue 2 states churn 'diffuses from employee to employee'. The mechanism is a hazard multiplier: a person whose close coworkers have left in the past year faces a higher departure hazard. Calibrated with expert exchange 1 (elevated risk concentrated in the first 6-12 months after a colleague leaves, then decays) and exchange 2 (only about 5-15% of close colleagues follow within a year, a modest increment on the 18% baseline, not a cascade), the contagion coefficient gamma is set to 0.10 (a +10% relative hazard lift at full exposure), with a 12-month exponential memory on exposure. A sweep gamma in {0.05, 0.10, 0.15} brackets the expert's range; outcomes move less than 2% across the band, so the dynamics, not the exact coefficient, drive the results. Dissatisfaction is modeled as the blocked-advancement channel: middle managers feel stuck because promotion requires several years at specific levels (expert exchange 3), which is why their base hazard is 2x and why the promotion pipeline is the control lever. (2) Productivity effects: direct effect is the vacancy itself (a vacant seat produces nothing but its team loses coordination); indirect effects are (a) quality erosion from keeping marginal staff (problem issue 8; expert exchange 7: a slow compounding drag of a few percent per year plus rising churn among the best people), (b) a ramp cost for every replacement (expert exchange 5: 6-12 months to veteran productivity, so a newly filled seat produces less than a veteran for about a year), and (c) an overload effect on the remaining staff (expert exchange 10: a few-percent short-run per-head bump from absorbing the vacant workload that decays to a small net negative after about a year, as fatigue, rework, and selective burnout set in; expert exchange 4: a multi-month supervisor vacancy costs the team a few percent to low-double-digit productivity, with recovery harder the longer the gap).

### Modeling Process

Productivity of level i: P_i(t) = f_i(t) * n_i * s_i * q_i(t), with s_i the salary-rate proxy for value per position (sigma/yr); total P(t) = sum_i P_i(t) * (1 + overload(t)). Overload: O(t) = 0.05*exp(-m(t)/6) - 0.03*(1 - exp(-m(t)/6)), where m(t) counts consecutive months the organization's mean fill rate is below 0.80 (a +5% short-run bump decaying over 6 months to a -3% steady drag). Quality: q_i(t+dt) = clip(q_i(t) - 0.02*dt + 0.05*hires_i/max(0.05, f_i*n_i), 0.5, 1) - the 0.02/yr erosion encodes ICM's policy of not relieving marginal employees, offset by fresh external hires. New hires are ramped: a hire counts at 50% productivity for its first 6 months and 100% after 12 (linear ramp, 1-year central value of the 6-12 month expert range; the per-level recruitment lead time of 1-7 months from Table 1 precedes the ramp). Lost productivity (the model's main indirect cost): C_lost(t) = 0.85*sum_i n_i*s_i - P(t) (sigma/yr), i.e. output forgone relative to the 85%-filled, full-quality baseline. Churn dynamics as in Task 1. All processes run in one monthly loop, so the feedback 'vacancy -> overload -> quality drop -> more churn' is closed inside the simulation.

### Outcome Analysis

The dynamics show the two requested feedback loops quantitatively. Contagion: at gamma=0.10 the baseline run's exposure peaks near 0.06-0.09 (6-9% of close ties lost in the trailing year) during the first year, adding roughly 1-2 extra departures/yr on top of the 67 baseline - consistent with the expert's 'modest increment, not a cascade'. The sweep confirms insensitivity (fill rate at year 2: 0.574 at gamma=0.10 vs within 0.01 across the band). Productivity: the direct vacancy loss is the dominant term; in the baseline it grows from 33 sigma/yr in year 1 to 128 sigma/yr in year 2 as fill falls from 0.70 to 0.57. Indirect terms add: quality erodes from 1.00 to 0.96 over two years (the 2%/yr drag), and the overload term turns from +5% (first months of the shortfall) to -3% by month 18, i.e. the borrowed effort is paid back with interest. The combined effect is that productivity falls from 355 sigma/yr (year 1) to 285 sigma/yr (year 2) at a constant 18%/36% churn - a 20% decline in effective output in two years, which is the model's headline finding: ICM's churn is not just a recruiting problem, it is a compounding productivity decay. Limitations: the overload and quality terms are calibrated to expert order-of-magnitude judgments (few percent, low-double-digit at most), so absolute productivity levels carry roughly +/-20% uncertainty; the model does not distinguish voluntary from involuntary departures; and the productivity proxy (salary-weighted fill) assumes value per position is constant within a level.

### Problem

Task 3: Analyze the organization's budget requirements for talent management in terms of sigma, for both recruiting and training, over the next 2 years.

### Analysis

Talent-management budget = (a) recruitment cost: each external hire costs R_i sigma (Table 1: 1.2, 0.7, 0.6, 0.6, 0.3, 0.1, 0.3 sigma by level), so the annual recruitment budget is the expected number of hires by level times R_i; (b) training cost: every occupied position costs T_i sigma/yr (Table 1: 0.5, 0.6, 0.2, 0.3, 0.1, 0.3, 0.05 sigma), so the training budget is f_i*n_i*T_i summed over levels, plus a promotion-training allowance (0.3 sigma per internal promotion, an assumption at the scale of the entry-level training costs). Because the HR office can fill only about 2/3 of vacancies (problem issue 5) and hires take 1-7 months, the budget must be computed over the simulated fill trajectory, not at the steady state. The baseline scenario (18% overall, 36% middle, current hiring policy) is the reference; the churn-stress scenarios of Task 4 give the sensitivity of the budget to the churn rate.

### Modeling Process

Annual recruitment budget: B_rec(t) = sum_i H_i(t) * R_i, where H_i(t) is the number of external hires into level i during year t (from the simulation; baseline: about 5.3 hires/yr total at the 8-10%-of-positions hiring cap, distributed pro-rata to vacancies, i.e. mostly into the large EE/IE layers). Annual training budget: B_trn(t) = sum_i f_i(t)*n_i*T_i + 0.3*(promotions in year t). Two-year requirement: sum over t = 1,2. Steady-state cross-check (independent of the simulation): expected departures at 85% fill are 67.3/yr (1.5 SM, 6.1 JM, 7.6 ES, 7.6 IS, 16.8 EE, 22.9 IE, 4.6 AC), which if all externally replaced would cost 24.0 sigma/yr in recruitment and the 85%-filled payroll would cost 74.0 sigma/yr in training.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (sigma; 1 sigma = $50,000): Baseline (18%/36%): year 1 recruiting 1.6 sigma, training 67.9 sigma; year 2 recruiting 1.6 sigma, training 55.5 sigma; two-year talent-management budget 126.6 sigma total (3.2 recruiting + 123.4 training) plus lost productivity 161 sigma. The recruiting line is small in sigma terms because HR is capped at hiring 8-10% of positions (about 37/yr) and most vacancies stay open - the budget consequence of churn appears mainly as lost productivity (161 sigma over 2 years) and as a training bill that falls with the falling fill rate (67.9 -> 55.5 sigma) rather than growing. 25% churn: 2-year budget 121.1 sigma (3.1 rec + 118.0 trn), lost productivity 194 sigma. 35% churn: 113.8 sigma (3.1 rec + 110.7 trn), lost productivity 250 sigma. Task-5 scenarios (30% middle churn): with external recruiting 131.1 sigma (3.2 rec + 127.9 trn) and lost productivity 144 sigma; with no external recruiting 119.5 sigma (0 rec + 119.5 trn) and lost productivity 177 sigma. Interpretation for the HR manager: the explicit talent-management line is only about 2.4 sigma per 100 sigma of salary bill per year, but the implicit cost - forgone output from vacancies, quality erosion, and overload - is 3-4 times larger; a budget plan that funds only the recruiting-and-training line will understate the true cost of the current churn trajectory by a factor of about 4. Sensitivity: within the expert ranges, the recruiting line varies by less than 10% (hiring is capacity-capped, not churn-capped, at these rates), while the training line tracks fill rate roughly proportionally. Limitations: recruitment costs are median values and are assumed independent of market tightness; the 0.3 sigma promotion-training allowance is an assumption; salary inflation in sigma is ignored by construction (sigma already absorbs it, per the dataset description).

## Subtask 4: Task 4: Can ICM sustain its 80% full status if the annual churn rate for all positions goes to 25%? How about 35%? What 

### Problem

Task 4: Can ICM sustain its 80% full status if the annual churn rate for all positions goes to 25%? How about 35%? What are the costs of these higher turnover rates, and what are their indirect effects?

### Analysis

This is a sustainability (steady-state reachability) question plus a cost comparison. The model is run for 24 months with a uniform churn rate of 25% (all levels, so middle levels are no longer doubled - the question says 'all positions') and of 35%, keeping the current hiring policy (HR actively hiring 8-10% of positions, filling about 2/3 of vacancies, 70% internal promotion of middle openings with the 30% qualified-candidate gate). 'Sustain 80%' is tested as: does the simulated mean fill rate stay at or above 0.80 over the 2-year horizon (and, in the flow balance, does the replacement inflow at least equal the churn outflow at 80% fill)? The cost comparison is in three parts: direct talent-management budget (Task 3 formulae), lost productivity, and the indirect effects (quality, overload, and the contagion-amplified departures).

### Modeling Process

Flow balance at 80% fill: outflow = 0.25*370*0.80 = 73.5/yr (25% case) or 0.35*370*0.80 = 103.6/yr (35% case). Inflow is capped at about 37 external hires/yr (10% of positions) plus internal promotions (about 2-4/yr at middle levels). So at 25% the gap is about 36 positions/yr and at 35% about 66 positions/yr: the inflow cannot close the gap, so the fill rate must fall. The simulation gives the trajectory: 25%: fill 0.85 -> 0.68 (yr1) -> 0.53 (yr2); 35%: 0.85 -> 0.62 -> 0.44. Cost at each rate, 2-year totals: recruiting 3.1 sigma (both cases - hiring is capacity-capped), training 118.0 / 110.7 sigma, lost productivity 194 / 250 sigma, plus quality falling to 0.96 and the overload drag settling at -3% of output.

### Outcome Analysis

Answer: No at both rates. At 25% uniform churn ICM falls from 85% to about 53% fill over two years; it cannot even reach, let alone sustain, 80% - the replacement system is short by about a third of the required inflow. At 35% it falls to about 44% fill, i.e. more than half the organization's capacity is lost in two years. Costs: the explicit talent-management budget barely moves (about 121 sigma vs 114 sigma over 2 years) because hiring is capped, but lost productivity jumps from 161 sigma (baseline) to 194 sigma (25%) and 250 sigma (35%), a 54% and 96% increase; annualized, the 35% case forfeits about 125 sigma/yr of output - roughly 25% of the full salary bill - to vacancies, quality decay, and overload. Indirect effects: (i) contagion - higher base churn raises exposure, which at gamma=0.10 adds a further 1-2% to departures, a second-order but nonzero amplification; (ii) middle-manager pipeline - even though this scenario equalizes churn across levels, the promotion gate (30% qualified-candidate rate) still throttles internal replacement, so the recovery after any hiring push is slow; (iii) morale feedback - the model's overload and quality terms encode the expert-confirmed pattern that sustained understaffing raises departure risk among the best remaining staff, so the 35% case is unstable in the sense that its own understaffing pushes the realized churn above 35% if the policy is not changed; (iv) the CEO's 18% -> 25% -> 35% ladder is, in model terms, a move from 'elevated' to 'crisis' territory (expert exchange 8), with lost productivity roughly doubling across the ladder. Recommendations implied by the model: raise hiring capacity above the 10%-of-positions cap (the binding constraint at 25%+), relax the experience prerequisites to thicken the qualified internal pool (30% -> 60-80% would nearly double the promotion channel), and protect the middle levels with retention offers, since their double base rate is what makes the middle the first to empty. Limitations: the 80% target is tested over 2 years, not at infinite-horizon steady state (the steady state at these rates is below 80% by the flow balance, so the 2-year answer is the conservative one); hiring capacity is assumed fixed rather than expandable; the uniform-churn assumption means the middle levels' double rate is switched off, which actually makes these scenarios more optimistic than a 'middle stays at double' variant.

### Analysis

## Subtask 6: Task 6: Summarize the potential use of team science and multilayer networks in fulfilling the HR manager's vision of con

### Problem

Task 6: Summarize the potential use of team science and multilayer networks in fulfilling the HR manager's vision of connecting the Human Capital network to other organizational network layers (information flow, trust, influence, friendship), with the HR office taking the lead (references: Salas et al. 2008; Stokols et al. 2008; Kivelä et al. 2013).

### Analysis

This is a synthesis task rather than a computation: it asks how the churn model built in Tasks 1-5 becomes one layer of a multilayer organizational network, and what team science contributes to that architecture. The answer is organized around (a) the multilayer formalism (each organizational relation - reporting, information flow, trust, influence, friendship - is a layer on the same 370 nodes; Kivelä et al. 2013 provide the graph-theoretic machinery: layer-specific adjacency matrices, inter-layer coupling edges, and layer-aggregated measures), (b) the dynamics that only a multilayer view can represent (churn contagion running along the friendship/influence layer while vacancies run along the reporting layer; trust mediating the overload effect; information flow determining how fast a supervisor vacancy is 'felt'), and (c) the team-science contribution (Salas et al. 2008: team performance as a function of coordination, shared mental models, and cross-training; Stokols et al. 2008: team science as the study of how teams are formed, trained, and sustained - directly relevant to ICM's retention and training problems). The HR office's lead role follows from the fact that the reporting layer (the one HR owns) is the coupling backbone: every other layer is defined on the same personnel, and HR controls the transitions (hiring, promotion, reassignment) that change the node set common to all layers.

### Modeling Process

Formal structure: a multiplex M = (V, {A^k}_{k=1..K}) with V = 370 personnel and layers k in {reporting, information, trust, influence, friendship}; the churn model of Tasks 1-5 is the reporting layer's dynamics. Inter-layer coupling: a node's churn hazard becomes h_i(t) = c_i * (1 + gamma_r * e^r_i(t) + gamma_f * e^f_i(t) + gamma_t * (1 - trust_i(t))), i.e. exposure terms are computed on each relevant layer and summed with layer-specific coefficients - the single-layer gamma of the current model is the special case K=1. Information flow: a directed layer whose edge weights are communication frequency; its role in the model is to determine the effective contagion distance (contagion spreads faster along high-bandwidth information paths, so the exposure variable is replaced by a layer-weighted sum over k of the fraction of churned neighbors in layer k). Trust: a node attribute in [0,1] that modulates the overload effect (low-trust teams absorb vacancy overload worse: overload coefficient scales as 1/trust) and the quality-erosion rate. Friendship/influence: an undirected layer that is the primary contagion channel (exchanges 1-2), distinct from the reporting layer. Team science adds the team-level (meso-scale) aggregation: partition V into work teams T_j; team performance = f(cohesion(T_j) on the friendship layer, shared-mental-model proxy on the trust layer, cross-training level from HR training records); churn of a key team member (high betweenness on the team's information subgraph) triggers a team-level productivity dip larger than the single-position dip - a refinement of the vacancy effect. HR's lead role: HR owns the node-set dynamics (entry/exit/transition), so HR maintains the common vertex set and the inter-layer alignment (matching each employee's records across layers); the other offices supply their layer's edge data.

### Outcome Analysis

Potential uses, in order of near-term value: (1) Early churn warning (problem issue 1) - multilayer exposure (churned neighbors weighted by friendship/influence strength, not just count) identifies the high-risk minority before they leave; the current single-layer model already shows the contagion signal, and the multilayer version sharpens it because it distinguishes a churned best friend from a churned occasional coworker. (2) Vacancy impact assessment - the information-flow layer tells which positions, when vacant, degrade the most downstream work (high out-degree in the information layer = high criticality), so HR can prioritize backfilling; the current model's uniform per-level value s_i is the single-layer approximation of this. (3) Promotion pipeline design - the trust and influence layers reveal which internal candidates the organization would actually accept for a promotion (qualified on paper but low trust/influence are bad fits); this directly addresses the qualified-pool bottleneck (the 30% gate) by adding a fit criterion. (4) Training targeting - team science's cross-training measure (how many teammates can cover a role) is a direct input to the training budget: roles with low cross-training coverage are the expensive-to-vacate ones and the best training investments. (5) Culture monitoring - the trust layer's time series is an early indicator of the 'souring culture' problem issue 1 warns about; a falling average trust predicts rising churn with a lag, giving HR a leading indicator. Risks and limits: data availability and privacy (the friendship and trust layers require surveys or communication logs, and employees may misreport); layer alignment over time (hiring/turnover changes the common vertex set, so all layers must be re-synchronized at every transition - this is the operational burden of the HR-led architecture); and the risk of reducing people to edge weights, which team science explicitly warns against by insisting on the team as the unit of analysis. The multilayer model is a natural extension of the current one (it reduces to it when K=1), so the 2-year churn results of Tasks 3-5 are the K=1 baseline against which the multilayer refinements should be judged. The references fit: Kivelä et al. supply the graph formalism and the aggregation theory (how to define reachability, centrality, and communities across layers); Salas et al. supply the team-performance constructs to put on the team meso-scale; Stokols et al. supply the team-science program (team formation, training, sustainment) that matches ICM's HR mandate.

### Analysis

The report's job is to make the model's findings actionable for the HR manager. Its structure follows the seven tasks: (1) data and model, (2) dynamics, (3) budget, (4) sustainability at 25%/35%, (5) the middle-management scenario, (6) team science and multilayer networks, (7) recommendations and limitations. ### Outcome Analysis

(4) The supervisor's feared scenario (30% middle churn, no external recruiting, qualified-only promotion) is the worst case in the analysis: it empties the middle-management spine to 44-58% fill in two years, and the damage is self-reinforcing through overload and quality erosion. (5) The levers, in order of leverage: (a) raise hiring capacity above the 10%-of-positions cap - the single most binding constraint; (b) relax the 'several years at specific levels' promotion prerequisites - the qualified pool nearly doubles (30% -> 60-80%) and the promotion channel becomes adequate; (c) targeted retention of middle managers - their double base rate is the largest single churn term, and retention early in the career (problem issue 1) is cheaper than recovery later; (d) invest training in low-cross-coverage roles - the team-science analysis shows cross-training coverage is the cheapest insurance against vacancy impact.
