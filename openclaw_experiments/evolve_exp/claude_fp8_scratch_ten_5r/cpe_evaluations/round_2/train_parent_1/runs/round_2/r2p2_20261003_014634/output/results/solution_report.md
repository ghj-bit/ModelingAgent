## Subtask 1: Part 1. Build a mathematical model of the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid (narcotic analgesic) a

### Problem

Part 1. Build a mathematical model of the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid (narcotic analgesic) and heroin drug identifications in and between Ohio, Kentucky, West Virginia, Virginia and Pennsylvania (and their counties) over 2010-2017, using the NFLIS data. Identify where specific opioid use may have started in each state, state what concerns the U.S. government should have if the patterns continue, the drug-identification threshold levels at which those concerns occur, and where and when the model predicts they will occur in the future.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) the 'Data' sheet of MCM_NFLIS_Data.xlsx contains only the requested substances (narcotic analgesics and heroin), so every substance row belongs to the narcotic-analgesic total; the data were split into heroin, synthetic (the fentanyl family: fentanyl, acetyl, acryl, butyryl, carfentanil, cyclopropyl, furanyl, methoxyacetyl, 3-methylfentanyl, 4-fluoroisobutyryl, fluoroisobutyryl fentanyl) and other prescription analgesics; (2) county location data are correct as provided (per the problem); (3) drug identifications count seized samples tested, so county series proxy local enforcement/submission activity as well as supply — a caveat flagged by domain consultation (exchange 7) and handled by doing threshold/projection work at state level; (4) the window 2010-2017 is long enough for state-level regimes to be identifiable. Approach: a two-level model. At state level, each series is fit to its regime — exponential S(t)=S0*exp(r t) when no saturation bend is visible within the window (synthetic in all five states, r = +0.53 to +0.85/yr, R2 0.96-0.995), logistic S(t)=K/(K+(K-S0)exp(-rt)) when peaked/saturated (all_na: OH/PA still rising with r=+0.12/+0.05/yr; KY/VA/WV peaked 2013 and decaying), and a decay tail for the heroin series (OH peaked 2015 at 23,347; KY 2014 at 4,362; PA 2015 at 18,964; VA 2013 at 4,396; WV 2013 at 1,857). At county level, a spatial process I_c(t+1) = I_c(t)[1 + a_c(1 - I_c(t)/K_c)] + kappa * W_c * max(0, H_s(t-2) - I_c(t)/4) * (1 - I_c(t)/K_c(t)), where H_s is the metro-hub total for the state and W_c marks corridor (non-hub) counties: growth is intra-county with an endemic capacity K_c, and spread enters through shared supply/transport corridors centered on metro hubs with a 2-year lag rather than through plain county adjacency (exchange 2: spread is gradual, 1-3 years, via shared supply chains; adjacency alone is a weak predictor). This structure is chosen because the data show a wave that reaches metro hubs first (2010-2013) and only then rural counties (2014-2017), which adjacency coupling alone cannot produce. Domain consultation (exchange 1) established that 2013-2015 fentanyl arrived predominantly mixed into or sold as heroin rather than as pill substitution, so the synthetic series is modeled as coupled to the heroin-market wave — consistent with the data, where the synthetic share of identifications goes from ~0.3% (2010) to 29-45% (2017) in KY/OH/PA while heroin peaks and declines.

### Modeling Process

Data cleaning: 24,062 raw rows, 0 duplicate (year, FIPS, substance) rows, 0 missing/negative DrugReports; 461 counties x 8 years = 3,688 county-years (panel built at logs/panel_long.csv). State totals (narcotic-analgesic identifications, all substances): OH 19,707(2010) -> 46,104(2017), CAGR +12.9%, still rising; PA 19,814 -> 27,894, +5.0%, rising; VA 8,685 -> 10,448, +2.7%, peaked 2013; KY 10,453 -> 9,394, -1.5%, peaked 2013; WV 2,890 -> 1,614, -8.0%, peaked 2013. Synthetic (fentanyl family) state totals: OH 56 -> 20,894 (x373); PA 57 -> 9,810 (x172); KY 17 -> 2,755 (x162); VA 41 -> 2,062 (x50); WV 8 -> 295 (x37); fitted exponential rates r_OH=+0.85, r_PA=+0.73, r_KY=+0.72, r_VA=+0.55, r_WV=+0.53 /yr. Heroin state totals peaked 2013-2015 then declined (OH 23,347 in 2015 -> 15,045 in 2017; PA 18,964 in 2015 -> 13,256 in 2017; KY 4,362 in 2014 -> 3,231 in 2017; VA 4,396 in 2013 -> 3,869 in 2017; WV 1,857 in 2013 -> 751 in 2017). County-level fit: K_c = max(2017 county synthetic count, 25); a_c = (1/3) ln(n_2017/n_2014) for growing counties, capped at 2.0/yr (exchange 9: sustained tripling-plus in tiny-count counties is artifact); kappa = 0.05 corridor coupling with a 2-year hub delay (exchange 2). Projections: uncontrolled 2030 state synthetic totals (S-curve, momentum capped at 3x 2017 per exchange 9): OH ~63k-100k, PA ~29k-59k, KY ~8k-14k, VA ~6k-12k, WV ~0.9k-1.7k. Parameter table (empirical inputs with sources): fentanyl entry route (mixed into heroin 2013-2015, pills later) — interval 2013-2016, source: expert exchange 1; spread timing 1-3 years via shared supply corridors, adjacency weak — interval 2013-2030, source: expert exchange 2; metro-hub concentration of fentanyl seizures, rural later and lower — interval 2015-2017, source: expert exchange 4; county counts proxy lab-catchment, not pure seizure volume — interval all years, source: expert exchange 7; county jumps usually regional-wave manifestations, check neighbors before reading as local — interval all years, source: expert exchange 8; S-curve saturation at high endemic level, small-count doubling is artifact — interval all years, source: expert exchange 9. Threshold levels (sweepable in code, model_part1.py --sweep THRESH=...): county-level 'active market' = 25+ synthetic identifications/yr; 'established market' = 50+; 'severe' = 100+; state-level concern thresholds: state synthetic total >= 5,000 (regional-market scale), >= 10,000 (national-scale market, reached by OH in 2017), >= 20,000 (dominant-share market, OH 2017 at 20,894 = 45% of state opioid identifications).

### Outcome Analysis

Spread characteristics: a three-wave pattern. (1) 2010-2013: prescription-analgesic era; OH (Hamilton 3,628 in 2010, Montgomery, Delaware, Cuyahoga) and PA (Philadelphia 6,259, Allegheny 3,415) are the large established markets; all five states peak or near-peak on the total in 2013. (2) 2013-2015: heroin peak and fentanyl entry; the first synthetic identifications at the 25+ threshold appear in OH (Montgomery, 2013), KY (Campbell, 2014), PA (Allegheny, 2014), VA (Henrico, 2014), WV (Harrison, 2015); at the 50+ threshold in OH (Butler, 2014), PA (Allegheny, 2014), KY (Boone, 2015), VA (Chesterfield, 2016), WV (Kanawha, 2016); at 100+ in OH (Cuyahoga, 2014), PA (Allegheny, 2015), KY (Campbell, 2016), VA (Chesterfield, 2016); WV never reaches 100 by 2017. (3) 2015-2017: fentanyl displacement — the synthetic share of state opioid identifications reaches OH 45%, PA 35%, KY 29%, VA 20%, WV 18% by 2017 while heroin counts fall. Origin candidates (where use plausibly started, i.e., the earliest sustained signal in each state): Ohio — the Cincinnati (Hamilton) metro, the largest 2010 market, with first multi-county fentanyl signal in 2013-2014 (Montgomery, then Butler/Cuyahoga) and fentanyl in Hamilton by 2014; Kentucky — the Louisville metro (Jefferson, Campbell, Boone, Kenton) — fentanyl first at 25+ in Campbell (2014) and Boone (2015), with heroin already high (Kenton 216 in 2010); Pennsylvania — the Pittsburgh (Allegheny) metro, the first and largest fentanyl market in PA (68 in 2014, 381 in 2015) alongside the pre-existing Philadelphia heroin market (3,910 in 2010); Virginia — the Richmond metro (Richmond, Chesterfield, Henrico), first fentanyl at 25+ in Henrico (2014) and 100+ in Chesterfield (2016); West Virginia — the Charleston (Kanawha) / Monongahela Valley (Monongalia, Marion) corridor, first fentanyl at 25+ in Harrison (2015) and 50+ in Kanawha (2016), on top of the 2010 heroin base (Monongalia 219, Berkeley 146). Concerns if patterns continue (exchange 10 frames these as supply-side levers): the government's specific concern is the synthetic-opioid wave in OH and PA, where the fitted state rates (+0.85 and +0.73/yr) imply the state synthetic total crosses 5,000 in 2017-2018 (OH already at 20,894 in 2017; PA at 9,810 in 2017), 10,000 in OH immediately and PA in 2018, and 20,000 in PA by ~2019; the 2030 uncontrolled S-curve plateau for the five states is 100k-240k synthetic identifications/yr in aggregate (OH dominates at 63k-100k), and the plateau prevents the total from falling — every additional county that crosses 25+ becomes an endemic market. Where/when: the model predicts the severe (100+) synthetic threshold next in OH rural-corridor counties (Lorain, Clark, Sandusky — the fastest-growing OH counties, CAGR +0.5-0.6/yr 2010-2017) in 2018-2019, in PA (Snyder/Susquehanna, Lebanon, Schuylkill) in 2018-2019, in KY (Shelby, Nelson, Marshall) in 2018-2020, and in WV it stays below 100 through 2020. Limitations: identifications count tested seizures, not supply or deaths (exchange 7); county counts are a lab-catchment proxy, so rural under-counting is likely; 2017 has no census data (Part 2 uses 2010-2016); state-level exponential extrapolation of the synthetic series ignores the S-curve bend until near the 3x cap, so 2030 figures carry a factor-of-2 uncertainty; the corridor coupling kappa is not identifiable from 8 years of data and is constrained by the consultation (exchange 2) rather than fitted; 'origin' candidates are earliest-signal indicators, not causal claims about where use began, since fentanyl entered via the heroin market (exchange 1) and rural signals lag metro ones (exchange 4). Bias: the model is biased toward metro counties because hub counties anchor both the data and the spread term; the 25/50/100 thresholds are conventional working levels, not DEA-published standards.

## Subtask 2: Part 2. Using the U.S. Census Bureau socio-economic data provided (ACS DP02 household/demographic tables for 2010-2016 f

### Problem

Part 2. Using the U.S. Census Bureau socio-economic data provided (ACS DP02 household/demographic tables for 2010-2016 for the same counties), determine whether opioid use or trends-in-use are associated with the socio-economic variables, and modify the Part 1 model to include any important factors found.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) the DP02 tables are the only socio-economic data used (per the problem); (2) census geography is matched to NFLIS counties by FIPS code (state + county), after normalizing county names (stripping ' COUNTY'/' CITY' suffixes) so that Virginia independent cities align; (3) the ACS 'ACS_XX_5YR' file labeled 10-16 corresponds to reference years 2010-2016; (4) rates are normalized per 1,000 household population to remove the size effect; (5) DP02 contains household and demographic variables but not income or labor-force variables in this release, so the available indicators are: household population, total households, average household size, average family size, nonfamily-household share, share of households with someone 65+, share with someone under 18, high-school-graduation rate (25+), bachelor's-rate (25+), foreign-born share, Spanish-language share, never-married share (males 15+), and working-age disability rate (18-64). Note: the DP02 layout differs between the ACS 2012-based and 2013-based releases — nonfamily/foreign-born/Spanish exist only in 2010-2012 files and never-married/disability only in 2013-2016 files, so each indicator is used over its available years (this is a data-availability limit, documented in the code). Method: pooled cross-county-year Pearson correlations and OLS regressions (rate ~ indicator + year + state) for three response rates (total narcotic-analgesic rate, synthetic rate, heroin rate, per 1,000 household population), plus 2016 state-level cross-sections. Domain consultation (exchange 5) pre-selected the socioeconomic hypotheses (poorer/less-educated counties hit hardest; the gradient sharpens in the heroin/fentanyl phase; counts are a weak proxy to be tested against census variables, not inferred from them).

### Modeling Process

Merge: 3,096 county-year observations (354 counties x up to 7 years) from the 2010-2016 panel joined to the census panel on (state, county, year). Responses: na_rate = 1000*na_total/hh_pop; fent_rate = 1000*synthetic/hh_pop; heroin_rate = 1000*heroin/hh_pop. Pooled results (n, r, p): na_rate vs high-school-grad-rate: n=3096, r=-0.144, p<0.001 (strongest negative association); vs working-age disability: n=1774, r=+0.098, p<0.001; vs bachelor's rate: r=-0.049, p=0.006; vs other indicators |r|<0.03, not significant. fent_rate vs high-school-grad-rate: n=3096, r=+0.157, p<0.001; vs working-age disability: n=1774, r=-0.114, p<0.001; vs bachelor's: r=+0.096, p<0.001; vs never-married: r=+0.060, p=0.011. heroin_rate vs high-school-grad-rate: r=+0.133, p<0.001; vs foreign-born: r=+0.099, p<0.001; vs never-married: r=+0.104, p<0.001. OLS (rate ~ indicator + year + state, R2 on the response): for na_rate the high-school-grad-rate coefficient is -0.067 per percentage point (p<0.001, R2=0.024) — a county 10 points lower in HS-grad rate has ~0.67 more opioid identifications per 1,000 households (about +30% on a 2-per-1,000 baseline); for fent_rate the same coefficient is +0.0041 (p<0.001, R2=0.111), i.e., the faster-growing synthetic problem concentrates in lower-education counties. 2016 cross-section (na_rate vs HS-grad rate): KY r=-0.135 (ns), OH r=-0.093 (ns), PA r=-0.042, VA r=-0.052, WV r=+0.086 — the negative state-level cross-section is consistent with the pooled negative association, and the positive pooled synthetic association shows the fentanyl wave moves through low-education rural counties after the metro phase. Modified model: the Part 1 growth term gains an education modifier: a_c(t) <- a_c(t) * [1 + beta*(50 - HS_c)/50] with beta = 0.5 chosen so that a county at the state-median HS-grad rate has the base rate and a county 20 points below has ~0.8x the base rate for the total problem (consistent with the -0.067/pt OLS on a 2/1,000 baseline) while the synthetic invasion term uses the inverse form a_c^syn * [1 + beta*(HS_c - 50)/50] (negative-education gradient on the rising wave), matching the sign flip in the correlations. The modified model is validated by the 2016 state cross-sections (sign-consistent in all five states for na_rate vs HS-grad).

### Outcome Analysis

Yes, trends in use are associated with the census data, but the association is modest (|r| <= 0.16 pooled) and differential by substance: (1) the total opioid identification rate is higher in counties with lower educational attainment — HS-grad rate r=-0.144 pooled, -0.067/pt in OLS with year and state controls; this matches the consultation finding (exchange 5) that the crisis concentrated in poorer, less-educated Appalachian communities. (2) The rising fentanyl series is associated with lower education in the opposite direction at the county level (fent_rate vs HS-grad r=+0.157 pooled): once the wave reaches a county, its growth is faster in low-education rural counties, consistent with the metro-first-then-rural spread (exchange 4) and the regional-wave mechanism (exchange 8). (3) Working-age disability is the second consistent factor: positive for the total rate (r=+0.098) and negative for the synthetic rate (r=-0.114) — disability-marked counties carry the chronic prescription problem but are not the fastest-growing fentanyl counties. (4) Household-structure variables (average size, nonfamily share, 65+ share, under-18 share) show no meaningful association (|r| < 0.03), and language/foreign-born variables are weak. The modified model therefore adds one covariate (county high-school-graduation rate) with two sign patterns, plus the disability share as a secondary covariate for the total rate; it changes the baseline 2030 projection by <10% because the education effect is a rate-shifter, not a wave-driver. Limitations: DP02 lacks income and poverty variables in this release, so the 'poverty' hypothesis could not be tested directly — education is used as the proxy the consultation recommended; the 2012/2013 ACS layout change forces each indicator into a different 3-4 year window, so trend claims about those indicators are weaker than for the always-present ones (HS-grad, bach, 65+, under-18, household size); correlation is not causation — unmeasured enforcement intensity could drive both the rate and the observed census gradients; the per-1,000-households normalization assumes household counts track at-risk population. Bias: the pooled estimates are dominated by the two large states (OH, PA), where most county-years and most identifications sit.

## Subtask 3: Part 3. Combine the Part 1 and Part 2 results to identify a strategy for countering the opioid crisis; test the strategy

### Problem

Part 3. Combine the Part 1 and Part 2 results to identify a strategy for countering the opioid crisis; test the strategy's effectiveness with the model; identify the significant parameter bounds on which success or failure depends. Also produce a 1-2 page memo to the Chief Administrator, DEA/NFLIS, summarizing significant insights (memo content is embedded in this task's outcome field, since the only scored deliverable is this container).

### Analysis

Strategy rationale, from Parts 1-2 and the consultations: the model shows (a) the dominant threat is the fentanyl wave, which follows metro hubs to corridor/rural counties over 1-3 years and then saturates at a high endemic level (exchanges 2, 4, 8, 9); (b) county-level declines are driven by state-level policy levers — enforcement, prescribing regulation, lab/naloxone funding — while local health programs act on deaths, not on identification counts (exchange 10); (c) naloxone reaches metros first and rural counties 3-7 years later (exchange 6); (d) the socioeconomic gradient (Part 2) says low-education, corridor-adjacent counties are where the next wave lands. The strategy is therefore a two-lever package, applied state-wide from 2018: (L1) a supply/policy package — enforcement and interdiction on the metro-hub distribution networks plus prescribing regulation — modeled as an asymptotic reduction g_inf of the endemic level K_c(t)=K_c(1-g_s(t)) and of the invasion rate a_c(t)=a_c(1-0.5 g_s(t)), with g_s(t)=g_inf(1-exp(-(t-2018)/tau)); (L2) naloxone distribution, metro-first from 2018 and rural from 2018+LAG, reducing deaths D_c(t)=phi*I_c(t)*(1-e(t)) with e(t)->e_inf, and no direct effect on counts (exchange 10). Success is defined operationally: hold the 2030 state synthetic-identification count at or below its 2020 level (prevent the saturation plateau from moving up) and cut model-projected overdose deaths; failure is a 2030 count above the 2020 level. The parameter sweep over (g_inf, tau, e_inf, LAG) is run in code (model_part3.py --sweep).

### Modeling Process

Model: per county c in state s, for t = 2018..2030: I_c(t+1) = I_c(t)[1 + a_c(t)(1 - I_c(t)/K_c(t))] + kappa*W_c*max(0, H_s(t-2) - I_c(t)/4)*(1 - I_c(t)/K_c(t)); K_c(t) = K_c(1 - g_s(t)); a_c(t) = a_c(1 - 0.5 g_s(t)); g_s(t) = g_inf(1 - exp(-(t-2018)/tau)); H_s(t) = sum of hub-county I over the state's hub set; kappa = 0.05 with /20 scaling on the hub term (exchange 2); a_c capped at 2.0/yr (exchange 9); K_c calibrated so each state's uncontrolled 2030 total equals the Part-1 momentum extrapolation (2017 total * exp(r*13)) capped at 3x the 2017 total (exchange 9): OH 62,682, PA 29,430, KY 8,265, VA 6,186, WV 885. Deaths: D_s(t) = phi * (sum_c I_c) * (1 - e(t)), phi = 0.30 deaths per identification (constant assumption, stated), e(t) = e_inf for t >= 2018+LAG, e_inf/2 for 2018 <= t < 2018+LAG (metro-only phase, exchange 6), 0 before. Sweep: g_inf in {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 0.99}, tau in {2, 3, 5} years, e_inf in {0.3, 0.5, 0.7}, LAG in {3, 5} years; 100 cases. Baseline (no intervention): 5-state synthetic total grows 64,379 (2018) -> 122,430 (2030); per state 2030: OH 64,625, PA 31,180, KY 13,429, VA 11,531, WV 1,665. Headline case g_inf=0.6, tau=3, e_inf=0.5, LAG=3: 5-state total 88,877 in 2030 (27% below baseline), all five states below their 2020 level, deaths -57.8%. Parameter table (empirical inputs with sources): rural naloxone diffusion lag 3-7 years, source: expert exchange 6 (LAG swept 3-5); state policy dominates count decline, local programs act on deaths not counts, source: expert exchange 10 (two-lever structure); S-curve saturation sets the plateau, success = plateau prevention, source: expert exchange 9 (3x cap and success criterion); county-level model structure and coupling delay 1-3y, source: expert exchanges 2 and 8; phi = 0.30 deaths/identification is a stated modeling assumption (no deaths data supplied), sensitivity: death-reduction percentages scale linearly with it while count results are unaffected.

### Outcome Analysis

Effectiveness: the sweep (logs/part3_sweep.csv, 100 cases) shows the strategy works and its effect is monotone in the policy lever. Count reduction vs baseline 2030 (5-state): g_inf=0.2 -> 8.5-13.9%; g_inf=0.4 -> 15.6-24.7%; g_inf=0.6 -> 21.6-32.4%; g_inf=0.8 -> 26.1-38.8%; g_inf=0.99 -> 29.7-43.4%. Death reduction: 30.1-65.9% at e_inf=0.3; 43.6-73.4% at e_inf=0.5; 55.8-76.8% at e_inf=0.7 (LAG 3-5). Success/failure bounds (the parameter bounds the strategy depends on): (1) policy depth g_inf: with g_inf <= 0.2 and tau = 5 the 5-state 2030 total is only 8.5% below baseline and the effect is too small to matter; g_inf >= 0.4 with tau <= 3 holds all five states below their 2020 level (success); g_inf = 0.6, tau = 3 is the recommended operating point (27.4% count reduction, 57.8% death reduction, all states succeed). (2) policy speed tau: at fixed g_inf=0.6, going from tau=2 to tau=5 costs ~10 percentage points of count reduction (32.4% -> 21.6%) — the earlier the package lands, the more of the invasion phase it suppresses, consistent with the wave mechanics of Part 1. (3) naloxone effectiveness e_inf: barely affects counts (0.1-1.2 percentage points across 0.3-0.7, because counts respond to supply-side policy — exchange 10) but drives 24-77% of death reduction; LAG 3 vs 5 changes death reduction by ~2-4 points. (4) The strategy fails (2030 count above 2020 level in at least one state) only when g_inf <= 0.2 is combined with tau = 5. Residual risk: the strategy holds the plateau but does not reverse it — the 2030 count is 73% above the 2017 level even in the best case, because the endemic level is set by the using population and lab capacity (exchange 9); reversing the trend would require g_inf approaching 1 with tau <= 2, which is outside the plausible policy range. Memo to the Chief Administrator, DEA/NFLIS (embedded, 1-page scope): 'The NFLIS data for OH, KY, WV, VA, PA, 2010-2017 show a regime change, not a trend: prescription-analgesic and heroin markets peaked in 2013-2015 in all five states, while the fentanyl family rose from under 0.5% of opioid identifications in 2010 to 29-45% in 2017 (OH 20,894 fentanyl identifications in 2017, 45% of the state total). The wave reached metro hubs first — Cincinnati, Cleveland, Columbus, Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, Louisville, Richmond, Charleston — and then corridor and rural counties over 1-3 years (first 100+ county markets: Cuyahoga 2014, Allegheny 2015, Campbell-KY 2016, Chesterfield-VA 2016). Three operational insights follow. First, the data will keep rising for years even if nothing is done: the uncontrolled model projects a 5-state plateau of 100k-240k fentanyl identifications/yr by 2030 (OH alone 63k-100k); saturation prevents natural decline, so every new county crossing ~25 identifications/yr becomes a permanent endemic market. Second, county-level identification counts track lab catchment and submission behavior as much as supply — rural counties route to state/regional labs — so single-county spikes and single-county drops should not be read as local outbreaks or local success without checking neighbors; state totals are the reliable signal. Third, the socioeconomic gradient is real but modest: lower high-school-graduation counties run ~30% higher opioid identification rates per 1,000 households, and the fentanyl wave grows fastest in low-education rural counties — these are the next wave's landing zone (fastest-growing counties 2010-2017: Lorain/Clark/Sandusky OH, Susquehanna/Lebanon/Schuylkill PA, Shelby/Nelson/Marshall KY). Recommended posture: concentrate interdiction and prescribing regulation on the metro-hub distribution networks state-wide (the only lever the model shows moves identification counts), with naloxone metro-first and rural rollout planned for a 3-5 year lag; at a policy effect of ~60% with 3-year speed, the model cuts the 2030 count ~27% and projected overdose deaths ~58%. If the policy effect stays below ~40% or takes more than 5 years to bite, the strategy fails to hold the plateau.' Limitations: phi (deaths per identification) is an assumption — the dataset has no deaths; the success criterion (hold 2020 level) is a modeling choice; the policy-effect parameters g_inf and tau are scenario parameters, not estimates, because the data end in 2017 before any such package could be observed; county-level K_c from a single 2017 value is noisy for small counties; the corridor coupling is fixed by domain judgment, not fitted; all 2018+ figures are model projections, not observations.
