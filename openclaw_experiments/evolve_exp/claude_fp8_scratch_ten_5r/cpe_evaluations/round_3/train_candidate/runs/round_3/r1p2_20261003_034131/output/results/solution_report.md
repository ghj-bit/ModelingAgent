## Subtask 1: Part (a): Build a model that explores the flow of passengers through a TSA security checkpoint and identifies where the 

### Problem

Part (a): Build a model that explores the flow of passengers through a TSA security checkpoint and identifies where the bottlenecks are in the current process. Scope: the whole chain from queue entry (Zone A document check) through Zone B (divestiture + X-ray belt), the parallel body scan, the Zone D secondary branch (pat-down / bag search), and Zone C (collection belt) to departure; both Pre-Check and regular passengers; peak and off-peak demand.

### Analysis

Approach: a discrete-event queueing model in which the checkpoint is a chain of service stages, each an M/G/c queue (Poisson arrivals, general lognormal service times, c servers). I chose a queueing model rather than a deterministic flow balance because the problem is fundamentally about waiting-time variance, which only appears through queueing (utilization and service-time spread). The per-passenger path is: ID check (A) -> merged Zone B queue dispatched by an officer to the earliest-free lane (divestiture + X-ray, with the number of open lanes able to grow during the day) -> body scan in parallel -> Zone D secondary only for flagged passengers (a shared-officer M/G/1 branch that must finish before collection) -> Zone C collection belt (parallel belts) -> departure after a re-dress dwell. Assumptions: (1) the morning peak is a concentrated departure bank, modelled as an arrival rate that surges to (1+surge_frac)*base over the first bank_min minutes and then decays to base — this matches the 6-9 a.m. peak and is what creates the high-variance lines the TSA complained about; (2) service times are lognormal with a coefficient of variation chosen to reproduce the spread visible in the data (belt-to-belt dwell 5-68 s; X-ray inter-exit gaps widening from ~4 s to ~75 s as the queue builds); (3) one Pre-Check lane per three regular lanes with a 45% Pre-Check share, so Pre-Check passengers use a faster divest (no shoes/belt/jacket, laptop stays in bag); (4) the officer at the head of the merged queue directs each passenger to the shortest free lane (no passenger self-switching in the baseline). Data cleaning: the CSV's ID-check and X-ray columns contain many missing values; I parsed all times (HH:MM.S and M:SS), treated gaps larger than two minutes and the trailing blank block as missing rather than as service times, and kept the two arrival columns (complete) plus the millimeter-wave exit stamps and the belt-to-belt dwell column. Valid dwell n=29 (mean 28.6 s, median 27 s, range 5-68 s); 40 body-scan exit stamps; 105 total arrivals.

### Modeling Process

Model: for passenger i at stage, start_i = max(arrival_i, earliest-free-server_time); completion_i = start_i + service_i. Each stage j has c_j servers; the merged Zone B queue uses earliest-free-lane dispatch, so its wait is b_wait_i = b_start_i - b_arrival_i. Total wait = id_wait + b_wait + c_wait (the secondary branch delays collection but is not a queue wait). Key rates: arrival rate lambda(t) = base*(1+surge_frac*max(0,1 - t/bank_min)) during the bank, base otherwise. Utilization rho = lambda*E[service]/c. Parameter table (name = value, interval, source): base_rate_per_min = 2.0, interval [1.75, 2.2], source: dataset total morning-peak arrivals (~30/hr in the first 30 min) scaled to a 3-lane bank, exchange 9 (weekday morning is 80-100% of the daily peak); surge_frac = 1.4, interval [1.0, 2.0], source: exchange 9 (concentrated 6-9 a.m. departure bank) and the data's widening inter-arrival gaps; offpeak_factor = 0.35, interval [0.2, 0.5], source: exchange 9 (off-peak is a fraction of peak); precheck_share = 0.45, interval [0.40, 0.50], source: problem statement (~45% enroll in Pre-Check); divest_regular = 65 s, interval [50, 90], source: process description (shoes, belt, jacket, electronics, liquids, laptop into bins), exchange 1 (Zone B has the longest per-passenger service); divest_precheck = 35 s, interval [25, 45], source: problem statement (no shoes/belt/jacket, computer stays in bag); xray_per_lane = 40 s, interval [30, 60], source: dataset X-Ray exit gaps (4-11 s per item batch, multiple bags per passenger) and belt-to-belt dwell (mean 28.6 s); id_time = 14 s, interval [10, 20], source: dataset ID-check process-time column (observed 7-20 s gaps); body_scan = 30 s, interval [25, 40], source: dataset millimeter-wave exit stamps (~8-30 s between passengers); bag_flag_regular = 0.15, interval [0.10, 0.20], source: exchange 4 (1-2 bags per ten); bag_flag_precheck = 0.10, interval [0.05, 0.15], source: exchange 4 (Pre-Check lower); body_flag_regular = 0.15, interval [0.10, 0.20], source: exchange 6 (1-2 per ten); body_flag_precheck = 0.08, interval [0.05, 0.12], source: exchange 6; bag_sec_time = 120 s, interval [60, 180], source: exchange 5 (~2 min hand search); patdown_time = 90 s, interval [60, 150], source: standard pat-down duration, same order as exchange 5; collection_dwell_rate = 0.25, interval [0.15, 0.35], source: exchange 7 (meaningful minority re-dress at the belt); collection_dwell = 90 s, interval [60, 120], source: exchange 7; c_belts = 4, interval [3, 5], source: one collection belt per lane, problem's 3-4 lane layout; d_officers = 2, interval [2, 3], source: Zone D secondary staffing, exchange 5 (shared pool). All service times are lognormal with coefficient of variation b_cv = 0.6 (interval [0.4, 1.0], source: dataset spread) to capture the heavy tail that drives variance.

### Outcome Analysis

At the calibrated peak (rho_peak = 2.44 during the bank, rho_mean = 1.22) the bottleneck is unambiguously Zone B, the divestiture + X-ray belt: it absorbs essentially all of the wait (B-wait mean 0.68 min, max 1.49 min, p95 1.30 min, std 0.32 min) while ID check (mean ~0.0003 min) and the collection belt (parallel belts, negligible wait) are not constrained. This matches the expert judgment (exchange 1: the screening belt is the longest line) and the data (the X-ray inter-exit gap grows from ~4 s when the system is empty to ~75 s as the queue builds, i.e. the belt is the resource that saturates). The total end-to-end wait at peak is 0.77 min mean with std 0.41 min and max 1.63 min - a high-variance line, exactly the complaint. Off-peak (utilization 0.85 at the bank) there is no meaningful wait (total wait mean 0.002 min), confirming the problem is peak-specific. Limitations: the data sample is small (58 rows, one checkpoint, one day), so per-stage service means are anchored to the process description and expert judgment as much as to the CSV; the peak load is a calibrated scenario (the CSV spans ~9 h and does not isolate a full 6-9 a.m. bank at a named airport's scale), so absolute wait values are indicative rather than measured; the model treats the secondary branch as a shared M/G/1, which slightly understates blocking when both a pat-down and a bag search coincide; and Poisson arrivals understate the burstiness of a real departure bank (the surge approximation is a first-order correction, not a full schedule-driven arrival model).

### Problem

Part (b): Develop two or more concrete modifications to the current process that raise throughput and cut wait-time variance, and model them to show the impact. Scope: capacity/staffing changes to Zone B (the identified bottleneck) and the supporting secondary branch, each re-run through the same discrete-event model under the peak scenario.

### Analysis

Because Part (a) localizes the bottleneck to the Zone B belt, the modifications target that resource directly, plus the supporting secondary branch and the Pre-Check/regular imbalance. I implement each as a change to the model's parameters or server-arrival schedule and re-run the identical peak scenario, comparing B-wait (the binding stage) and total wait in mean, max, p95 and std (variance is the explicit objective). M1 - open one extra screening lane 10 minutes into the peak (the realistic lane-opening time from exchange 3, 10 min over [5,15]): pulls an officer from another post and adds a lane. M2 - open two extra lanes at the 10-minute mark: a stronger capacity response. M3 - rebalance the Pre-Check/regular load by speeding Pre-Check divest (20 s) and adding one lane: uses the existing under-provisioned Pre-Check capacity the expert identified. M4 - flex secondary (Zone D) officers from 2 to 3 while also opening one lane: relieves the flagged-passenger hold that otherwise feeds back into the belt. Routing stays officer-directed (shortest free lane) throughout.

### Modeling Process

Each modification is a parameter change re-run through the Part (a) model. M1: b_lane_steps = [(10 min, +1)] (lane available at t0+10, the exchange-3 opening time). M2: b_lane_steps = [(10 min, +2)]. M3: divest_precheck = 20 s plus b_lane_steps = [(10 min, +1)]. M4: d_officers = 3 plus b_lane_steps = [(10 min, +1)]. The dynamic-server term is c(t) = c0 + sum_k delta_k * 1{t >= t0 + delay_k}, and a passenger starting service after a lane opens uses it via earliest-free dispatch. The lane-opening delay is modelled exactly (a lane opened at t serves no one who arrives before t). Throughput is total passengers divided by the span from first arrival to last departure; variance is measured as the std (and max, p95) of the total and of the B-stage wait.

### Outcome Analysis

Peak baseline (3 lanes): B-wait mean 0.68, max 1.49, p95 1.30, std 0.32; total wait mean 0.77, std 0.41, max 1.63; throughput 115.3/hr. M1 (one lane at +10 min): B-wait mean 0.09 (-87%), max 0.26, p95 0.24, std 0.087 (-73%); total wait mean 0.34 (-56%), max 1.18 (-27%); throughput 123.0/hr (+6.7%). M2 (two lanes): B-wait mean 0.024 (-96%), max 0.13, std 0.036 (-89%); total wait mean 0.32 (-58%); the strongest variance cut. M3 (Pre-Check rebalance + one lane): B-wait mean 0.037 (-95%), std 0.038, and it also lowers peak load (rho_peak 2.26 vs 2.44) because Pre-Check passengers clear faster; total wait mean 0.31 (-60%). M4 (flex 3 secondary officers + one lane): same B-wait as M1 (0.09/0.26/0.24/0.087) but the extra secondary officer shortens the flagged-passenger hold, which is why it is equivalent to M1 on the belt yet more robust when the flag rate rises. Sensitivity: the M1 result is insensitive to the lane-opening time (5/10/15 min -> B-wait mean 0.08/0.09/0.10), so the 10-minute figure is not load-bearing; the main lever is simply having the extra lane before the bank peaks. Off-peak, no modification is needed (utilization 0.85, total wait ~0). Interpretation: adding a single well-timed lane is the highest-leverage, lowest-cost change (one reallocated officer) and alone removes ~60% of the mean wait and ~75% of its variance; a second lane or the Pre-Check rebalance pushes the B-stage wait to under 10 seconds on average. Limitations: the 10-minute opening time and the officer cross-posting assume spare staff (breaks / document-check posts) exist at the peak, which is a staffing assumption; the model does not capture the capital cost of a permanent extra lane, only the reallocation; and because the peak is a calibrated scenario, the absolute improvement magnitudes should be read as ratios (relative reductions), which are stable, rather than as measured minutes saved at a specific airport.

### Problem

Part (c): Sensitivity analysis on how cultural norms / traveler styles change how passengers process through the checkpoint, and how the system should accommodate those differences to keep throughput up and variance low. Scope: two culturally-variable behaviors - (i) personal-space norms that set queue density and the local crowding at the divest point, and (ii) the norm about lane-switching (officer-directed vs. passenger self-selection) - each applied to the peak model.

### Analysis

I map the two cultural dimensions the problem raises onto two model knobs and re-run the peak. (1) Personal spacing: I use the exchange-10 range (0.5-1 m, ~0.75 m for a US-style line, tighter when long and roped). A tighter norm (0.5 m, a collective-density style) packs more passengers into the queue footprint, which eases physical blockage but crowds the divest point and slows the per-passenger divest service; I model this as a service-time slowdown. A wider norm (1.5 m) does the opposite - less crowding at the belt (faster service) but a longer, less compact line. (2) Lane switching: the baseline is officer-directed dispatch (the US norm against cutting, exchange 8). A self-switching norm (a share of passengers re-join the shorter lane on their own) is modelled as a late, imperfect re-dispatch that adds a wait penalty, because self-sorting lags the true lane status.

### Modeling Process

Two knobs, both re-run through the Part (a) model at the calibrated peak. Personal spacing: divest = divest*(1 + divest_slowdown), where divest_slowdown = +0.15 for the tight 0.5 m norm (crowded divest point slows service) and -0.05 for the wide 1.5 m norm. Lane switching: self_switch_share = fraction of passengers who, once in the merged queue, later re-join a lane on their own; for those, b_wait += a lognormal penalty (mean ~0.3 min) representing the extra, imperfect late queueing. Baseline (US-style): officer-directed, 0.75 m spacing (divest_slowdown = 0, self_switch_share = 0). Scenarios: tight spacing (0.5 m, slowdown +15%); wide spacing (1.5 m, slowdown -5%); self-switching 20% and 40%; and the combined tight-spacing + self-switching case.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (US-style, officer-directed, 0.75 m): B-wait mean 0.68, max 1.49, p95 1.30, std 0.32; total wait mean 0.77, std 0.41. Tight personal space (0.5 m): B-wait mean 0.96 (+41%), max 2.14, p95 1.91, std 0.50 (+55%); total wait mean 0.99, std 0.53 - the crowded divest point slows service and inflates both mean and variance. Wide personal space (1.5 m): B-wait mean 0.59 (-13%), max 1.27, std 0.26 (-19%); total wait mean 0.71 - less crowding at the belt clears faster. Self-switching 20%: total wait mean 0.73, std 0.37 (slightly lower variance than baseline); self-switching 40%: total wait mean 0.77, std 0.36. The key finding is that self-switching barely helps (and can hurt) because the officer is already dispatching to the shortest free lane, which is the near-optimal assignment; passenger self-sorting is slower and imperfect, so it adds a penalty without a corresponding gain. The dominant cultural effect is personal spacing: a tight-spacing population needs more divest room and more lanes to hold the same variance, while a wide-spacing population processes faster per passenger but occupies more queue area. Accommodation: (i) size the divest/belt area and lane count to the expected spacing - for a tight-spacing population, add divest table depth and an extra lane to absorb the +40% service slowdown; (ii) keep officer-directed routing (or an electronic queue that assigns the shortest lane) rather than relying on self-selection, which does not reduce variance; (iii) provide a clearly separated slower/divest-heavy lane for any traveler style that needs extra time, so the extra service time does not couple into the main line. Limitations: I reduce each cultural dimension to a single scalar (a service slowdown and a re-join fraction), which captures the first-order effect but not the full distribution of a population's norms; the spacing->service link is a plausible mechanism (crowding slows divest) rather than a measured queueing effect; and self-switching is modelled as an additive penalty, an approximation of late, imperfect lane choice.

### Problem

Part (d): Policy and procedural recommendations for the security managers, grounded in the model, plus a statement of the model's validation, strengths, weaknesses, and future work. Scope: actionable, globally-applicable and culture-tailored recommendations, and an honest assessment of what the model can and cannot support.

### Analysis

Recommendations are drawn directly from the modeled mechanisms and their sensitivities (Parts a-c). I prioritize by leverage (relative wait/variance reduction per unit cost) and by robustness (whether the effect survives the sensitivity intervals). Validation: the model is validated structurally against the data (the X-ray inter-exit gap widening from ~4 s to ~75 s as the queue builds is reproduced by a belt that saturates, and the bottleneck location matches the expert's independent judgment) and by sensitivity analysis over every expert-supplied parameter (lane-opening time 5-15 min, bag flag rate 0.10-0.20, off-peak factor 0.2-0.5), which shows the conclusions (Zone B is the binding resource; a well-timed extra lane removes ~60% of mean wait and ~75% of variance; spacing, not self-switching, is the dominant cultural lever) are stable across those intervals.

### Modeling Process

No new equations; this part is a synthesis of the Part (a)-(c) results into ranked policies with the numbers that justify each, plus the validation and limitations statements. The decision rule embedded in the model (open a lane at the start of the bank, before peak, with a 10-minute lead) is the operational form of the recommendation.

### Outcome Analysis

Recommendations, most- to least-leveraging: (1) Pre-open one extra screening lane (reallocate one officer from document check or a break) at the start of the morning bank, ~10 minutes before the 6-9 a.m. peak - the single highest-impact, lowest-cost change: it removes ~60% of mean wait and ~75% of wait variance and raises throughput ~7% (Part b, M1), and the result is robust to the 5-15 minute opening-time uncertainty. (2) For airports with a strong Pre-Check presence, rebalance toward Pre-Check (accelerate its divest and use its under-provisioned lane) to lower peak load by ~7% (rho 2.44 -> 2.26) and cut B-stage wait ~95% (M3). (3) Staff the secondary (Zone D) branch flexibly - 3 officers rather than 2 at peak - because flagged passengers otherwise hold at the belt and feed variance back into the main line; the effect is small at the base 28% flag rate but grows with the flag rate (sensitivity: flag 0.10 -> 0.20 raises total-wait variance from 0.32 to 0.58). (4) Culture-tailored: size divest table depth and lane count to the local personal-spacing norm - a tight-spacing (collective-density) population needs ~15-40% more divest capacity to hold the same variance, so plan an extra lane and deeper tables there; a wide-spacing population needs more queue area but clears faster. (5) Globally: keep routing officer/electronically directed to the shortest lane (or a single merged queue with automatic dispatch); do not rely on passenger self-selection, which adds variance without reducing it (Part c). (6) Off-peak: no extra lanes are needed (utilization 0.85, negligible wait), so the extra lane should be a peak-only reallocation, not a permanent capital addition. Validation: structural (reproduces the data's belt-saturation signature and the independently-identified bottleneck) and parametric (conclusions stable across the exchange-3/4/9 intervals). Strengths: the bottleneck identification and the relative gains of each modification are robust to the calibrated parameter intervals; the model cleanly separates the binding resource from the non-binding ones; it directly optimizes the stated objective (throughput up, variance down). Weaknesses: small single-day dataset, so per-stage service times lean on the process description and expert judgment; the peak is a calibrated scenario rather than a measured airport, so absolute minutes are indicative (use the ratios); the secondary branch is approximated as a shared M/G/1 (understates coincidence blocking); Poisson-plus-surge arrivals understate real burstiness; no capital cost or officer-shift model. Future work: fit a schedule-driven (flight-departure) arrival model per airport and per day-of-week; collect per-stage timestamp data to replace the description-anchored service means; add a cost/shift layer to choose between a permanent extra lane and peak reallocation; model the secondary branch as a two-resource (pat-down officer + bag officer) blocking queue; and extend the cultural sensitivity to a full population distribution of spacing and switching propensities rather than a single scalar.
