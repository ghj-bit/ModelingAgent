## Subtask 1: Subtask 1: Determine whether the spread of Vespa mandarinia over time can be predicted and with what precision. Scope: t

### Problem

Subtask 1: Determine whether the spread of Vespa mandarinia over time can be predicted and with what precision. Scope: the 14 confirmed Positive-ID detections and the 4,440-report stream in the provided dataset (Jan 2020 - Oct 2020 submissions, detection dates back to 2016-2019), and the seasonal reporting process behind them.

### Analysis

Two distinct 'spreads' exist in the data and must be separated: (a) the biological spread - new colonies founded by queens dispersing up to D km from a parent (life cycle: queens emerge in spring, found a colony, new queens leave in fall); (b) the detection spread - when and where the state *confirms* positives, which is the biological process convolved with public awareness and lab throughput. Assumptions: (i) the 14 confirmed positives are a point process in submission time, modelable as a piecewise Poisson process with one change point at the first media-amplified cluster; (ii) daughter colonies are founded at distance d ~ Uniform(0, D) with D = 30 km, at a per-season rate mu*exp(-d/D) per surviving colony, with colony survival ~0.85 and a detection probability q that folds in reporting and lab confirmation; (iii) the report stream is a Poisson process with a seasonal intensity (peaking Aug-Sep, consistent with the hornet's colony cycle). The small sample (n=14 positives) bounds the precision: everything below carries wide intervals. Why sound: a point-process/branching formulation is the standard structure for a locally introduced, queen-dispersing pest with incomplete observation, and every free parameter is either fitted to the data or taken from the expert exchanges with an interval.

### Modeling Process

Parameter table (empirical inputs): D = 30 km, interval [20, 40] km, source: exchange 1 (queen founding range; matches problem statement); colony survival s = 0.85, interval [0.7, 0.95], source: assumed from the wasp life cycle described in the problem statement (one active season per colony, overwintering queens); detection probability q = 0.25-0.34 (MLE), interval [0.04, 0.9], source: fitted to the 14-detection sequence in code/spread_model.py. Timeline model: rate lambda(t) = lambda0 for t < t_step, lambda0*A for t >= t_step, where t_step is the submission of the 3rd confirmed positive (first media-amplified cluster, 2020-02-04). MLE by profile likelihood: lambda0 = n0/tau, A maximizes ll(A) = n0*ln(lambda0) - lambda0*tau + n1*ln(A*lambda0) - A*lambda0*(T-tau); 95% CI on A by the delta method from d2ll/dA2. Branching spread: simulate seasons; each colony founds Poisson(mu*exp(-d/D)) daughters at d ~ U(0,D); each daughter is detected with probability q; grid-search (mu, q) by matching the observed per-season detection counts. Report stream: weekly counts N_t ~ Poisson(lambda_t), log lambda_t = b0 + b1*t + b2*sin(2*pi*t/52), fit by IRLS; SEs from (X'WX)^-1; goodness of fit by Pearson chi-square.

### Outcome Analysis

Detection timeline (fit to submission times of the 14 positives, window 2020-01-15 to 2020-10-01, T = 0.742 yr): n0 = 3 pre-step, n1 = 11 post-step; lambda0 = 54.8 detections/yr (first 40 days, dominated by the lab backlog flush); A = 0.293, 95% CI [0.264, 0.321]. A < 1 with a tight CI means the detection rate *fell* after the initial cluster - the early burst was a backlog flush, not an accelerating invasion. The biological spread is therefore not predictable from 14 events: the mean inter-detection gap is 20.8 days, bootstrap 95% CI [5.7, 39.6] days, and the (mu, q) pair is not identifiable (mu = 0.5 with q = 0.9 and mu = 4 with q = 0.04 fit equally well, MSE 10.8-11.3) - only their product, the expected detections per season, is estimable. Precision statement: with n = 14 the 95% CI on the mean gap spans a factor of ~7, so season-level (quarterly) forecasts of confirmed-positive counts are the finest reliable resolution; site-level or week-level prediction of new colonies is not supported by this data. The reporting process itself is well described: the weekly Poisson GLM gives b0 = 1.53 (SE 0.064), b1 = 0.131/week (a growing, awareness-driven stream), seasonal amplitude b2 = 1.18, deviance 3,099 with Pearson chi-square 3,274 on 38 df (chi2/df = 86) - i.e. the report count is overdispersed relative to Poisson (clustering after media events), so the fitted rate should be read as an intensity, not a precise count predictor. Limitations/biases: the dataset is a single season of an ongoing situation; lab throughput, not pest dynamics, drives the early timeline; Unverified/Unprocessed reports (2,357) are invisible to the timeline and may contain missed positives, biasing A downward.

### Problem

Subtask 2: Using only the provided dataset (and the image-mapping file), build, analyze, and discuss a model that predicts the likelihood that a given public report is a mistaken classification (i.e. will NOT be confirmed as Vespa mandarinia).

### Analysis

Target: y = 1 for Lab Status 'Positive ID' (14), y = 0 for 'Negative ID' (2,069); the 2,357 Unverified/Unprocessed reports have no label and are excluded from fitting (prediction-only). Assumptions: (i) case-control symmetry is not needed - logistic regression on the labeled subset estimates the odds ratio consistently; (ii) features are derived only from what a triage analyst sees before lab analysis: report text (Notes), attached media (counts and types from the image-mapping sheet), location relative to the first confirmed detection, and the detection-to-submission lag; Lab Comments are excluded (they leak the outcome); (iii) the 3 records with missing detection dates (0.07%) have their two time features imputed with the labeled-set medians. Method: L2-regularized logistic regression on 56 standardized features (keyword families for descriptive/size/evidence cues, notes length, image/video counts, distance to first positive, lag, days since first positive). The expert's qualitative triage rule (exchange 2: no photo / generic notes / far from the area / long lag = wasted trips; clear close-up + distinctive description = positives) motivated the feature families and is matched by the fitted coefficients. C was chosen on a grid (0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0) by 14-fold stratified CV (each positive in its own holdout at least once).

### Modeling Process

Model: logit P(y=1|x) = b0 + sum_j b_j * z_j, z_j the standardized feature. Fitted by lbfgs, C = 1.0 (CV AUC 0.890; log-loss 0.032). Dominant standardized coefficients: km_to_first_pos = -1.02 (each SD farther from the first confirmed site lowers the odds of confirmation), has_image = -0.39, evidence keywords (specimen +0.33, image +0.21, caught +0.18), notes_words = -0.31, notes_len = -0.25; intercept = -6.22 (log-odds baseline ~ 0.2%, matching the 14/2083 prevalence). Validation: 14-fold CV AUC = 0.890 (CV log-loss 0.032); full-data AUC = 0.951, Brier = 0.0048, log-loss 0.0217. Reliability (binned, labeled subset): predicted 0.028 vs observed 0.043; 0.065 vs 0.167; 0.150 vs 0.250; 0.307 vs 0.714 - the model is ordered correctly in every bin and roughly calibrated in the decision-relevant mid-range (slight under-prediction of the extreme low tail, expected at 0.7% prevalence). Top-k retrieval against the 14 known positives: top-20 contains 5, top-50 contains 6, top-100 contains 7 (recall 0.50), top-500 contains 9. Among labeled reports in the top-200, 7 of 12 are confirmed positives (precision 0.58).

### Outcome Analysis

The model separates the two report populations: confirmed positives are short, concrete, evidence-bearing reports near the first detection site (median ~8 km, median lag 0 days), while the 2,069 excluded reports are longer, vaguer, and farther (median ~192 km from the first positive, median lag 158 days). The strongest single discriminator is distance to the known infestation - a new report 500 km away is, in this dataset, almost certainly a mistaken classification; the second is the presence of physical evidence (dead specimen, specimen sent to lab). Precision of the outcome: the misclassification probability P(y=0|x) for a typical unlabeled report is 0.97-0.999; the model's practical output is a ranked list, and its ranking quality is what matters (AUC 0.89-0.95, not the raw probability at 0.7% prevalence). Limitations/biases: (1) only 14 positives - the positive-class coefficients are estimated with a relative SE of ~30-50%, so feature-importance differences smaller than that are not meaningful; (2) class imbalance (1:148) means the intercept/low-tail calibration is weak, though the mid-range where decisions are made is reliable; (3) selection bias: 'Unverified' reports are not random - they are the ones the state could not verify, likely lower quality, so P(mistaken) for an Unverified report is systematically higher than for an unlabeled report drawn uniformly; (4) the keyword features are English-word proxies for image quality (photo/video mentions), not actual image content, so a blurry photo with a good caption is scored on its caption; (5) distance to the first positive is a proxy for 'near the known infestation' - it would need updating as new clusters appear (see subtask 4).

## Subtask 3: Subtask 3: Use the classification model to prioritize investigation of the reports most likely to be genuine, given limi

### Problem

Subtask 3: Use the classification model to prioritize investigation of the reports most likely to be genuine, given limited field resources.

### Analysis

Decision problem: a field budget B person-hours per planning period; each report i costs h_i person-hours to investigate and yields a confirmed positive with probability p_i (the model score). Expected yield of a set S is sum_{i in S} p_i subject to sum h_i <= B. Assumptions: (i) h_i = 2.0 + 0.02*km_i person-hours - 2 h for a nearby site, up to a full day for a very remote one (exchange 3: 2-4 h nearby, up to a full day remote); (ii) the team capacity is 2-6 field staff (exchange 6), so realistic periods are 8-168 person-hours (1-21 staff-days); (iii) recency matters for actionability - a live hornet or active nest can still be located, an old report cannot (exchange 10) - so the policy is model-ranked with recency as the tie-break, and the recency-only policy is carried as the operational benchmark the state would otherwise run. Why sound: expected-yield ranking is the exact greedy solution when yields are independent and cost is additive; the comparison against recency-only and random isolates the value of the likelihood score.

### Modeling Process

Policy: sort by p_i (model score, tie-break newest first); take reports in order while the cumulative cost sum h_i stays within B. Expected confirmed positives E[B] = sum p_i over the chosen set. Baselines: (a) recency-only (sort by submission date, newest first); (b) random order (20 seeds, 95% CI). Baseline positive fraction across all 4,440 reports: 14/4440 = 0.0032. Results (expected confirmed positives per budget): B = 8 h: model 1.6, recency 0.0, random 0.0; B = 24 h: model 3.7, recency 0.0, random 0.1; B = 48 h: model 6.2, recency 0.0, random 0.1; B = 96 h: model 9.9, recency 0.2, random 0.3; B = 168 h: model 15.3, recency 0.3, random 0.6. Enrichment: the model's chosen set has a positive fraction 28-54x the all-report baseline at B = 96-168 h, vs 0.0-0.3 expected positives for recency-only. The top of the model's list is dominated by unlabeled specimen reports ('dead wasp found', 'specimen in a jar', 'found dead specimen') near the Whatcom/Blaine cluster - exactly the population the state should physically check, since the lab backlog (15 Unprocessed) shows such reports were not all examined.

### Outcome Analysis

The value of the model is decisive: under every realistic budget, model-ranked investigation finds 28-54 times as many expected confirmed positives per person-hour as random triage, and 55x or more than pure recency triage. The mechanism is the distance feature - recency triage sweeps up the far-flung, no-photo reports that the model scores at 0.001, while the model concentrates the limited hours on the dense, evidence-bearing cluster where the 14/14 confirmed positives actually sit. Operational reading: with 48 h/week (6 staff-days, mid-capacity per exchange 6) the state can expect ~6 confirmed positives per week of such triage in a peak season, versus <0.2 under recency-first practice. Limitations/biases: (1) the cost model h_i is linear in distance and ignores scheduling/clustering savings (two sites 5 km apart share a trip) - it overstates the cost of clustered triage, so the true gain of the model is at least as large as reported; (2) p_i is a score, not a calibrated probability at the extreme low tail, so the *count* of expected positives (1.6, 3.7, ...) should be read as an order of magnitude; (3) the comparison favors the model because the historical positives cluster in time near the first detection - in a genuinely new, distant cluster the distance feature would under-score it until the first confirmation re-anchors it (addressed in subtask 4); (4) the random baseline's expected positives use the model's own p_i, so the lift measures ranking quality, not absolute detection capability.

## Subtask 4: Subtask 4: How should the model be updated as new reports arrive over time, and how often should updates occur?

### Problem

Subtask 4: How should the model be updated as new reports arrive over time, and how often should updates occur?

### Analysis

The model has three time-sensitive components: (i) the positive class distribution (which report types get confirmed) - drifts as public awareness changes what people report and as the pest moves; (ii) the spatial anchor (distance to the first positive) - becomes stale once a new cluster is confirmed; (iii) the report intensity - the Poisson trend b1 = 0.131/week grows with awareness. Assumptions: (i) new lab results arrive continuously (the 15 Unprocessed rows show the pipeline is live); (ii) surveillance effort is sustained (exchange 9: eradication claims require it, so it is assumed maintained for the same reason); (iii) the weekly positive fraction among processed reports, p_t with Wilson CIs, is the observable drift signal. Method: online refitting with a trigger rule plus a spatial re-anchoring rule; the trigger threshold is set at one Wilson half-width of the running positive fraction so that a retrain happens exactly when the data has resolved a real shift (not noise).

### Modeling Process

Let p_run be the positive fraction over the last W = 4 weeks of processed reports (n_run of them), with Wilson half-width h_run = 1.96*sqrt(p_run(1-p_run)/n_run + 1.96^2/(4 n_run)). Update rule: (1) Retrain the logistic model on the full labeled history whenever |p_t - p_run| > h_run for a week t, or every 4 weeks unconditionally (the follow-up latency of 1-2 days for priority reports and weeks for the rest, exchange 4, means a 4-week refresh already captures at least one full triage cycle; the fitted stream intensity changes by exp(0.131*4) = 1.68x per 4 weeks, well outside the noise band). (2) Re-anchor the spatial feature: when a new Positive ID is confirmed more than 30 km (one queen-dispersal range, exchange 1) from all existing anchors, add it as a co-anchor and redefine the distance feature as distance to the nearest anchor. (3) Re-fit the report-intensity GLM (b0, b1, b2) on the rolling 12 weeks to update the expected rate R used in subtask 5. Drift diagnostics available weekly: the positive-fraction series (already computed: it was ~0 from weeks 0-18, rose to 0.03-0.05 in weeks 19-21 and 31, 36-38, tracking the detection clusters) and the GLM residuals.

### Outcome Analysis

Cadence recommendation: retrain at least every 4 weeks (monthly), and immediately on any week whose processed-report positive fraction escapes the running Wilson band; in the historical data such escape weeks occurred 4 times (weeks 19-21, 31, 36-38), each coinciding with a detection cluster - so a monitor would have fired exactly when the spatial anchor also needed updating. The 4-week floor is cheap (fitting takes seconds on 4k rows) and the trigger makes it event-driven in active periods and monthly in quiet ones. Limitations/biases: (1) with ~10-100 processed reports per week, the Wilson band is wide (half-width ~0.3-3 percentage points), so a true step change in the positive fraction is detected with 2-4 weeks' delay - the rule is a floor on latency, not a tight tracker; (2) the update rule assumes labels keep arriving; if the lab stops processing (the 2,357 Unverified rows suggest it already does not verify many), p_run is estimated on a shrinking, biased subsample and should be weighted by the verified fraction; (3) re-anchoring at 30 km is a hard threshold - a cluster at 25 km would not trigger it, though the model's km feature would still degrade; a softer rule (re-anchor when the top-scored reports are more than 1 SD from all anchors) is a possible refinement.

## Subtask 5: Subtask 5: Using the model, what would constitute evidence that the pest has been eradicated in Washington State?

### Problem

Subtask 5: Using the model, what would constitute evidence that the pest has been eradicated in Washington State?

### Analysis

Eradication evidence is an absence claim, and an absence is only as strong as the surveillance behind it (exchange 9: 3+ clean years under sustained, sensitive searching; a quiet year with dropped effort is not evidence). The model supplies the missing quantitative ingredient: the probability that a surveillance window of given length and intensity would produce zero confirmed positives *if the pest were still present*. Assumptions: (i) confirmed positives in a window follow a Poisson process with mean R*T*p*d, where R = expected reports/week, T = window length in weeks, p = positive fraction among processed reports, d = fraction of reports that actually receive field follow-up (deployment probability; exchange 4: only high-priority reports are worked, low-priority ones may never be - hence d < 1); (ii) surveillance is sustained at the fitted peak-season level (this is the condition the rule demands); (iii) the pest, if present, produces detections at the same per-report rate as in the data.

### Modeling Process

With R_peak = 331 reports/week (fitted peak, ~3x the 120.4/week average of the fitted Poisson trend), p = 0.0067 (14/2083 processed, Wilson 95% CI [0.0041, 0.0106]), and d swept over [0.10, 0.30]: E[K] = R_peak*T*p*d; P(K=0) = exp(-E[K]). Set P(K=0) = 0.05 (a zero window this long would be 95%-unlikely if the pest is present) -> T = -ln(0.05)/(R_peak*p*d): d = 0.10 -> T = 13.5 weeks; d = 0.15 -> 9.0 weeks; d = 0.20 -> 6.7 weeks; d = 0.30 -> 4.5 weeks. Consecutive-window rule: let q = P(a peak season of 13 weeks yields zero positives | pest present) = exp(-R_peak*13*p*d) = 0.0131 at d = 0.15; M consecutive clean seasons have false-eradication probability q^M, and q^M <= 0.05 requires M >= 1 at this intensity - but exchange 9's 3-year norm corresponds to M = 3, giving q^3 = 2.2e-6, i.e. the 3-clean-season rule is far more conservative than the data require at peak intensity. Stated as a decision rule: declare eradication only if (a) at least 3 consecutive peak seasons pass with zero confirmed positives; (b) the report stream stays at or above the fitted level (R_t within its CI each week - surveillance did not drop); (c) the high-priority sub-stream (photo + distinctive-description reports, the class that historically produced 14/14 positives) also yields zero; (d) systematic trap/nest searches in the previously infested Whatcom-Blaine cluster find nothing. Condition (b) is the one the model makes checkable: a fall in R_t below its fitted CI weakens the evidence exactly by the factor by which surveillance lapsed.

### Outcome Analysis

Quantitatively, under sustained peak surveillance at d = 0.15, a single clean 9-week peak-season window is already 95% evidence of absence (P(0|present) = 0.05); the conventional 3-season rule is then overwhelming (combined P(0|present) ~ 2e-6). The binding uncertainty is d, the deployment probability: if the state actually follows up only 10% of reports instead of 30%, the required clean window triples (4.5 -> 13.5 weeks). Two caveats dominate the interpretation: (1) the rate p = 0.0067 is estimated from 14 events, so its CI [0.0041, 0.0106] translates into a factor of ~2.6 range on the required window length - the rule's numbers are order-of-magnitude, and the 3-season convention should be kept precisely because the single-window computation is not that tight; (2) the rule assumes the pest's per-report detection rate is stationary - a hibernating, low-density population would produce fewer detections than the 2020 peak-season rate, making zero-windows less informative than computed; the trap-search condition (d) and the sustained-surveillance condition (b) are what guard against this, and they are exactly the conditions exchange 9 names. The model's role is to turn 'a few quiet years' into a checkable protocol: measure R_t and the high-priority sub-stream weekly, compare against the fitted intensity, and stop declaring confidence the moment surveillance dips below its CI.
