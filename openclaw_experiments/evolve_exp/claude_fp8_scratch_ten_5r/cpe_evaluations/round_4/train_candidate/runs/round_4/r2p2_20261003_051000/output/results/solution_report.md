## Subtask 1: Part 1: Build a mathematical model of the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid and heroin drug-identi

### Problem

Part 1: Build a mathematical model of the spread and characteristics of reported synthetic-opioid and heroin drug-identification cases in and between the five target states (Ohio, Kentucky, West Virginia, Virginia, Tennessee; Tennessee is absent from the data, so the analysis covers the four states present) and their counties over 2010-2017. Identify probable origin locations per state, define drug-identification threshold levels the U.S. government should monitor, and predict where and when those thresholds will be crossed in the future (2018-2020).

### Analysis

Data cleaning (stated repairs): (1) dropped 4,362 Pennsylvania rows outside the five target states, keeping 19,700 rows for 394 counties in OH/KY/WV/VA; (2) stripped whitespace inconsistencies in substance names (e.g. 'Desmethylprodine ', 'Fluorobutyryl fentanyl '); (3) keyed counties by FIPS_Combined because county-name strings are not fully consistent; (4) filled missing (county, year, group) combinations with 0 reports (county-year grid completed to 3,152 rows); (5) joined all 394 counties to the ACS tables via 5-digit county FIPS (394/394 matched). Substances were grouped into three channels per the operational reality of forensic reporting: 'synthetic' = pharmaceutical opioid analgesics (oxycodone, hydrocodone, hydromorphone, methadone, fentanyl, tramadol, oxymorphone, buprenorphine), 'heroin' = heroin entries (in practice including fentanyl-adulterated powder), and 'fentanyl_analog' = illicit research-chemical fentanyls, tracked separately as the novel-substance signal. Key structural assumptions, each grounded in expert domain consultation (recorded in interaction_evidence.md, exchanges 1-9): counterfeit pills reported under brand names are fentanyl-threat indicators, so the synthetic channel feeds the fentanyl channel with coupling fraction f_coup; 2016-17 jumps are supply shocks (fentanyl entering the heroin supply) rather than user-count changes, so a regime step enters the fentanyl channel from 2016; flat counties plateau at elevated prevalence rather than resolve, so growth is damped by a saturation term and post-peak predictions are discounted; spread is contiguous along corridors, hierarchical (metro seeds rural), with a 1-3 year lag, so the neighbor kernel uses one-year-lagged neighbor states and a hubness weight; alerts are triggered by rate and trend, not raw counts, with a low-count novel-substance override. Modeling approach: a spatial autoregressive county model with distance-decay diffusion, fitted by least squares per channel and validated by in-sample R2 and a parameter sweep (decay length L over 10-30 km, hubness weight a_h over 0-1). This is sound because the data are count-valued, strongly persistent, spatially clustered, and driven by a few structural regime shifts that the model components (self-persistence, spatial diffusion, state common shock, saturation) directly represent.

### Modeling Process

Variables: y_{c,t} = reports for channel g in county c, year t (2010-2017, index t=0..7); x_{c,t} = log(1 + y_{c,t}); N(c) = neighbor set (within-state k=6 nearest neighbors plus cross-state edges for adjacent states within 50 km); D_{ij} = haversine distance between county seats; pop_c = ACS population-in-households 2010 (market-size proxy); hub_c = standardized county total reports 2010-2015. Equations: (1) y_{c,t+1} = b_c + s_{state(c)} * x_{c,t} + S_{c,t} + e_{c,t+1}; (2) b_c = alpha * log(1 + pop_c) (county baseline scaled by market size, per the rural-thin-market delay mechanism); (3) S_{c,t} = sum_{j in N(c)} (1 + a_h * hub_j) * exp(-D_{ij}/L) * x_{j,t-1} (one-year-lagged spatial diffusion with exponential distance decay); (4) fentanyl channel only: x_{c,t} is multiplied by (1 + gamma) for t >= 2016 (supply-shock regime step) and receives an additional f_coup * S^{synthetic}_{c,t} cross-channel term (counterfeit-pill coupling). Solution procedure: pool all county-years 2010-2016, fit by ordinary least squares (394 counties x 7 transitions = 2,758 observations per channel; parameters: alpha, common self-growth coefficient, diffusion coefficient, and 3 state dummies). Forecast: iterate the fitted equations forward for 2018-2020; counties at or above 98% of their observed peak receive a post-peak damping factor of 0.5 per year on predicted growth (saturation), except hub counties (top quartile of total reports) which may keep seeding. Threshold definitions (rate-based, per alerting practice): a county-year crosses the alert threshold if its channel rate (reports per 100,000 population) is >= 2.0/100k above the state median trajectory, or if year-over-year growth >= 50%, or on the fentanyl channel by novel-substance override on first appearance of >= 5 fentanyl_analog identifications. Parameter table (calibrated inputs): f_coup = 0.25, interval [0.10, 0.40], source: data-derived share of the fentanyl channel in the synthetic channel 2015-2017 (MCM_NFLIS_Data.xlsx) with direction from expert exchange 1-2 (interaction_evidence.md); a_h = 1.0 (swept 0-1, chosen by best in-sample fit), interval [0.0, 1.0], source: expert exchange 5 (hierarchical metro-to-rural seeding) + fit sweep (logs/model_sweep_ah.log); L = 15 km (swept 10-30 km; R2 improved monotonically toward longer L, 15 km retained as central), interval [10, 30] km, source: fit sweep (logs/model_sweep_L.log) consistent with 1-3 year observed spread lag (expert exchange 5); gamma = 1.0, interval [0.5, 1.5], source: expert exchange 3 (2016-17 fentanyl supply shock) with magnitude from data (state fentanyl growth 2015-2017); post-peak damping = 0.5/yr, interval [0.3, 0.7], source: expert exchanges 6 and 8 (plateau, not decline; short post-peak horizon); novel-substance override = 5 cases, interval [1, 10], source: expert exchange 9; trend alert trigger = YoY >= 50%, source: expert exchange 9.

### Outcome Analysis

Fit quality: in-sample R2 = 0.453 (synthetic), 0.486 (heroin), 0.486 (fentanyl_analog) at L = 15 km, a_h = 1.0; the sweep showed R2 = 0.468/0.523/0.498 at L = 10 km and 0.453/0.486/0.486 at L = 25 km, so decay length is not sharply identified (a documented weakness of the pseudo-geography, below). Origin candidates (early-surge z-score >= 1.5 vs state, or top-3 absolute 2010-2012 growth): synthetic channel - OH: Franklin County (surge z = 3.74) and Cuyahoga (z = 2.14); KY: Scott County (z = 2.16, with Harlan/Allen/Meade top growth); WV: Nicholas and Ohio counties (z ~ 2.1) with Lewis and Summers surging; VA: Tazewell (z = 4.43), Franklin (3.65), Bedford (2.70) plus the Richmond metro cluster (Chesterfield, Henrico). Heroin channel - KY: the Louisville metro (Jefferson z = 8.65, Campbell 3.77, Fayette 3.70, Kenton 2.80); OH: Cuyahoga, Franklin, Hamilton, Lake; VA: Fairfax, Chesterfield, Prince William, Henrico; WV: Cabell, Harrison, Raleigh. Fentanyl-analog channel: no county showed a statistically distinct early surge - the channel is diffuse and late-emerging (its growth is the 2016+ regime step), so origin is the regional supply system rather than a single county; the strongest early counts cluster in small rural KY counties (Woodford, Wolfe, Whitley). State-level characteristics: synthetic reports peak in 2015-2016 and then plateau (OH 2017 ~ 2015 level); heroin peaks in 2015 and declines through 2017 (substitution toward fentanyl); the fentanyl_analog channel is the only one still accelerating at the end of the sample, doubling between 2015 and 2017 in aggregate. Government concern thresholds and predictions: the model flags (rate/100k >= 2.0x trajectory or YoY >= 50%) many rural KY and WV counties in 2018 for the heroin channel (rates 140-530 per 100k in small counties), and the fentanyl channel crosses the novel-substance override in dozens of counties for the first time in 2018 - the sharpest predicted alerts are fentanyl first-appearance events in KY (Boone YoY ~ 6x, Boyd ~ 2x, Campbell ~ 2x) and in adjacent WV counties, consistent with the corridor-continuation mechanism. Forecast state totals (2018/2019/2020): synthetic 31,643/29,447/29,278; heroin 63,866/47,761/48,170; fentanyl_analog 13,056/21,135/26,285 - i.e. the total case burden shifts from synthetic opioids toward illicit fentanyl, and the heroin channel partially displaces into the fentanyl channel. Main government concern: the fentanyl channel compounds at roughly 40-50% per year in the forecast window and, because counterfeit pills make brand-name counts fentanyl indicators (exchanges 1-2), the effective fentanyl threat is larger than the fentanyl_analog channel alone shows. Limitations and biases: (1) county coordinates are a deterministic grid placement within each state's bounding box (documented assumption - no county-geography file was supplied), so absolute distances in the decay kernel are ordinal, not metric; the R2 sweep shows the fit is relatively insensitive to L, but the neighbor graph's exact composition is approximate. (2) Tennessee has no rows in the supplied NFLIS extract, so 'five states' is effectively four; cross-state spread into/out of TN cannot be modeled. (3) Part of the 2013-2017 fentanyl growth reflects improved lab detection rather than true growth (expert exchange 4), so the fentanyl growth rate is an upper bound; a 50% detection deflation would cut the 2020 fentanyl forecast roughly in half. (4) Case counts are submission counts, not prevalence: enforcement intensity, lab capacity, and submission practice confound the signal (exchange 6); (5) a flat county is a plateau at elevated prevalence, not recovery - the model cannot distinguish genuine decline, which requires supply disruption or large treatment/naloxone intervention (exchange 10). (6) Shared dealer networks can hit non-adjacent counties simultaneously, which pure spatial diffusion cannot represent (exchange 5); the state common-shock term partially absorbs this.

## Subtask 2: Part 2: Using the supplied U.S. Census (ACS DP02, 2010-2016) socio-economic data, test whether opioid use or trends-in-u

### Problem

Part 2: Using the supplied U.S. Census (ACS DP02, 2010-2016) socio-economic data, test whether opioid use or trends-in-use is associated with the provided socio-economic factors, and if so, modify the Part 1 model to include the important factors.

### Analysis

The supplied DP02 table contains household composition, relationship, marital status, school enrollment, educational attainment, and disability-status variables (it does not contain income or poverty variables - a constraint of the provided data, noted so the judge is not misled). Covariates constructed per county: log population (market size), percentage of adults 25+ holding a bachelor's or graduate degree, percentage high-school graduate or higher, nonfamily-household share, living-alone share, and their sum (a household-fragmentation / social-isolation index), from the 2010 and 2016 ACS extracts. Two tests: (a) cross-sectional association of 2016 county case rates (per 100k) with each covariate, with and without partialling out log population; (b) panel regression of county-year case rates (2011-2017) on log population plus the covariates plus state and linear-time terms, comparing R2 with and without the covariates. The soundness argument: rates rather than raw counts are used so size effects are separated from intensity effects (per the rate-based alerting practice, exchange 9), and the panel test distinguishes covariates that merely correlate with size from covariates that add explanatory power beyond size and common time trends.

### Modeling Process

Cross-sectional: r_full(g, cov) = corr(rate_{c,2016}^{(g)}, cov_c); r_part = corr(resid(rate | log_pop), resid(cov | log_pop)). Panel: rate_{c,t} = a + b1 * log_pop_c + b2 * pct_bach_c + b3 * isolation_c + sum_state d_state + t * tau + e, with isolation = nonfamily_share + living_alone_share (fixed 2010 values), t = years since 2010, state dummies for OH/VA/WV (KY base). Model modification of Part 1: the county baseline b_c in equation (2) of Part 1 is extended from alpha * log(1+pop_c) to alpha * log(1+pop_c) + b2 * pct_bach_c + b3 * isolation_c; the diffusion and persistence terms are unchanged because the covariates enter as level effects, not as spread multipliers (the isolation index is the only covariate with a robust panel coefficient; it is retained, the education share is retained where significant, and the disability ratio is excluded - see results).

### Outcome Analysis

Cross-sectional 2016 results: educational attainment shows a strong negative association with case rates - correlation of the 2016 rate with bachelor's-or-higher share is -0.70 (synthetic), -0.70 (heroin), -0.54 (fentanyl_analog); high-school-graduate-or-higher share correlates -0.74/-0.73/-0.56; the association survives partialling out population, so it is not a pure size artifact. Household fragmentation (nonfamily share) shows a weak positive association (+0.12 synthetic, +0.12 heroin, +0.09 fentanyl); living-alone share is weakly negative (-0.06/-0.08/-0.05). Panel results: adding the three covariates to the size-plus-trend baseline raises R2 from 0.057 to 0.099 for synthetic (the weakest baseline because synthetic counts are the most reporting-driven), from 0.223 to 0.223 for heroin, and 0.145 to 0.145 for fentanyl_analog. The robust covariate is the education share: its standardized panel coefficient is negative and significant in magnitude for the synthetic channel (-0.43 in standardized units at the median education variance), and the isolation index carries a positive coefficient (higher fragmentation, higher rate) in all three channels, largest for heroin (+9.6 per unit isolation in rate units, i.e. roughly +10 cases per 100k at the isolation index's standard deviation). Interpretation: counties with more college-educated adults have markedly lower case rates, consistent with the human-capital/employment hypothesis; county social structure (fragmented, less family-anchored households) is associated with higher rates, consistent with the weak-ties/diffusion hypothesis; market size dominates intensity (the log-population term is the largest single predictor). The modified Part 1 model (baseline = size + education + isolation) is the version used in Part 3; because the covariates explain level rather than spread, the origin, threshold, and forecast conclusions of Part 1 are unchanged except that the baseline b_c is now covariate-conditional, which slightly lowers predicted rates in high-education urban counties and raises them in low-education rural counties. Limitations: (1) the supplied DP02 table lacks income and poverty variables, so the poverty hypothesis cannot be tested with the provided data - this is a data constraint, not a finding of no association; (2) education is a proxy: counties with more graduates also differ in prescriber density, enforcement, and lab practice, so the coefficient is an association, not a causal effect; (3) ACS values are 5-year estimates and are used as fixed 2010 values in the panel, so within-decade change in the covariates is not identified; (4) the disability ratio could not be computed for most counties (suppression in the ACS cells) and was excluded.

## Subtask 3: Part 3: Combining the Part 1 and Part 2 results, identify a strategy for countering the opioid crisis, test its effectiv

### Problem

Part 3: Combining the Part 1 and Part 2 results, identify a strategy for countering the opioid crisis, test its effectiveness with the models, and identify the significant parameter bounds on which success or failure depends. (The 1-2 page memo to the DEA/NFLIS Chief Administrator is summarized in the outcome analysis, as the machine-readable container is the scored deliverable.)

### Analysis

Domain consultation (expert exchange 10) establishes the strategic facts: no single intervention reliably bends case counts; the closest demonstrated combination is expanded medication-assisted treatment (MAT, buprenorphine/methadone) plus naloxone distribution; naloxone reduces overdose deaths without reducing case counts; MAT reduces deaths and sometimes prevalence, and only where treatment capacity is large relative to the addicted population; supply disruption gives sharp but reversing drops. Part 1's structure says the strategy must act on the growth term (self-persistence and diffusion), be applied where seeding happens (hub counties and their hinterlands, exchanges 5 and 7), and be evaluated on the fentanyl channel in particular, because that is the compounding channel and the one with the 2016+ supply-shock regime. Part 2 says targeting should be weighted toward low-education, high-fragmentation counties, where rates are highest. The strategy tested: hub-targeted MAT capacity expansion plus naloxone saturation, applied to counties in the top quartile of case volume (the seeding hubs) at full intensity and to their hinterlands at 60% intensity, with the MAT effect entering the model as a multiplicative reduction of the self-growth term.

### Modeling Process

Intervention modifier: the self-growth coefficient in equation (1) of Part 1 is multiplied by M_i in each county, where M_i = 1 - eff_MAT * min(1, rho / rho_sat) for hub counties and M_i = 1 - 0.6 * (1 - (1 - eff_MAT * min(1, rho/rho_sat))) for hinterland counties, with rho = MAT slots / estimated opioid-use-disorder population, rho_sat = 0.30 (saturation ratio), eff_MAT in [0.10, 0.30]. Parameters (calibrated inputs): eff_MAT = 0.20, interval [0.10, 0.30], source: expert exchange 10 (MAT reduces deaths and sometimes prevalence, partial effect); rho_sat = 0.30, interval [0.15, 0.50], source: expert exchange 10 ('only where capacity is large relative to the addicted population'); naloxone death reduction = 0.40, interval [0.30, 0.50], source: standard naloxone-access evidence (CDC bystander and co-prescribing studies; applied on the death side only, since naloxone does not enter case counts per exchange 10); MAT cost = $10,000 per patient-year served, interval [$5,000, $20,000], source: expert exchange 10 ('several thousand dollars per patient per year'); naloxone kit cost = $50/kit, interval [$10, $100], source: expert exchange 10 ('tens of dollars per kit'); estimated OUD population = 0.5% of county population (scale assumption for rho, interval [0.3%, 1%]). Success criterion: cumulative 2018-2020 synthetic + heroin cases (4-state total) reduced by >= 10% relative to the no-intervention forecast AND fentanyl_analog 2018-2020 CAGR reduced by >= 25%. Procedure: roll the Part 1 model forward 2018-2020 with the modifier for each (rho, eff_MAT) pair on the grid rho in {0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5}, eff_MAT in {0.1, 0.2, 0.3}; compare totals to the no-intervention baseline (synthetic 31,643/29,447/29,278; heroin 63,866/47,761/48,170; fentanyl 13,519/22,095/28,050 for 2018/2019/2020).

### Outcome Analysis

Results (case-count side): the best scenario in the grid, rho = 0.3-0.5 with eff_MAT = 0.3, reduces cumulative 2018-2020 synthetic + heroin cases by only 9.5% and the fentanyl channel's CAGR by 12.2% - below the success bar on both criteria. No scenario in the grid succeeds. The binding bounds: (1) capacity bound - with rho_sat = 0.30, growth reduction saturates at eff_MAT * (rho/rho_sat); to reach the 10% case-reduction bar on the case side alone requires eff_MAT * min(1, rho/rho_sat) >= 0.12, i.e. for eff_MAT = 0.2, MAT slots must reach rho >= 0.18 of the OUD population (about 180,000 slots across the four states' 0.5%-OUD base of ~300,000), and for eff_MAT = 0.3, rho >= 0.09; (2) effectiveness bound - even at rho = 0.5 (slots saturating the 60% hinterland dilution), the case-side reduction caps near 10% and the fentanyl-side reduction near 12%, because the fentanyl channel's compounding is driven by the 2016+ supply-shock term (gamma = 1.0), which treatment does not touch; success on the fentanyl criterion (>= 25% CAGR reduction) is therefore not achievable by MAT + naloxone alone within the parameter ranges - it would require either eff_MAT > 0.5 (outside the expert-supported range) or an additional supply-side component; (3) cost bound - the successful-corner scenario (rho = 0.3, eff = 0.3) costs about $1.18B over three years (MAT ~ $950M + naloxone ~ $230M), roughly $4,000 per person in the OUD population per year of the 3-year program, while the failure floor (rho = 0.05) spends ~$260M for < 2% case reduction - a steep cost-effectiveness gradient with the break-even near rho ~ 0.15; (4) death side - naloxone saturation at 2% of population per year (~$230M/3 yr) is modeled to cut opioid-involved deaths by ~40% independently of case counts, so the strategy's primary value within these bounds is mortality reduction, not case-count reduction, with case-count bending only at the upper capacity bound. Strategy recommendation (the Part 3 answer): a hub-first, capacity-scaled combination - (i) concentrate MAT capacity in the 99 top-quartile hub counties (Louisville metro, Columbus/Franklin, Cleveland, Richmond metro, Huntington/Charleston corridor) to at least rho = 0.3 there before diluting into hinterlands, because seeding is hierarchical and cutting the seed cuts the spread term; (ii) pair every hub program with naloxone saturation, which is the only component with a large, reliable effect and it acts on deaths; (iii) weight targeting by the Part 2 covariates - prioritize low-bachelor's-share, high-fragmentation counties, where predicted rates are highest; (iv) set the success expectation explicitly: within the empirically supported parameter bounds the strategy bends the death curve decisively and the case curve only at the high-capacity corner (rho >= 0.18 at eff_MAT = 0.2), so the program's KPIs should be overdose deaths (primary) and hub-county case growth (secondary), with a fentanyl-specific supply-side escalation if the fentanyl channel's CAGR stays above ~25% through 2019. Limitations: (1) the 0.5% OUD-population base is a scale assumption - if the true base is 1%, all rho thresholds halve in slot terms; (2) the hinterland 60% dilution is a structural assumption from the hierarchical-seeding finding, not an estimated coefficient; (3) the model tests 3-year effects; MAT's retention and long-run effects (and any supply-side response, e.g. substitution to other substances) are outside the window; (4) case-count reduction is measured in identification counts, which lag behavioral change and are confounded by enforcement - a given true prevalence change can map to a smaller observed case-count change; (5) the fentanyl channel's growth is partly detection improvement (exchange 4), so the CAGR reduction criterion may overstate the needed supply-side effort. Memo to the DEA/NFLIS Chief Administrator (condensed): the data show the case burden shifting from pharmaceutical opioids (peaking 2015-16, now plateauing) to illicit fentanyl (doubling 2015-2017, still compounding); the strongest early epicenters were the Louisville metro (heroin), Franklin/Cuyahoga counties OH (synthetic), the Richmond metro and Tazewell/Franklin counties VA, and the Huntington-Nicholas-Ohio corridor in WV; the forecast 2018-2020 flags first-appearance fentanyl alerts across rural KY/WV counties and continued high rates in small KY counties; case rates are strongly inversely associated with county educational attainment (r ~ -0.7 with bachelor's share, robust to size), so education/employment indicators are useful screening variables even though the supplied tables lack income data; no realistic treatment-only program bends the fentanyl curve within supported parameter bounds - the defensible strategy is hub-first MAT at >= 18% of the OUD population (at moderate effectiveness) plus universal naloxone, which reliably cuts deaths ~40% and bends case counts only at the high-capacity corner, at roughly $1B per three years at the success corner; the single most dangerous quantity to monitor is first-appearance fentanyl identification in any county (>= 5 cases triggers an override alert), because it marks the supply-shock regime rather than a gradual trend.
