# Interaction Evidence

## Exchange 1
- **Question** (expert_question_1.md): When a new opioid first appears in one area, does it spread by crossing into nearby neighboring counties, or jump to distant cities?
- **Reply (gist)**: Both occur at different scales and differ by drug. Heroin / prescription opioids: predominantly local, contiguous diffusion across adjacent counties and transport corridors — county adjacency is a reasonable first-order structure. Fentanyl / synthetics: more jump-like — high-potency, low-bulk commodities move through distribution hubs and can appear in distant metros near-simultaneously, seeded by a single supplier/shipment. Practical implication: adjacency-only diffusion understates synthetic spread; distance-decay-only understates local clustering; a gravity-type kernel (local adjacency plus distance-decay weighted by county population/market size) captures both.
- **Effect on work**: Adopted for the Part 1 spatial model. Infection/pressure from county j to i decomposes into (a) a contiguous term, active only when counties i and j share a border, and (b) a long-range term with distance decay exp(-d_ij / L) weighted by the source county's market size (population). Fentanyl-family cases receive a strong long-range weight w_jump; heroin and other narcotics receive a weak one (w_jump ~ 0), with the local term dominating. Distance-decay scale L is set so that ~90% of long-range pressure comes from within a few hundred km (L ~ 100 km), consistent with the "distribution hubs / a few hundred mile corridor" description; L is swept 50–300 km in model validation. Population weights for the market-size term come from the ACS household/estimate data (county totals). Interval of validity: 2010–2017, five-state region (KY, OH, PA, VA, WV).

## Exchange 2
- **Question** (expert_question_2.md): After a town's opioid problem peaks, does the problem fade by itself, or does it stay high for many years?
- **Reply (gist)**: Stays high for many years; behaves like a persistent, self-sustaining condition, not a single epidemic wave that burns out. Once county counts rise they plateau at an elevated level and decline only slowly over 2010-2017. Reasons structural: established supply networks, stock of dependent users, continued inflow of new users. What changes is composition (prescription-opioid identifications fall while heroin then fentanyl rise), keeping the aggregate burden elevated. Sharp declines come from specific disruptions (major supply interdiction, market shift, overdose-death spike removing users), not natural decay. Do not assume a symmetric rise-and-fall curve; use a saturating plateau with slow decay, and allow substance substitution to keep the aggregate elevated.
- **Effect on work**: Per-substance model for county c and substance class s at year t becomes a saturating, slow-decay dynamic rather than a symmetric epidemic curve. Case level X_s,c,t follows dX/dt = recruitment (local growth + gravity spread from other counties) - delta * (X - K_s,c), where K_s,c is a county-substance capacity (plateau) and delta a small annual decay rate; equilibrium is a plateau at K, so a peak decays only slowly toward the sustained level. Substance substitution is modeled by letting the total burden T_c,t = sum_s X_s,c,t stay elevated as dominant substance shifts (fentanyl recruitment partly draws from the opioid-addicted stock established by earlier heroin/prescription use). Delta kept small (annual decay <~10%) so plateaus persist over the 8-year window; disruption-driven drops are handled in Part 3 as exogenous shocks. Interval of validity: 2010-2017 observation window; used for short-horizon (1-3 yr) forecasting.

## Exchange 3
- **Question** (expert_question_3.md): Before fentanyl cases spike in a state, which kind of place sees the first fentanyl cases — big cities, small towns, or areas already hit by heroin?
- **Reply (gist)**: Areas already hit by heroin see the first fentanyl cases — fentanyl enters through existing opioid supply and user networks. Earliest fentanyl identifications appear in counties with substantial prior heroin (and often prescription-opioid) activity: those markets have the distribution channels, dealer networks and user base that fentanyl displaces/supplements. Big cities often show up early too, but because they are also heroin hubs/distribution centers — city size itself is not the driver. Small towns with little prior opioid activity lag. Best single predictor of early fentanyl appearance = prior heroin burden; metropolitan distribution status is secondary.
- **Effect on work**: Fentanyl recruitment in county c at year t is made proportional to the established opioid-burden stock in c: R_fent,c,t = a * H_c,t-1 + b * N_c,t-1 + (local growth + gravity spread from other counties), where H = prior-year heroin cases, N = prior-year other-narcotics (prescription) cases, a > b (heroin is the dominant entry channel), and the spread terms use the gravity kernel from exchange 1. This also makes the origin inference for Part 1 concrete: the county whose prior-year heroin burden was highest (adjusted for size) is the most likely fentanyl entry point per state. City size enters only through H (hubs are heroin hubs), not as an independent driver. Interval of validity: 2010-2017, five-state region.

## Exchange 4
- **Question** (expert_question_4.md): In rural counties, are police the main source of drug evidence sent to crime labs, or do hospitals and medical examiners contribute a lot?
- **Reply (gist)**: Police are the dominant source. NFLIS submissions come overwhelmingly from law enforcement submitting evidence seized in criminal investigations; hospitals/medical examiners contribute little to drug-identification counts of this kind (their toxicology/overdose-death specimens flow into other surveillance systems). Counts measure enforcement activity and seizure volume, not true prevalence. In rural counties with fewer police resources, counts can understate actual opioid presence, and a rise in counts may partly reflect increased enforcement attention rather than a real increase in use.
- **Effect on work**: Interpretation constraint applied everywhere NFLIS counts are read as "prevalence": county-year DrugReports are a detection-adjusted signal, not use itself. Concretely: (i) Part 1 spread/origin inferences use relative changes and cross-county comparisons within the same enforcement channel, and any stated "prevalence" is qualified as identification intensity; (ii) per-county capacity K_s,c (exchange 2) is scaled by county size (population from ACS) rather than assumed per-capita, so a small rural county's absolute counts are not over-read; (iii) the fentanyl rise 2013-2017 is interpreted partly as growing enforcement/laboratory attention to fentanyl (new substance, increased testing), not purely as a use spike; (iv) a limitation is recorded that count growth can reflect enforcement attention (detection shift). Interval of validity: entire analysis window.

## Exchange 5
- **Question** (expert_question_5.md): For a state fighting the opioid crisis, which single action usually works best — better prescription tracking, naloxone, or treatment access?
- **Reply (gist)**: No single one works best; they are complements acting on different parts. Prescription tracking (PDMPs) reduces initiation/diversion (new prescription supply) but is partial and can push users toward heroin/fentanyl if treatment is unavailable. Naloxone reduces deaths, not use; saturation blunts mortality without shrinking the epidemic. Treatment access, especially medication-assisted treatment (MAT: methadone/buprenorphine), is the only lever that reduces the stock of dependent users and sustains recovery — largest effect on long-run prevalence, most cost-effective. If forced to pick one for durable impact: treatment/MAT, because it addresses persistence; but its effectiveness depends on the other two being present (tracking limits new supply, naloxone keeps people alive long enough to reach treatment).
- **Effect on work**: Part 3 strategy = combined bundle with treatment/MAT as the primary lever: (1) MAT expansion reduces the dependent-user stock, modeled as an increase in the per-capita recovery/removal rate r_mat applied to X_s,c,t (i.e., adds r_mat * X to the decay term, and lowers the effective plateau K for untreated dynamics); (2) PDMP/tracking reduces new prescription-opioid initiation, modeled as a multiplicative reduction (1 - theta_p) on the recruitment of the "other narcotics" class (theta_p in 0-1); (3) naloxone is modeled as a mortality-avertment lever that does NOT enter the case-count dynamics (consistent with exchange 4's distinction between use and deaths) — it is reported separately as lives-saved at fixed prevalence, using a naloxone-reversal share of overdose deaths. Success bounds identified in Part 3: treatment must reach a critical share of the dependent stock (r_mat above the natural recruitment rate for the epidemic to shrink), and PDMP must not be deployed without treatment (exchange 5's diversion warning: if theta_p applied without r_mat, fentanyl recruitment R_fent = a*H + b*N is not reduced and may rise as N falls). Interval of validity: short-to-medium horizon strategy test on 2018-2020.

## Exchange 6
- **Question** (expert_question_6.md): Do areas with more job loss and poverty tend to have worse opioid problems than wealthier, stable areas?
- **Reply (gist)**: Yes, but the relationship is real, moderate, and bidirectional — not a clean one-way cause. Counties with higher poverty, lower median income, higher unemployment, weaker labor-force attachment tend to show higher opioid burden (deaths, treatment admissions, often identification counts); the association is consistent but explains only a modest share of variation. Wealthier, stable counties are not immune; some affluent suburban counties have high counts. Cautions: (1) reverse causality — addiction destroys employment and drives poverty, so job loss is partly a consequence; treating socioeconomic variables as purely exogenous overstates their causal role; (2) confounding — poverty and opioid burden both correlate with rurality, deindustrialization, education, region. Include socioeconomic factors as associated covariates with moderate effect sizes; do not interpret as strong causal drivers.
- **Effect on work**: Part 2 modifies the Part 1 model by adding ACS socioeconomic covariates to the county capacity/plateau term K_s,c (exchange 2) and to the baseline initiation rate: K_s,c = K0 * (population_c)^(beta0) * exp(g1*z(poverty) + g2*z(unemployment) + g3*z(median_income, negative) + g4*z(labor_force_participation)), with standardized (z-scored within 5-state pool) covariates. Coefficients g_j are estimated by fitting to the data and are expected to be small/moderate per exchange 6; the model reports R^2 / partial-correlation magnitudes and explicitly labels the socioeconomic terms as associated (correlational) covariates, not causal drivers, with the reverse-causality and confounding caveats (rurality, deindustrialization, education) stated as limitations. Interval of validity: ACS 5-year estimates, 2010-2016 vintages (no 2017 ACS per problem statement).

## Exchange 7
- **Question** (expert_question_7.md): When fentanyl arrives in a town, do overdose deaths usually jump quickly or climb gradually over a couple of years?
- **Reply (gist)**: Jump quickly — within months, not years. Fentanyl's lethality comes from potency and users' inability to judge dose (a user accustomed to heroin takes a fentanyl-adulterated dose and dies in the same event); the mortality signal is close to a step change tied to when supply actually switches over. Counties with a sharp rise in fentanyl identifications typically show a sharp, roughly coincident rise in overdose deaths, often within the same year, sometimes within a quarter. The "gradual over a couple of years" pattern is more characteristic of the earlier heroin/prescription-opioid phases (tolerance and dose escalation build up over time). Qualifications: reported death data lag identification data by months (toxicology, death investigation), so in a model the death response may appear delayed even though the underlying event is abrupt; jump size depends on how much of the local supply is replaced (partial substitution = smaller, still rapid, step).
- **Effect on work**: Implied death-rate model (Part 1 concerns + Part 3 naloxone test): county-level overdose-death level D_c,t = kappa * X_fent,c,t with a short reporting lag of ~0.5 yr (D responds to X_fent from the previous half-year, i.e., D_c,t = kappa * 0.5*(X_fent,c,t-1 + X_fent,c,t)), plus a baseline mortality from non-fentanyl opioids that evolves gradually (consistent with the slow, tolerance-building phase for heroin/prescription opioids). For threshold/concern analysis in Part 1: the concern threshold is framed in fentanyl identification count per county (the jump trigger) and the death response is near-annual, so "when they will occur" predictions are made at annual resolution with the caveat that the underlying step can be intra-year. Naloxone (exchange 5) multiplies the fentanyl-driven death term by (1 - rho_n), rho_n = effective fraction averted; this is the parameter bound tested in Part 3. Interval of validity: 2013-2017 fentanyl era, five-state region; short-horizon forecasting.

## Exchange 8
- **Question** (expert_question_8.md): When a county hits a crisis level, do officials usually react within the same year, or does help typically lag one or more years?
- **Reply (gist)**: Help typically lags one or more years — rarely within the same year. Sources of lag: (1) identification counts and overdose data are reported with delay (toxicology, death investigation, annual compilation), so officials see the crisis level well after it occurs; (2) budget/program cycles are annual or biennial, so response is usually funded in the following cycle; (3) declaring an emergency, standing up a program, or expanding treatment capacity takes additional time. Naloxone distribution and PDMP changes move faster than treatment expansion, but even those typically take months to a year to scale. Treat the policy/response term as a delayed feedback: roughly 1-2 years between crossing a threshold and any measurable effect.
- **Effect on work**: Policy levers in Part 3 enter with explicit response lags. If a county crosses crisis threshold theta_crisis (defined as fentanyl identification count or total-opioid burden above a state percentile) in year t, the countermeasure effect begins in year t + lag, lag ~ 1-2 yr (lag = 1 yr for naloxone/PDMP-type actions, lag = 2 yr for treatment-capacity expansion, both within the stated 1-2 yr band). The Part 3 strategy test therefore evaluates "react at the threshold with a 1-2 yr delayed response" against a "no reaction" baseline, and the significant parameter bound includes lag: if lag exceeds the time over which fentanyl cases double (estimated from the 2014-2017 growth, ~1.5-2 yr doubling), the delayed response arrives after the peak and cannot prevent the jump, only the plateau height. Data-reporting lag (exchange 4, 7) compounds this: detection of the threshold is itself delayed ~0.5-1 yr, so effective threshold-crossing-to-effect time is ~1.5-3 yr. Interval of validity: 2013-2017 and short-horizon 2018-2020 strategy test.

## Exchange 9
- **Question** (expert_question_9.md): In small rural counties, is there usually enough local medical capacity to treat many opioid addicts, or do patients have to travel elsewhere?
- **Reply (gist)**: Local capacity is usually not sufficient in small rural counties — patients typically travel elsewhere (commonly an hour or more, sometimes across state lines) for MAT. Rural counties have far fewer physicians, and especially far fewer buprenorphine-waivered prescribers and methadone clinics per capita; methadone clinics are scarce, concentrated in metro areas (daily dosing + regulatory/siting barriers), many rural counties have none. Outpatient counseling, psychiatry, inpatient detox beds similarly thin. Travel burden is itself a major reason rural patients drop out of treatment. Capacity is unevenly distributed: a rural county adjacent to a metro may be reasonably served; an isolated one is effectively unserved. Treat rural treatment capacity as a binding constraint that scales with population density and proximity to a metro, not a fixed per-capita resource.
- **Effect on work**: The Part 3 treatment lever r_mat (exchange 5) is made county-dependent: r_mat,c = r_max * A_c, where A_c is an accessibility factor in [0,1] that increases with county population density and decreases with distance to the nearest metro (A_c = 1 for metro-adjacent counties, low for isolated rural counties; computed from ACS population/density and distance to the largest county seat within the state). This creates a significant parameter bound: in isolated rural counties, even fully funded treatment cannot reach a critical share of the dependent stock because A_c is small — the strategy test shows r_mat,c < recruitment rate there, so the epidemic continues to grow despite the policy; the county-level outcome depends on accessibility, not just funding. Travel burden also raises the effective dropout: the recovery rate is multiplied by a retention factor that is lower in rural counties. This bound (accessibility threshold) is reported as the main condition under which the Part 3 strategy fails. Interval of validity: 2010-2016 ACS geography; 2018-2020 strategy test.

## Exchange 10
- **Question** (expert_question_10.md): If a state set a fentanyl alarm level, what county number of fentanyl identifications per year would make officials sound the alarm?
- **Reply (gist)**: No single defensible raw number; thresholds should be set relative to county size and baseline. Absolute floor: a handful (~5-10/yr) is already meaningful in a small county (fentanyl rarely appears in isolation; each identification implies many unreported uses). Practical alarm range: ~20-50 fentanyl identifications/yr in a typical county = established local problem, not isolated seizure. Large metro counties: raw counts run into hundreds/thousands, so a fixed number is useless; use a rate (per 100k population) or a growth trigger. Better than a fixed count: alarm on rate of change — e.g., fentanyl identifications doubling within a year, or fentanyl exceeding ~10-20% of total opioid identifications. Catches the step-change dynamic and is comparable across counties.
- **Effect on work**: Part 1 concern thresholds, defined per county c in year t, are a disjunction of three triggers: (T1) absolute floor — X_fent,c,t >= 10 (small-county meaningful-presence floor, expert floor 5-10); (T2) established-local-problem — X_fent,c,t >= 20 AND >= 20/100k * (population_c / 100k) is not used raw; instead T2 = X_fent,c,t >= max(20, 0.20 * (pop_c/100k) * (X_total,c,t / X_total_ref)) simplified to X_fent,c,t >= 20 for non-metro and rate-based for metro; (T3) growth trigger — X_fent,c,t >= 2 * X_fent,c,t-1 (doubling within a year) OR share s_c,t = X_fent,c,t / X_total,c,t >= 0.10. A county is flagged "concern" when T1 or T2 or T3 holds; "alarm/crisis" when T3 holds with T1 or T2 (i.e., rapid growth on an already-established base). These thresholds feed the Part 3 policy-lag trigger (exchange 8): policy response begins lag years after the first flagged year. Metro vs non-metro split uses ACS population (metro county = population > 500k, roughly the largest handful). Interval of validity: 2013-2017 calibration; applied to 2018-2020 projection.
