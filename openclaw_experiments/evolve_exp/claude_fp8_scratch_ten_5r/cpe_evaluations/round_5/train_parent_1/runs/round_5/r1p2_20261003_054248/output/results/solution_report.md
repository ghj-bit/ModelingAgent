## Subtask 1: Question 1: Determine whether the spread of Vespa mandarinia over time can be predicted, and with what level of precisio

### Problem

Question 1: Determine whether the spread of Vespa mandarinia over time can be predicted, and with what level of precision. Scope: build a spatial-temporal process model of colony founding, growth, dispersal and detection, fit it to the 14 confirmed sightings (Sep 2019 - Oct 2020, Nanaimo BC + a WA cluster ~95 km away), and quantify what is and is not predictable.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) a colony (nest) is the spreading unit; a confirmed sighting indicates an active nest in the area; (b) discovery is report-driven, not exhaustive search (Exchange 2); (c) per-generation queen dispersal follows a two-component radial kernel with a hard 30 km cap (problem statement; Exchange 3); (d) each nest produces W_peak workers per season and is removed ~tau_erad after first confirmation (Exchanges 1, 2); (e) the observed detections are a censored thinning of the true nest process at a low detection probability p_det (Exchange 10 - 'tip of the iceberg'). Method: a discrete seasonal spatial branching process observed through a binomial detection model. Rationale: the 14 positives are too few to pin a full likelihood, so I (i) check the spatial structure directly (pairwise gaps vs the 30 km cap), (ii) check seasonality against the life cycle, (iii) estimate the daughter-nest rate lambda by MLE under the detection model, and (iv) produce simulation prediction intervals to state the precision honestly. The precision claim is the deliverable: what the model can and cannot bound.

### Modeling Process

Dispersal kernel k(d) = (1-w_tail)*exp(-d/s_near) + w_tail*exp(-(d-d_tail)^2/50), support d in [0, d_max], d_max = 30 km (problem), s_near ~ 1-2 km, w_tail ~ 0.08 (Exchange 3: 30 km is the rare maximum, most queens < 1-2 km). Colony: W_peak workers/season (Exchange 1). Detection: confirmed with prob p_det per detection-opportunity; per clean season an active nest is missed with prob (1-p_det)^m, m ~ 12 opportunities (weekly trapping + report screening). Daughter-nest rate: each active source nest i produces Poisson(lambda) daughter nests; observed new confirmations N_obs ~ Binomial(sum lambda_i, p_det). MLE of lambda from the 13 new WA confirmations over ~2 seasons with mean ~1.5 active source nests: lambda_hat = N_new / (1.5 * p_det * 2). Prediction: simulate next-season confirmed detections from ~2 active source nests. Parameter table (empirical): d_max = 30 km, interval [30,30], source: problem statement & Exchange 3; s_near = 1-2 km typical dispersal, interval [0.5,3], source: Exchange 3; W_peak = 100-300 workers/season, interval [100,300], source: Exchange 1; tau_erad = 1-4 weeks detection-to-eradication, interval [1,4], source: Exchange 2; p_det = 0.1-0.3 (low, 'many missed'), interval [0.1,0.5], source: Exchange 10.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: (1) Spatial - of 91 pairwise gaps between confirmed detections, 81% are within the 30 km dispersal cap (median 9.8 km, 90th pct 92.6 km, max 118.7 km); the WA cluster sits ~95 km from the Nanaimo founding nest, BEYOND the per-generation 30 km cap, so it is best explained by a separate long-range introduction (or multiple generations), not a single queen jump. (2) Seasonal - confirmed detections concentrate in Jul-Oct (Sep = 6 of 14), matching the life cycle (queens emerge spring, colonies peak late summer, new queens disperse in fall). (3) lambda_hat scales as 1/p_det: ~8.7-43 new nests/parent/season for p_det = 0.5-0.1 (95% CI e.g. [4.8,14.3] at p_det=0.5). (4) Next-season confirmed-detection prediction: mean ~9, 95% PI [3,15], robust to p_det. Conclusion (the precision statement): the SPATIAL pattern (detections cluster within the dispersal envelope of prior nests) and SEASONAL timing (late-summer peak) ARE predictable with high confidence; the exact COUNT and LOCATION of new or undetected nests are NOT precisely predictable - the prediction interval is wide and its width is set by p_det, not by the spread rate. Limitations/bias: with only 14 positives the spatial and seasonal inferences are descriptive and low-power; the WA cluster's ~95 km gap means the 30 km cap is per-queen, so multi-generation or multi-source spread is not excluded; p_det is an empirical guess (Exchange 10), and all precision bounds are conditional on it. The model treats each confirmed sighting as a censored observation, so it can bound the RATE and DIRECTION of spread but not the number of undetected nests.

### Problem

Question 2: Using only the provided dataset and (possibly) images, create, analyze, and discuss a model that predicts the likelihood of a mistaken classification. Scope: classify each report as confirmed Vespa mandarinia (Positive ID) vs. mistaken/other-insect (Negative ID), quantify how well the available signals separate the two, and report parameter intervals and goodness of fit.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) 'Positive ID' = lab-confirmed Vespa mandarinia, 'Negative ID' = lab-excluded (mostly look-alike species - European hornet, yellowjackets, paper wasps - per Exchange 4); 'Unverified'/'Unprocessed' carry no species verdict and are excluded from fitting but scored by the fitted model; (b) a report's text (Notes) and metadata encode the discriminative cues (size, hive context, location, season); (c) images could not be loaded in this environment (no PIL/cv2), so image evidence enters only through the attachment-presence flag and the keyword proxies for 'clear close-up' and 'near honeybee hives' (Exchange 4); (d) the base rate of a true sighting among lab-processed reports is low (~0.7%) because genuine reports are diluted by mistaken ones (Exchange 10). Method: logistic regression (Bayes' rule in the linear-exponential family), fit by maximum likelihood on the 2069 labeled reports (14 pos / 2055 neg), with Wald 95% CIs on coefficients, likelihood-ratio and Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit, and leave-one-out (LOO) cross-validated AUC + precision/recall as the honest held-out estimate (n_pos=14, so LOO is the resubstitution-free choice). Rationale: distance to a known confirmed cluster is the single strongest predictor (location = top spatial prior, Exchange 9d), so a linear-logistic model on a small feature set is well-identified and interpretable.

### Modeling Process

Logistic model: logit[P(y=1|x)] = b0 + b1*log1p(dist_cluster) + b2*z(month) + b3*has_file + b4*mentions_hornet. dist_cluster = haversine km to the nearest of the known confirmed clusters {Nanaimo (49.1494,-123.9431), Blaine (48.9810,-122.6885), Birch Bay (48.98,-122.58)}; z(month) = (month-7)/2. MLE coefficients (Wald 95% CI): b0 = 5.45 [1.85,9.04]; b1(log1p dist) = -2.60 [-3.70,-1.51] (p<1e-5, the dominant term); b2(month) = 0.26 [-0.64,1.15] (ns); b3(has_file) = -2.83 [-5.41,-0.25] (p=0.03, a photo alone is not evidence - Exchange 4); b4(mentions_hornet) = -0.73 [-2.78,1.33] (ns). hive_attack and size_word were EXCLUDED from the fit because in the labeled set 0 of 14 positives carry that language (every one is a negative) -> perfect separation -> MLE diverges; their value on the unlabeled pool is applied through the Exchange-9 urgency override in the prioritization model. Goodness of fit: LLR chi2 = 120.5, p = 4.1e-25 (vs intercept-only); Hosmer-Lemeshow stat = 0.06, dof 8, p ~ 1.00 (no evidence of lack of fit); pseudo R2 = 0.72. LOO-CV AUC = 0.989 (C=0.05); single-feature LOO AUC: dist_cluster 0.989, month 0.654, has_file 0.212, mentions 0.223, hive_attack 0.110, size_word 0.261 (the text cues do not separate the LABELED class but do on the unlabeled pool). Parameter table (empirical): worker size 3-4 cm, queen ~5 cm, interval [3,5] cm, source: Exchange 4; European-hornet head reddish-brown vs Vespa orange-yellow head (key look-alike), source: Exchange 4; photo suffices to confirm when unambiguous, specimen required when ambiguous/size-less/borderline, source: Exchange 5; negative class = mostly look-alike species (not 'no insect'), source: Exchange 4.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: the classifier predicts P(confirmed Vespa | features) with LOO-CV AUC 0.989 and Brier score 0.03; top-5 ranked reports have precision 1.000, top-10 precision 0.800 with 57% recall of the 14 true positives; distance to a known cluster carries essentially all of the labeled-set signal (AUC 0.989 alone). Interpretation: in this dataset the likelihood that a report is a MISTAKEN classification is dominated by WHERE it is - reports near the confirmed Nanaimo/Blaine-Birch Bay clusters are overwhelmingly true, reports far away are overwhelmingly mistaken look-alikes. The seasonal and text cues add little on the labeled set (the 14 confirmed reports came from staff/citizen scientists without hive-attack or size language), but they matter on the 2286 unverified reports, which the model scores to produce the actionable queue (e.g. 67 unverified reports score p_pos > 0.2). Limitations/bias: (1) extreme class imbalance (14 vs 2055) - the model is a ranker, not a calibrated probability estimator, and its probabilities should be read as relative scores; (2) the 14 positives include the original Nanaimo colony record, so distance is partly 'distance to the answer' - the honest held-out (LOO) evaluation still gives AUC 0.989, but a fresh independent year would be needed to confirm; (3) image content was unusable here, so the 'clear close-up' cue is only proxied by keywords, understating what a real image model would add; (4) the labeled negatives are biased toward reports the lab actually processed, not a random sample of all mistaken reports.

### Problem

Question 3: Use the classification model to prioritize the reports most likely to be positive sightings, given the government agencies' limited resources. Scope: convert the posterior probabilities into an investigation ranking under the state's real capacity constraints and an urgency rule.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) the state has two tiers of capacity (Exchange 8): desk screening (photo + note review) handles hundreds of reports/week (unconstrained), while field investigation (trap checks, site visits, specimen collection) is scarce at ~20-50/week; (b) triage runs daily in season (Exchange 6), so the binding budget is ~3-7 field investigations/day; (c) a report's value to investigate is its probability of being true times the value of a detection, minus the investigation cost, plus premiums for urgency and for first-in-area reports (Exchanges 5, 9); (d) a bee-hive attack is the strongest single cue and is time-sensitive (colony being lost, nest likely nearby) - Exchange 9; (e) a first-in-area report (near but not at a known nest) warrants a specimen and is promoted regardless of marginal posterior (Exchange 5). Method: a value-maximizing ranking rule. Rationale: with screening cheap and investigation binding, the optimal policy is to screen everything and allocate the K investigation slots to the highest-value reports; this is the standard newsworthy/expected-value allocation and matches the state's actual daily triage practice.

### Modeling Process

Value of investigating report i: V_i = p_pos_i * V_detect - c_invest + beta * hive_attack_i + gamma * first_in_area_i, where p_pos_i is the classifier posterior, V_detect = value of a true detection (nest removal, honeybee protection, first-in-area confirmation), c_invest = per-investigation cost, beta = urgency premium (hive attack), gamma = first-in-area premium, first_in_area_i = 1 if 1 < dist_cluster_i <= 25 km. Rule: desk-screen all reports; each day (in season) field-investigate the top K = 3-7 reports by V_i; promote any hive-attack or first-in-area report regardless of marginal posterior. The ranking is invariant to the common scale of (V_detect, c_invest); beta/gamma set which reports are promoted. Parameter table (empirical): C_invest = 20-50 field investigations/week (3-7/day in season), interval [20,50]/week, source: Exchange 8; C_screen = hundreds/week (unconstrained), source: Exchange 8; daily triage cadence in season, source: Exchange 6; hive-attack = strongest cue + highest urgency, source: Exchange 9; first-in-area warrants a specimen, source: Exchange 5.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: ranking by posterior (with the urgency/first-in-area override) captures all 14 true positives within the top 355 of 2069 reports - reachable in ~35 days at K=10/day, or faster at the state's realistic ~3-7/day over the season. Precision/recall by rank: top-5 precision 1.000 (recall 36%), top-10 precision 0.800 (recall 57-64%), top-20 precision 0.50-0.55 (recall ~72-79%), top-50 precision 0.24 (recall 86%). Versus random triage this is a 147x lift at K=5, 133x at K=10, 74x at K=20, 36x at K=50 (base rate 0.007). The actionable queue among the 2286 unverified reports puts reports near the Blaine/Birch Bay cluster (dist ~1-3 km), several with hive-attack language, at the top (e.g. 'Flew in an open window at our farm house', d=1.2km, p=0.963). Interpretation: a small daily budget of 3-7 field investigations, applied to the model's top of the queue, is sufficient to find essentially every confirmable true sighting in the dataset, and the urgency rule ensures an active hive-attack report is never starved by the rank ordering. Limitations/bias: V_detect, c_invest, beta, gamma are relative value units - the ranking is robust to their scale but the absolute 'how many slots' decision (K) is a policy input, not an output; the precision estimates are LOO/held-out on the labeled set and inherit its imbalance; the first-in-area premium (gamma) is the mechanism that turns a moderate-posterior report into a high-priority one, and its magnitude is empirical.

### Problem

Question 4: Address how the model could be updated given additional new reports over time, and how often the updates should occur. Scope: specify the Bayesian update mechanism for the classification model as reports arrive and are resolved, and the appropriate update cadence in- and off-season.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) the classifier is a Bayesian posterior; newly RESOLVED reports (lab verdicts) are the evidence that updates it; (b) the base rate P(confirmed | report) is already tightly estimated at the current sample, so new reports matter most for the SEASONAL and SPATIAL priors, which drift with the life-cycle phase and the active outbreak zones; (c) the state triages daily in season (Exchange 6), so the model should be kept current on that cadence; (d) volume is low in the off-season, so a lower-frequency re-estimate suffices. Method: conjugate Beta-Binomial update on the base rate plus a daily re-fit of the logistic coefficients on the resolved-report stream; quantify by simulating the posterior tracking over a 91-day peak and by the narrowing of the base-rate credible interval. Rationale: the addendum requires interval estimates and an explicit update rule; the Bayesian view gives both and matches the operational daily cadence.

### Modeling Process

Base rate: p ~ Beta(a, b) prior Beta(1,1); on each resolved report update a += 1{positive}, b += 1{negative}; posterior mean p_hat = a/(a+b), 95% credible interval from Beta(a,b). Logistic coefficients: re-fit by MLE on the cumulative resolved set each update. Update cadence: in-season (high volume, ~hundreds of reports/week at the Jul-Aug peak) = DAILY (fold the day's resolved reports into the running posterior and re-estimate the seasonal term); off-season (low volume) = weekly to monthly or end-of-season re-estimate. Quantification: at the current sample (14 pos / 2055 neg) p_hat = 0.0072, 95% CI [0.0041,0.0113] (width ~0.007); adding resolved reports keeps the base-rate CI narrow (width ~0.008) - the base rate is not the limiting quantity; the value of daily updating is keeping the seasonal/spatial priors current. Parameter table (empirical): in-season triage cadence = daily, off-season = batch/weekly-monthly, source: Exchange 6; seasonal peak volume ~1,400 submissions in August (from the dataset submission-date column).

### Outcome Analysis

Results: the base-rate posterior is stable and already precise (95% CI width ~0.007 at n~2069; adding +20 pos/+800 neg moves p_hat from 0.0072 to 0.0121 with CI width ~0.008), confirming that the base rate is not the bottleneck. The simulated daily-vs-monthly tracking shows raw daily posteriors are noisy (small per-day samples), but the PRACTICAL daily update folds resolved reports into the running Beta posterior (stable) and, more importantly, keeps the SEASONAL and SPATIAL priors current as the life cycle advances and outbreak zones shift. Interpretation: the correct policy is a DAILY online update in season (matching the state's daily triage, Exchange 6) and a lower-frequency re-estimate off-season; each update is a Bayes step on the newly resolved reports plus a re-estimate of the seasonal term. Limitations/bias: the update rule assumes reports are resolved in a roughly timely order and that the seasonal prior's drift is smooth; a sudden new outbreak zone (a new cluster far from the existing ones) would require re-seeding the spatial feature, which the daily re-fit of dist_cluster handles automatically but the kernel's cluster list must be extended - a discontinuity the model should flag. The base-rate interval does not capture the uncertainty in the seasonal/spatial coefficients, which are wider (the month coefficient CI [-0.64,1.15] spans zero).

## Subtask 5: Question 5: Using the model, what would constitute evidence that the pest has been eradicated in Washington State? Scope

### Problem

Question 5: Using the model, what would constitute evidence that the pest has been eradicated in Washington State? Scope: define a quantitative eradication criterion from the detection model and the life cycle, and state how it depends on surveillance intensity.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) there is no fixed clock that alone establishes freedom - it depends on surveillance intensity (Exchange 7); (b) each summer is a queen-release opportunity: a single missed nest releases new queens in fall, so one quiet year is weak evidence (Exchange 7); (c) at least two full seasons of queen emergence and colony founding must be observed clean to be confident (Exchange 7); (d) absence of confirmed reports is weak evidence of absence unless surveillance (traps + reporting) is high and sustained (Exchange 10); (e) detection accumulates over the repeated opportunities in a foraging season (trap checks + public reports + hive-attack reports). Method: compound the per-season miss probability over T clean seasons and solve for the number of seasons T* that reaches a target confidence (0.95); show T* scales with 1/p_det. Rationale: this encodes the expert's 'absence of looking vs absence of hornets' distinction and the life-cycle requirement into a testable rule.

### Modeling Process

Let p_det = per-opportunity detection probability and m = detection opportunities per active season (~12, weekly trapping + report screening over the ~12-week foraging season). If an active nest exists, the chance of missing it over a whole season is q = (1 - p_det)^m. After T consecutive clean seasons, P(free | T clean) = 1 - q^T (under the prior that a nest could be present). T* = smallest T with P(free) >= 0.95. The rule: declare eradication only if (i) >= 2-3 consecutive full seasons with zero confirmed positives AND (ii) surveillance (trap density + reporting) is held at or above the level that set p_det; reset the clock on any confirmed positive or any drop in surveillance. Parameter table (empirical): 2-3 consecutive clean seasons at active surveillance = working eradication threshold, interval [2,3], source: Exchange 7; p_det = 0.1-0.5 (low at sparse surveillance), interval [0.1,0.5], source: Exchange 10; m ~ 12 detection opportunities/season, source: Exchange 6 (weekly cadence) + Exchange 8 (screening capacity).

### Outcome Analysis

Results: with m = 12 opportunities/season, P(free | 2 clean seasons) = 0.920-1.000 and P(free | 3) = 0.977-1.000 across p_det = 0.1-0.5; T* for 95% confidence is 3 clean seasons at p_det=0.1, 2 at p_det=0.2, and 1-2 at p_det>=0.3. Interpretation: at normal-to-high surveillance (weekly trapping plus sustained public reporting, p_det ~ 0.2-0.5), 2-3 consecutive seasons with zero confirmed detections constitute strong (>=95%) evidence of eradication, matching the expert's working threshold; the life-cycle logic is that each missed season lets a surviving nest release new queens, so two full queen-emergence/founding seasons must be observed clean. At LOW surveillance (p_det ~ 0.1 - few traps, sparse reports), even 2-3 clean seasons is weak evidence and the required quiet period lengthens, because absence of reports then reflects absence of looking as much as absence of hornets. Limitations/bias: T* is conditional on m and p_det, both empirical; the criterion assumes the surveillance level is measurable and sustained (a hidden drop in effort invalidates it); it is a population-level statement for the whole state, not a per-location one - a local re-introduction far from existing traps would reset the clock even if state-wide reporting looks quiet; and the 0.95 confidence target is a policy choice, not a biological fact.
