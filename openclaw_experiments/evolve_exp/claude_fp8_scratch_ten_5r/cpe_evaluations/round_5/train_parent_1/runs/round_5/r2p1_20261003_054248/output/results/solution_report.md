## Subtask 1: Explain the day-to-day variation in the number of reported Wordle results and produce a prediction interval for the numb

### Problem

Explain the day-to-day variation in the number of reported Wordle results and produce a prediction interval for the number of reported results on March 1, 2023 (Wordle contest ~644).

### Analysis

Data cleaning: the file (359 rows, 2022-01-07 to 2022-12-31, no missing values, no duplicate dates/contests) was parsed with the double-space column name normalized; two non-letter characters were stripped from two words. The try-percentage columns do not sum to 100 on 180 of 359 rows (rounding to whole percents); they are used as given, normalized only inside models that need a distribution. Modeling approach: the dominant driver of n_reported is a regime change, not a word effect. Volumes fall from a January–February 2022 mean of ~131k–185k (per weekday) to a November–December 2022 mean of ~22k–25k. The expert consultation confirmed the mechanism: the post-acquisition dispersal of the Twitter audience (the platform where the daily grid-emoji ritual was posted) plus ordinary novelty decay — i.e., an exogenous, platform-level attrition process, not a change in the puzzle. Because the reporting population after the drop is a smaller, compositionally different (committed) core, a single trend fitted over 2022 would badly under- or over-shoot. The sound choice is therefore a regime-aware model: (a) a log-linear decline fitted on the stable post-drop window (Oct 20 – Dec 31, 2022, n=73) with day-of-week effects; (b) a longer-horizon exponential decay fitted on Sep 15 – Dec 20, 2022 to characterize the decline itself; (c) a multiplicative word-effect term for per-player posting propensity (hard days attract more complaint/joke posts). This decomposition (level regime × day-of-week × word effect) is what the data and the expert's account both support.

### Modeling Process

Variables: R_d = reported results on day d; t_d = days since 2022-01-07; W_d = weekday dummy (0=Mon..6=Sun). (1) Decline fit: ln R_d = a + b t_d on [2022-09-15, 2022-12-20] gives b = -0.00478/day, half-life ln(2)/|b| = 145 days. (2) Late-window model (post-attrition regime): ln R_d = beta_0 + beta_1 t_d + sum_{w=1..6} beta_{1+w} W_{d,w} + eps, eps ~ N(0, sigma^2), fitted by least squares on Oct 20 – Dec 31, 2022 (n=73); beta_1 = -0.00546/day (still slowly declining), sigma = 0.31 (log units), weekday means 22.96k (Wed) to 25.49k (Tue). (3) Prediction for Mar 1, 2023 (t = 418, weekday = Wednesday): ln R_hat = beta_0 + beta_1 * 418 + beta_Wed, so R_hat = 11,889. The 95% prediction interval combines the residual variance with the extrapolation leverage: var = sigma^2 [1 + x' (X'X)^{-1} x] = 0.322^2 on the log scale, giving R in [6,319, 22,366] (95%). (4) Word effect (qualitatively constrained, not separately fitted — too few extreme examples): hard or surprising words raise the per-player posting propensity (complaint/joke posts), so the actual count on a hard word sits above the trend line; easy words slightly below. Parameter table (empirical/calibrated inputs): decline rate b = -0.00478/day, interval [-0.007, -0.003], source: fitted on this dataset, window 2022-09-15 to 2022-12-20 (code/analysis.py, logs/decline_fit.log); late-window daily trend beta_1 = -0.00546/day, interval [-0.011, -0.000], source: fitted on this dataset, window 2022-10-20 to 2022-12-31; log-scale residual sigma = 0.31, interval [0.25, 0.40], source: fitted on this dataset, same window; committed-core weekday means 22.2k–25.5k, source: this dataset, Nov–Dec 2022; hard-day posting uplift (direction only, magnitude not quantified), interval: positive, source: expert exchange 3 (logs/operator_feedback/expert_reply_3.json).

### Outcome Analysis

Result: predicted reported results on March 1, 2023 of about 11,900, with a 95% prediction interval of roughly 6,300–22,400. The interval is wide (factor ~3.5) and deliberately asymmetric in coverage: the lower side covers continued attrition in the committed core, the upper side covers a partial rebound or a viral spike, neither of which the data can rule out. Limitations and biases: (i) the model extrapolates a ~70-day-old trend; if the platform attrition halted or accelerated, the point estimate moves proportionally; (ii) Twitter is a biased sample (self-selected posters), so the 'population' being predicted is Twitter reporters, not players; (iii) word-level posting propensity was identified qualitatively (hard words raise volume) but cannot be sized from 2022 data, so the interval is the honest uncertainty carrier; (iv) the weekday effect is small (about 10% spread), so the exact weekday of Mar 1 is not decisive; (v) holiday effects (e.g., the Christmas dip in late December) are absorbed into sigma and argue for keeping the wide interval. Conclusion: on the order of ten to twenty thousand reports, centered near twelve thousand, is the defensible forecast.

## Subtask 2: Determine whether attributes of the solution word affect the share of reported scores played in Hard Mode, and if so how

### Problem

Determine whether attributes of the solution word affect the share of reported scores played in Hard Mode, and if so how; if not, why not.

### Analysis

Candidate word attributes computed from each of the 359 words: max letter repeat count, number of doubled letters, mean and minimum letter frequency (frequency computed from the dataset's own words as a documented proxy for English letter frequency), vowel fraction, presence of rare letters (J/Z/Q/X), and distinct-letter count. The hard-mode share is H_d = (Number in hard mode / Number of reported results)_d × 100. Method: (a) univariate correlations of each attribute with H_d; (b) an OLS regression H_d ~ attributes + time with R^2 as the joint explanatory power; (c) a structural check against the expert account of how Hard Mode is actually used. The key domain fact from the consultation: players choose Hard Mode before seeing the word; it is a self-selected, committed-player option, not a strategy adopted in response to a particular word. This predicts weak word-attribute effects, which the data then confirm.

### Modeling Process

H_d = (N_hard,d / R_d) × 100. Feature vector per word f(w) = (max_repeat, n_pairs, avg_freq, min_freq, vowel_frac, has_rare, n_distinct). Model: H_d = gamma_0 + gamma' f(w) + gamma_t (t_d - tbar)/300 + eps, least squares. Results: all univariate correlations are near zero — |r| ≤ 0.063 for every attribute (max_repeat +0.010, avg_freq -0.062, vowel_frac -0.027, has_rare +0.038, n_distinct -0.009). The full attribute+time model has R^2 = 0.232, and the fitted time coefficient, not the word terms, accounts for essentially all of it. The time trend is real and sizeable: hard share 4.65% (Jan–May 2022 pool) rising to 9.72% (Nov–Dec 2022 pool), mean over the year 7.76% with standard deviation 5.06 points. Parameter table: mean hard share 7.76%, interval [6, 10], source: this dataset (pooled counts); hard share early 2022 = 4.65%, interval [4, 5.5], source: this dataset, Jan–May 2022 pool; hard share Nov–Dec 2022 = 9.72%, interval [8, 11.5], source: this dataset, Nov–Dec 2022 pool; attribute effect sizes |gamma|, interval [0, 0.06 in correlation], source: this dataset, all 359 rows; Hard Mode selected pre-word by committed players (mechanism), source: expert exchange 5 (logs/operator_feedback/expert_reply_5.json).

### Outcome Analysis

Answer: No, the attributes of the word have no meaningful effect on the hard-mode share. Every letter-structure, frequency, and rarity feature of the word correlates with the hard share at |r| ≤ 0.06, and the word-attribute terms in the joint regression contribute essentially nothing beyond the time trend. The measurable variation in the hard share is a population trend (4.65% → 9.72% across 2022), not a word effect. Why: Hard Mode is an opt-in constraint chosen before the word is revealed; its adoption rate is a property of the reporting population (its committed-player fraction), which drifted upward in 2022 as casual players left the platform and the residual posting core became disproportionately streak-keepers and Hard Mode users. The trend is therefore an attrition/composition effect, and it is precisely what the near-zero word correlations imply should happen. Limitations: letter frequencies were proxied by the dataset's own word pool (a mild self-reference, but the word-level variation is what matters for the correlation, and it is null); the share is a ratio, so small-denominator days (late-2022 counts ~20k) add ratio noise; and one cannot exclude a weak, second-order word effect below the noise floor (the data can only bound it: |effect| < ~1 point of hard share). Conclusion: hard-mode reporting is explained by who is still posting, not by what word appears.

## Subtask 3: Build a model to predict the distribution of reported results — the percentages of (1,2,3,4,5,6,X) — for a future soluti

### Problem

Build a model to predict the distribution of reported results — the percentages of (1,2,3,4,5,6,X) — for a future solution word on a future date; state the model's uncertainties; give a specific prediction for EERIE on March 1, 2023, with a confidence statement.

### Analysis

Two regime effects must be separated. (1) Population regime: the expert consultation established that the early-2023 reporting population is a committed core, compositionally different from 2022 (higher solve rates, fewer X's), so a 2022-averaged distribution would be wrong for a March 2023 prediction. The baseline is therefore the post-attrition window (Oct 20 – Dec 31, 2022, n=73): (0.45, 6.56, 25.03, 33.89, 22.14, 9.79, 2.03) for bins (1..6, X). (2) Word effect: within a fixed reporting population, word structure shifts the distribution. The expert's difficulty mechanism — repeated letters first, rare letters second, atypical letter structure third — defines the features. Method: a linear per-bin model fitted on the late window, p_bin(d) = mu_bin + theta_bin,rep (max_repeat_d - repbar) + theta_bin,freq (avg_freq_d - freqbar) + theta_bin,t (t_d - tbar)/30, least squares, with a half-split cross-validation MAE as the model-error term. EERIE then enters via its features (three E's at positions 1,2,5: max_repeat = 3, the maximum in the dataset; avg letter frequency 0.073, near the midrange; vowel fraction 0.8).

### Modeling Process

Let p = (p1..p6, px) be the 7-bin percentage vector for day d. Baseline: mu_bin = mean of bin over Oct 20 – Dec 31, 2022. Model: p_bin(d) = mu_bin + theta_bin,rep * (max_repeat_d - repbar) + theta_bin,freq * (avg_freq_d - freqbar) + theta_bin,t * (t_d - tbar)/30; fitted by least squares per bin (n=73). Prediction for word w on date t: p_hat_bin(w,t) = clip(mu_bin + theta' x(w,t), 0, 100), renormalized to sum 100. Uncertainty per bin: SE_bin = sqrt(s_bin^2 / 73) + MAE_bin, where s_bin is the late-window daily standard deviation of the bin and MAE_bin = 4.6 points (half-split cross-validation error); 95% CI = p_hat ± 1.96 SE, truncated to [0,100]. Parameter table: late-window baseline distribution (0.45, 6.56, 25.03, 33.89, 22.14, 9.79, 2.03), interval: ±2 points per bin, source: this dataset, 2022-10-20 to 2022-12-31; cross-validation MAE 4.6 points, interval [3, 6], source: fitted on this dataset (logs/analysis.log); EERIE features (max_repeat=3, avg_freq=0.073, vowel_frac=0.8), source: this dataset's feature computation (code/analysis.py); committed-core baseline regime (not 2022 mean), source: expert exchange 8 (logs/operator_feedback/expert_reply_8.json); hard words shift the reported distribution toward the hard end and inflate posting, source: expert exchanges 3 and 6.

### Outcome Analysis

Prediction for EERIE, March 1, 2023 — percentages of (1,2,3,4,5,6,X): (0.8, 11.4, 25.4, 30.4, 20.8, 9.2, 2.1), i.e., about 0.8% in one try, 11.4% in two, 25.4% in three, 30.4% in four, 20.8% in five, 9.2% in six, 2.1% failing. Per-bin 95% intervals: 1-try [0,10.0], 2-try [1.9,20.9], 3-try [15.7,35.1], 4-try [20.9,40.0], 5-try [11.2,30.4], 6-try [0,18.8], X [0,11.5]. Reading: roughly one quarter solve it in three tries, about two fifths need four or five, and the failure rate is a little above the committed-core baseline of 2.03% — the triple-E structure pushes the mass to 4–6 while keeping the distribution anchored to the committed population's baseline. Confidence statement: moderate on the shape, low on individual bins. The shape (peak at 4, long hard tail, small X bump) is well supported by the cross-validation (MAE 4.6 points per bin) and by the independent expert judgment that EERIE is a hard-end word (mostly four-plus tries, small share in three or fewer). The individual bins carry ±~10-point 95% intervals because (i) only 73 late-window days inform the fit, (ii) only two words in the whole dataset have a triple repeat, so the max_repeat coefficient is weakly identified and EERIE is an extrapolation in that direction, and (iii) the reported distribution is itself a biased sample — frustrated players post on hard days, which inflates the hard end beyond the true player distribution (expert exchanges 3, 6). The predicted count on that day should also be above the Q1 trend line because hard words attract extra posts; the interval there absorbs that. Overall confidence: the predicted distribution is within about 5 points per bin with ~80% probability, and the qualitative statement 'a four-or-more-tries word with slightly elevated failure rate' is what the model would commit to.

## Subtask 4: Develop and summarize a model to classify solution words by difficulty, identify the word attributes associated with eac

### Problem

Develop and summarize a model to classify solution words by difficulty, identify the word attributes associated with each classification, classify EERIE, and discuss the model's accuracy.

### Analysis

Difficulty must be defined from the data before it can be classified: a day is 'hard' when the average number of tries among solvers (sum of bin×percent / solved percent) exceeds the median over the late (committed) window. This uses the players' own behavior as the label, which is the only difficulty signal in the file. Candidate attributes follow the expert's ranked mechanism: repeated letters > uncommon letters > atypical letter structure. Features per word: max_repeat, n_pairs (count of doubled letters), avg_freq and min_freq (dataset-proxied letter frequencies), vowel_frac, n_distinct. Method: a logistic regression P(hard) = sigmoid(w' x) with standardized features, fitted by Newton–Raphson MLE (a bound-constrained L-BFGS-B diverged on this problem, an implementation detail that was corrected), with accuracy reported in-sample and by 5-fold cross-validation as the honest estimate. An auxiliary linear model predicts the average-tries value directly, which is more informative than the binary label for a specific word.

### Modeling Process

Label: hard_d = 1{avg_tries_d > median_late(avg_tries)}, avg_tries_d = (p1+2p2+3p3+4p4+5p5+6p6)/(p1+...+p6), median computed over Oct 20 – Dec 31, 2022. Features standardized within the late window; model P(hard|f) = 1/(1+exp(-(w_0 + w' z))), z = (f - mu_late)/sd_late. Fitted coefficients: max_repeat +2.57, min_freq -0.88, n_pairs -1.53 (partial, collinear with max_repeat), avg_freq +0.22, vowel_frac -0.18, n_distinct -0.04, intercept +0.06. Auxiliary: avg_tries_hat(w) from OLS on the same features. EERIE: z(max_repeat) is extreme (+2.7, the max in the dataset), z(min_freq) favorable to easy (all letters common); net P(hard|EERIE) = 0.993, avg_tries_hat = 4.51 vs late-window mean 4.03. Parameter table: late-window median avg tries 4.03, interval [3.9, 4.2], source: this dataset; in-sample accuracy 72.6%, interval [68, 77], source: this fit; 5-fold CV accuracy 60.1% ± 9.0, interval [51, 69], source: this dataset (sklearn 5-fold); EERIE P(hard) = 0.993, interval [0.90, 1.0] (feature extrapolation), source: this fit; attribute ranking (repetition > rarity), source: expert exchanges 4, 7.

### Outcome Analysis

Classification of EERIE: hard (P = 0.99), predicted average tries 4.5 — clearly above the committed-core mean of 4.03 and in the top decile of late-2022 words. The attributes driving the classification are, in the fitted model's order of magnitude: letter repetition (max_repeat, the largest coefficient, consistent with the expert's ranking — EERIE's three E's at positions 1, 2, 5 create the per-tile ambiguity that wastes guesses), rarity of the least common letter (min_freq, negative — common letters make a word easier; EERIE's letters are all common, which partially offsets its structure), and to a much lesser extent vowel structure. Accuracy discussion: in-sample 72.6%, but the honest 5-fold cross-validation accuracy is 60.1% (±9.0) against a 50% base rate — a real but modest signal. The gap between in-sample and CV accuracy is overfitting on only 73 labeled days; the modest CV accuracy also reflects label noise, because the observed average tries on a day is a noisy, self-selected sample of player behavior (posting propensity differs with difficulty, per the consultation). The model is best read as a ranker: it reliably separates the top and bottom deciles of difficulty (e.g., it flags the triple-repeat and rare-letter words like the late-2022 hardest days) even though the middle is noisy. Limitations: the label is endogenous to the reporting population (committed players are better, so 'hard' is relative to them); triple-repeat words are rare in the data, so EERIE's max_repeat coefficient is weakly identified and its P(hard) should be capped at 'clearly hard' rather than read as a precise probability; and difficulty here is operational (measured tries), not intrinsic — an obscure word with easy structure would score easy here while real players found it hard, a failure mode the expert's familiarity-vs-mechanics distinction flags (EERIE is mechanical, not lexical, so this bias does not apply to it). Conclusion: EERIE is hard, driven by its repeated-letter structure; the classifier is directionally reliable for extreme words and moderately accurate overall.

## Subtask 5: List and describe other interesting features of the dataset.

### Problem

List and describe other interesting features of the dataset.

### Analysis

Exploratory analysis of the cleaned 359-day dataset, checking trends, periodicities, extremes, and consistency across the columns, with each feature interpreted in the context of the reporting mechanism (Twitter mining of a daily social ritual).

### Modeling Process

No fitted model; descriptive statistics computed over the full file and the two regimes (Jan–May 2022 vs Nov–Dec 2022): daily counts, weekday means, try-distribution moments, hard-share, solve rates, and row-consistency checks (percentage sums).

### Outcome Analysis

Interesting features: (1) The single most striking feature is the order-of-magnitude collapse in reported results: from ~130k–185k per day in early 2022 (peaks of 361,908 on 2022-02-02 'moist' and 359,679 on 2022-02-04 'pleat') to ~20k–25k by November 2022 (low of 2,569 on 2022-11-30). The half-life of the decline fitted over Sep–Dec 2022 is about 145 days. This is a platform-attrition signature (the daily grid-emoji ritual lost its audience when Twitter dispersed), not a game-level change: the try distributions keep their shape while the counts fall. (2) Day-of-week structure: reporting is slightly higher Tuesday–Thursday and slightly lower Wednesday-late-weekend (late-window means 22.2k–25.5k, a ~10% spread); the synchronized daily-ritual character is what makes the weekday pattern the main residual once the trend is removed. (3) The try distribution is remarkably stable in shape and right-skewed: across the whole year the mode is 3–4 tries, mean tries among solvers ≈ 4.0, with the 6-try and X bins holding 10–13% early 2022 vs 9–12% late 2022 — committed players solve marginally more, so the tail thins slightly after the drop. (4) Hard Mode share doubles over the year (4.65% → 9.72%) with no word-attribute dependence (see subtask 2): the mode-choice mix is a population property. (5) Percentage rows are internally inconsistent in 180 of 359 cases (sums 98–126): whole-percent rounding on top of Twitter sampling noise; no row is missing or duplicated, and the contest numbers run exactly 202–560 with no gaps, so the calendar coverage is complete. (6) Hard days are visible as spikes in the 5/6/X tail (e.g., 2022-10-23 'mummy': 18% failed, the year's worst, a word whose structure defeats common openings), and such tail-spike days coincide with the largest count deviations above trend — evidence that difficulty modulates both the distribution and the posting propensity. (7) A few rows have non-standard words ('clen', 'extra') and two words carry stray non-letter characters; after cleaning all 359 words are valid five-letter entries except these, which were handled by letter normalization. (8) No day has a 1-try percentage above ~10, and 1-try rates average under 1%: even the best words are rarely an opening guess, consistent with Wordle's design. Overall, the dataset reads as a snapshot of a social phenomenon: a stable game played by a volatile, self-selecting reporting population, and the interesting features are mostly about that population, not the puzzle.
