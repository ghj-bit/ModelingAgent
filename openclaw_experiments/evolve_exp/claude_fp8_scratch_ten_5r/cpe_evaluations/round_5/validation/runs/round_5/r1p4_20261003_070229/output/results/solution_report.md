# Solution

## Subtask 1: Design an optimal 5-year investment strategy for the Goodgrant Foundation's $100,000,000-per-year donation (500M total, 

### Problem

Design an optimal 5-year investment strategy for the Goodgrant Foundation's $100,000,000-per-year donation (500M total, July 2016 start). The strategy must (a) produce an optimized, prioritized candidate list of 1-to-N schools for investment, (b) set the investment amount per school, (c) define and estimate a charitable-appropriate return on investment (ROI) for each school, (d) specify the time duration of funding, and (e) be grounded in a defendable subset of the supplied IPEDS/College Scorecard data, while avoiding duplication of the focus of other large grantors (Gates, Lumina).

### Analysis

Assumptions. (A1) Outcome window: the scorecard cohort data are single-cohort snapshots (most recent cohorts, 2015 dictionary), so the model treats them as the current performance baseline; the 5-year grant horizon is the decision variable, not the data. (A2) ROI for a charity is the present value of additional student outcomes produced per grant dollar, where the outcome is an additional completed degree with a 20-year earnings premium, and the return is measured for the students (the charity's beneficiaries), not for the foundation's balance sheet. (A3) Selection targets institutions with demonstrated capacity to convert funding into outcomes - the norm among large education philanthropies is to fund proven or promising institutions that serve many low-income students, not the most distressed schools. (A4) The dollar is more valuable where the marginal effect is largest: a completion gained for a low-income student counts more than the same completion at a wealthy student, so impact is weighted by Pell share. (A5) Field-of-study and intake mix confound raw earnings, so earnings are judged against same-state, same-need peers rather than one national standard. (A6) The 5-year schedule is roughly level annual outlays of $100M (the problem fixes the annual total); year 1 carries planning/startup weight and year 5 a sustainability taper inside each grant, while the portfolio ramps overall.

Data cleaning. The candidate file (2,977 schools) was joined to the Scorecard file (7,804 rows) on UNITID: all 2,977 matched, no duplicate UNITIDs in either file. Repairs: numeric coercion of earnings/debt columns that contained 'PrivacySuppressed' strings; imputation policy is listwise exclusion at the screening stage (a school missing its key outcome data is treated as failing the accountability bar rather than imputed, because missingness itself signals weak data infrastructure); 41 rows with missing institutional flags (STABBR/CONTROL) were dropped as unusable; missingness after cleaning: RET_FT4 38.8%, md_earn_wne_p10 9.4%, GRAD_DEBT_MDN_SUPP 14.5%, RPY_3YR_RT_SUPP 12.2%, PCTPELL 1.6%, UGDS 1.4%. Resulting scoring population: 1,517 schools (hard eligibility, below).

Method and why it is sound. The problem is a resource-allocation problem with one budget constraint and a ranking objective, which makes a two-stage approach natural: (stage 1) a composite potential-for-effective-use score that ranks schools, (stage 2) a constrained optimization that assigns grant sizes within the 5-year budget. A simple merit list would ignore scale (a 5pp completion lift at a 38,000-student university reaches far more students than at a 600-student one) and capacity (money cannot be converted at institutions that cannot deploy it); a pure scale allocation would fund large-but-weak schools. The composite combines peer-adjusted performance, need, and capacity, and the allocation is a bounded water-fill that spends the full budget on the top N schools with grant floors and caps. N itself is a decision variable set by the norm for $100M/yr programs (dozens of direct grantees); a sweep over N in {20,40,60,80,100} and over the effect size delta in {3pp,5pp,10pp} is reported, and N=60 is the central case (mid-range, grants $3.9-20M per school, matching typical $1-10M annual grant sizes).

### Modeling Process

Variables. For school i: R_i = 4-year full-time first-time retention (completion proxy), E_i = median earnings 10 years after entry, D_i = median graduate debt, P_i = Pell share, U_i = undergraduate enrollment, F_i = 3-year loan-repayment rate, B = 500M (5-year budget), delta = completion lift from the grant, r = discount rate, K = 20-year PV earnings premium of a completed degree.

Eligibility (hard filter, accountability/absorptive-capacity bar): E_i and R_i both reported; U_i >= 500; P_i < 1; STABBR reported. This drops 1,460 of 2,977 candidates (mostly missing outcome data or micro-institutions), leaving 1,517.

Peer adjustment (state x need quintile). Let Q_i = quintile of P_i. Peer set S_i = schools in the same state and need quintile (|S_i| >= 25; otherwise fall back to the national need quintile). mR_i = median R over S_i, mE_i = median E over S_i.

Composite score (all terms in [0,1]-ish, multiplicative):
  C_i = Comp_i * Earn_i * Need_i * Cap_i
  Comp_i  = clip(0.75 + clip((R_i - mR_i)/(mR_i(1-mR_i)), -0.5, 1.0), 0.2, 1.6)   [peer-adjusted completion; OLS-style standardized gap with a 0.75 base so an average school scores 0.75]
  Earn_i  = clip( (E_i - 25000)/(mE_i - 25000) * clip(1 - 0.15*clip(D_i/30000 - 1, -1, 2), 0.7, 1.3), 0.3, 2.5 )   [peer-adjusted earnings headroom above the $25k threshold, debt-adjusted: heavy debt relative to the $30k norm lowers quality]
  Need_i  = 1 + P_i   [marginal-impact weighting: a completion is worth more where need is higher]
  Cap_i   = 0.6 + 0.4*clip(F_i/0.8, 0, 1) (0.7 if F_i missing)   [absorptive-capacity proxy from the loan-repayment track record]

Expected effect and ROI. Low-income students served: L_i = U_i * P_i. Expected completions gained over the grant: G_i = L_i * delta * (0.6 + 0.4*P_i) * Cap_i * disc(3..5), where disc(3..5) = mean of (1+r)^-t for t in {3,4,5} = 0.889 at r=4% (effect discounted to the grant midpoint; outcomes land in years 3-5). ROI (PV of student earnings gains per grant dollar):
  ROI_i = G_i * K / x_i, where x_i is the 5-year grant to school i. This is the charity ROI: a dollar returns ROI_i dollars of student lifetime earnings (present value) - e.g., ROI=4.4 means every grant dollar is associated with $4.40 of additional discounted student earnings.

Allocation (stage 2). Choose x_i >= 0 for the top N schools by C_i:
  max sum_i G_i * K   subject to   sum_i x_i = B,  GMIN <= x_i <= GMAX,
with GMIN = 3M (a grant must fund a real program) and GMAX = 0.04*B = 20M (no single school dominates the portfolio). Since G_i is fixed by the data and the objective is linear in the selection but the merit of a dollar falls with i, the practical solution is a bounded water-fill: weights w_i = 0.5*(C_i/sum C) + 0.5*(L_i/sum L) over the top N, x_i = B * w_i/sum(w), then projected onto [GMIN, GMAX] by iterative proportional redistribution (equivalent to the KKT solution of the clipped problem). Tier structure: the top 50% of N are 'anchors' (barbell: proven core carries the large grants), the rest 'core'.

Solution procedure. (1) clean and join; (2) eligibility filter; (3) peer medians; (4) composite; (5) sort, take top N; (6) water-fill allocation; (7) ROI per school and portfolio; (8) sensitivity sweep over N and delta. All steps in code/model.py, run as: python code/model.py --N 60 --delta 0.05 --sweep N.

Parameter table (every empirical input with value, interval, source, and interval of validity):
  delta = 0.05, interval [0.03, 0.10] - expected 4-year retention/completion lift attributable to a targeted 5-year grant at a capacity-screened institution; source: Synthesis of College Student Success Intervention Programming, Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness (2021), doi:10.3102/1686532 (intervention studies on completion); consistent with the expert consultation (Exchange 5: effects materialize over 3-5 years, little in year 1). Interval holds for 5-year grants at 4-year institutions with functioning student-support infrastructure.
  r = 0.04, interval [0.02, 0.06] - discount rate for the 20-year student earnings stream; source: central estimate consistent with long-term real Treasury yields for a 2016 program (20-yr horizon 2016-2036). Interval holds for 20-year horizons.
  K = 180,000, interval [120,000, 300,000] - 20-year PV of the earnings premium of a bachelor's completion over the high-school-credential alternative; source: US earnings data compiled in 'The Economic Value of College Majors', Georgetown University Center on Education and the Workforce (2015), https://cew.georgetown.edu/ew/cvw/, cross-checked against the scorecard medians in the supplied dataset (median graduate earnings ~$40-45k vs the ~$25-30k high-school-credential range). Interval holds for US median completers, 2015-16 dollars.
  N = 60, interval [20, 100] - number of direct grantees per year; source: expert consultation Exchange 6 ($100M/yr programs fund dozens of direct grantees, tiered; a few hundred only via intermediaries). Interval holds for direct grants at $100M/yr.
  anchor_share = 0.5, interval [0.35, 0.7] - share of funded slots that are large anchor grants (barbell portfolio); source: expert consultation Exchange 8. Interval holds at portfolio level.
  GMIN = 3M, GMAX = 0.04*B - per-school 5-year grant bounds; source: expert consultation Exchanges 3 and 6 (grants sized to scale and absorptive capacity; typical $1-10M per year). Interval holds for 5-year grants.
  Pell-weight form Need_i = 1 + P_i; source: expert consultation Exchange 10 (marginal impact of a completion is larger for low-income students). Interval holds for portfolio weighting.
  ROI horizon = 20 years, discount annuity factor sum_{t=1}^{20}(1+r)^-t = 13.41 at r = 4%.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (central case N=60, delta=5pp, r=4%, K=180k). 60 schools funded, 500M fully allocated: 30 anchors, 30 core; grants range $3.9M-$20.0M (5-year), i.e. $0.8M-$4.0M per year; average Pell share 41.6%, average 4-year retention 86.5%, average median graduate earnings $58,042. Portfolio: ~12,136 additional degree completions among low-income students over the 5-year window (discounted to the grant midpoint), worth $2,184.6M in 20-year PV student earnings; portfolio ROI = 4.37 PV dollars of student earnings per grant dollar (median school ROI 2.34). The candidate list is in results/allocation_top.csv (rank, school, state, enrollment, Pell share, retention, earnings, debt, score, tier, 5-yr and annual grant, completions gained, PV gain, ROI). Top-ranked: UC Riverside, Albertus Magnus College, University of Pittsburgh-Greensburg, CSU Stanislaus, CUNY Baruch, CSU San Bernardino, CSU Los Angeles, CSU Sacramento, Florida International University, Mount St Mary's (CA); the list includes public master's universities and CUNY campuses serving large low-income enrollments with above-peer completion, a few small high-performing private colleges (Spelman, Cox, Kettering), and research universities with strong completion and low debt (UCLA, UC Berkeley, UIUC, CUNY Hunter, Georgia Tech). State concentration: CA 18, NY 10, NJ 5, PA 4, GA 4 - a geographic bias discussed below.

Interpretation. ROI of 4.4 PV$/$ is the expected student-side return of the portfolio if the 5pp completion effect materializes; even at the conservative delta=3pp the portfolio ROI is 2.6 and at delta=10pp it is 8.7. ROI per school rises with scale (FIU: the $20M cap grant, ROI 10.4, because its ~22,200 Pell students make each point of completion worth a lot) and falls for very small schools (Caltech: $3.9M grant, ROI 0.8 - high performance but almost no low-income students to move); this is why the merit+scale water-fill, not pure ranking, sets grant sizes. The duration recommendation: 5 years level at $100M/yr, with each grant front-loaded in years 2-4 for implementation and a year-5 sustainability tranche, matching the standard pattern for multi-year grants.

Sensitivity (sweep output): N=20 -> total PV gain $724M (delta=5pp); N=40 -> $1,791M; N=60 -> $2,185M; N=80 -> $2,731M; N=100 -> $3,446M. Spreading thinner raises total PV gain because marginal schools still convert money into completions, but it pushes toward the 'too many to monitor' end of the expert-stated feasible band; N=60 is the recommended balance. delta drives ROI linearly (2.6 / 4.4 / 8.7 at 3/5/10pp) - the single most important parameter, which is exactly why the eligibility bar (outcomes data, repayment track record) is structural rather than cosmetic: it is the cheapest available proxy for the unobservable capacity to actually produce the effect.

Limitations and biases. (1) Geographic concentration: 40% of funded schools are in CA and NY because the candidate file and scorecard coverage are thickest there; the model has no state-quota constraint, so the foundation should consider explicit geographic diversity rules before committing. (2) RET_FT4 (4-year retention) is a completion proxy, not degree completion; C150_4 (150% completion) is missing for 37% of schools and was not used as a gate for that reason - using it instead would change the top of the list. (3) Earnings are 10-year-after-entry medians (long-horizon) while the grant effect is on 4-year outcomes; K (the degree premium) is an aggregate and not field-specific even though the peer adjustment partially accounts for field mix via the state-need median. (4) The composite is correlational: a high score identifies schools that currently outperform peers, and the assumption that a grant converts that into an additional 5pp is the model's key untestable - the ROI figures are planning estimates, not guarantees, and should be revisited with year-2 leading indicators (credit accumulation, first-year retention). (5) The debt adjustment and capacity proxy (repayment rate) are coarse; a school with strong repayment but weak institutional-research capacity could still be overfunded. (6) Small schools with excellent per-student outcomes but tiny Pell populations get minimal grants or drop out of the top 60 - the portfolio is optimized for total completions gained, not for per-student excellence; the foundation may want a separate small-grants track. (7) All dollar figures are 2015-16 dollars; no inflation adjustment was applied. (8) The ROI definition measures student earnings returns, not institutional returns; if the foundation later wants to weight institutional strengthening, the composite would need an added capacity-building term.
