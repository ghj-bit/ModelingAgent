# Interaction Evidence

Problem 2024_C — Wimbledon 2023 momentum / match-flow modeling (target match 1701, Alcaraz v Djokovic).
Expert exchanges: 10 total.

## Exchange 1
- Question (expert_question_1.md): When a player's form suddenly swings, what on-court sign do coaches most reliably watch to feel momentum turning before it happens?
- Expert reply (summary): The single most reliable cue is the **combination of serve quality plus tempo/body language**, not any one cue. Specifically: (a) the trailing player starting to miss first serves and getting pushed to defensive second serves, (b) the returner stepping in and taking the ball early, (c) body language / between-point tempo slowing down (looking at ground, rushing) in the player about to lose the flow. Return position and depth is a secondary sign.
- How it shaped the work: Establishes the **dominant behavioral mechanisms** for the momentum model. Serve-quality degradation (first-serve % dropping, more second serves, more double faults) and return-pressure (returner taking ball early / deep returns) are treated as the primary state-transition drivers. Body-language/tempo cues are not in the data, so I operationalize them through **time gaps between points** (elapsed_time deltas) as a proxy, and rank serve-quality features as the top drivers. This fixes the causal topology: swing detection keys on serve/return pressure, not just raw point runs.

## Exchange 2
- Question (expert_question_2.md): When a player is about to lose the flow, is it the missed serves themselves, or the opponent stepping in and hitting harder returns, that comes first?
- Expert reply (summary): **Serve deterioration comes first** and is the more reliable leading sign. Sequence: server under pressure misses first serves / pushes them safe → forced into defensive second serves → returner recognizes the weak 2nd serve and steps in to attack. Returner's forward movement is largely a *response* to the weaker serve, not an independent trigger. Caveat: against a strong returner the stepping-in can be pre-planned and simultaneous; but generally watch the server's first-serve % and 2nd-serve quality first.
- How it shaped the work: Confirms a **two-stage causal chain (serve → return)** and fixes the *primary state transition* of the model. In the swing-prediction model, serve-quality features (first-serve %, 2nd-serve count, double-fault count) are treated as **leading indicators** (higher weights), while return-aggression features (return depth, returner net points, returner winners) are **lagging/response features** (lower weights). This ordering is what the model's feature importance must recover; it is the structural rule the coach test (subproblem 2) hinges on.

## Exchange 3
- Question (expert_question_3.md): In a long five-set match, do swings more often happen right after a big point, a broken serve, or a set change?
- Expert reply (summary): Swings follow a **broken serve** (most common trigger), then a **set change** (second). A single big point is the least reliable on its own — its effect fades within a game or two unless it converts into a break/hold/set. Breaks **cluster** (one break often precedes another). A set change is a hard reset (new balls, change of ends, fresh scoreboard) that reliably interrupts a run — a set-dominator can lose the next set, as in the 2023 final.
- How it shaped the work: Fixes the **location of swing events** for detection and prediction. (1) Swing indicators are anchored to *discrete state-change events* (break of serve, set change) rather than individual points. (2) A **break-clustering** assumption is added: after a break, the same player is more likely to break again soon, so the momentum state does not fully reset after one break. (3) **Set-change reset**: at each set boundary the momentum metric is partially reset (new balls/ends), so a run carried into the next set decays. This defines the state-transition points the model watches.

## Exchange 4
- Question (expert_question_4.md): After a player wins a long run of points, about how many games of good play by the other player does it take for the run's effect to fade?
- Expert reply (summary): No fixed number, but the effect typically fades within **one to two games** of the opponent playing well. A single clean hold often resets the flow; two consecutive good games almost always do. Fade is **faster** if the run ended on a break or set change (the reset interrupts it) and **slower** if broken only by a lucky point or tiebreak.
- How it shaped the work: Calibrates the **decay constant** of the momentum metric. A game is ~4–5 points, so "one to two games" → a geometric/EMA decay window of roughly **4–6 points** (half-life ~2–3 points). I set the base decay so a run's contribution is ~e^-1 (≈37%) after one game and ~e^-2 (≈13%) after two games. Modifiers: decay is steeper immediately after a break or set boundary, milder after a tiebreak/lucky point. This is the central parameter driving the "how much better" magnitude.

## Exchange 5
- Question (expert_question_5.md): Is a swing usually a small shift over several games, or a sudden flip that can happen in a single game?
- Expert reply (summary): **Sudden flips are more common** than gradual drifts. Most swings are **abrupt**, crystallizing in a single game (break of serve or set change), with the scoreboard flipping within a few points. Underlying pressure builds over the preceding games (missed first serves, defensive second serves), but the *visible* shift appears suddenly, not as a smooth multi-game slide. Gradual shifts are rarer and reflect slow erosion of serve quality or fitness, not a true momentum swing. Expect a **sudden flip** with preceding games as build-up.
- How it shaped the work: Splits the model into two time scales. (1) A **build-up / pressure index** (leading, several games) from serve-quality degradation predicts the *impending* flip. (2) The **momentum state** flips *abruptly* at the swing event (break/set), i.e., the visible "who is better" indicator should show a sharp transition, not a smooth ramp. This justifies detecting swings as discrete sign-flips of the momentum metric gated by a break/set event, and it sets the prediction horizon to the build-up window (~2–3 games) before the flip.

## Exchange 6
- Question (expert_question_6.md): How should a coach prepare a player to ride out an opponent's run before it fades? What to do during the run?
- Expert reply (summary): During an opponent's run, (a) **slow the game down** — use the full between-point time, take extra time on changeovers; disrupting rhythm is the most effective tactic. (b) **Protect the serve** — prioritize first-serve percentage over pace; avoid double faults. (c) **Play high-percentage, low-risk points** — deep, cross-court, keep the ball in, make the opponent hit one more shot; do not gamble for a spectacular winner. (d) **Target the reset points** — break, set change, or new balls are reliable interrupts; treat them as a fresh start and deliberately reset tempo. (e) **Stay externally focused** — consistent routine, no visible frustration; the run's carryover fades within 1–2 games of solid play.
- How it shaped the work: Provides the **memo content** (subproblem 4, coach advice) and validates the model's feature choices. The advice "prioritize first-serve % over pace" and "avoid double faults" is exactly what the leading serve-quality pressure index measures, and "use full between-point time / disrupt rhythm" maps to the elapsed_time-gap tempo proxy. "Target the reset points" corroborates the break/set-anchored swing locations. The memo's advice is derived from these mechanisms, not copied verbatim.

## Exchange 7
- Question (expert_question_7.md): Would a method built on serve quality and runs of points also work for women's matches, clay-court play, and table tennis, or do those play differently enough to break it?
- Expert reply (summary): **Partly.** (a) **Women's:** same logic, but the serve is a weaker weapon — first-serve % / 2nd-serve quality matter *less* as a momentum signal, while return quality and rally dominance matter *more*; a serve-heavy model underperforms. (b) **Clay:** breaks are more frequent and runs longer, so a *surface-specific baseline* is needed (or ordinary clay play reads as a swing); serve quality is a weaker discriminator, rally length and unforced-error rate carry more weight. (c) **Table tennis:** the structure **breaks** — serves alternate every two points, so "who serves" is not a persistent state and cannot anchor the model; serve advantage is smaller, points far shorter; runs still occur but the serve-quality mechanism does not transfer (need receive quality, error rate, tempo features instead).
- How it shaped the work: Defines the **domain of validity and limitations** (subproblem 3 generalization). The model's serve-weighted topology is valid for men's grass tennis; for women's and clay it requires *recalibrated feature weights* (down-weight serve, up-weight return/rally/UE) and *surface-specific baselines*; for table tennis it is not directly applicable (serve is not a persistent state → the primary anchor fails). This becomes the stated limitation and the basis for the "future models" recommendation (add return/rally/UE features and a baseline per surface/competition).

## Exchange 8
- Question (expert_question_8.md): How does a coach tell a real momentum shift from normal tennis noise?
- Expert reply (summary): By **whether the run is tied to a structural change** (break, hold under pressure, set) plus a change in serve quality and demeanor in *both* players — **not** by run length alone. A run of 3–5 points within one game or on one serve with no break, no serve-quality change, and no demeanor change is routine variance (noise). **Practical test: does the run survive the next server change or set change?** If it persists across a reset, it is real; if it evaporates at the next reset, it was noise.
- How it shaped the work: Provides the **decision rule / threshold** separating signal from noise. (1) A "swing" is confirmed only if it (a) is tied to a break/set event, (b) is accompanied by a serve-quality shift, and (c) **persists across the next server or set boundary** — run length alone is insufficient. (2) This directly calibrates the **coach's random-swing test** (subproblem 2): under the null (random swings) short within-serve runs are expected, so the test must look at *structural + persistent* runs, and a baseline of expected within-serve run distribution is the null reference. (3) It sets the minimum evidence for a detected swing, reducing false positives from ordinary variance.

## Exchange 9
- Question (expert_question_9.md): Going into a new match against a different player, what from past momentum swings should the player carry with him?
- Expert reply (summary): Carry the **patterns, not the outcomes.** Transfers (opponent-independent): (a) the player's **own serve-quality baseline** — the first-serve % / 2nd-serve reliability level below which he starts losing the flow (the early-warning line); (b) the **personal reset routine** (slowing tempo, protecting the serve, high-% play, using breaks/sets as resets); (c) the player's **own trigger profile** — which situations start his slides (being broken, losing a long game, a set change). Does **not** transfer: the opponent's specific tendencies, run lengths/timing, or body-language reads — those must be re-learned each match.
- How it shaped the work: Shapes the **subproblem-3 advice** ("going into a new match against a different player") and the model's *portable* vs *per-match* parameters. The player's own serve-quality baseline and trigger profile are treated as **player-level constants** (portable across matches), while opponent-specific serve/return tendencies are **per-match estimates** that must be refit. This justifies a two-tier parameterization: a portable player baseline (for early-warning thresholds and preparation) plus a fresh per-match estimate of the current opponent, and it is the basis of the coach advice to "reset your own baseline, not the opponent's."

## Exchange 10
- Question (expert_question_10.md): What's the most common coaching mistake that makes a player chase a momentum shift that isn't really there?
- Expert reply (summary): **Over-reading a run of points on a single serve** as a shift — treating any 3–5 point streak (especially within one game or on one player's serve) as a turning point when it is ordinary variance. The coach then compounds it by changing tactics / urging "take over now" (aggressive, low-percentage play), which converts a neutral situation into a real loss of flow: the player gambles on big serves/winners, misses, and hands the opponent the actual break. The underlying error is judging by **run length rather than by structural change + serve-quality change**. Chasing noise makes the player abandon the high-percentage play that was working.
- How it shaped the work: Reinforces the **noise-rejection rule** and **false-positive control** in the swing detector. A detected "swing" must clear the structural + persistence gate (exchange 8); a raw run-length threshold alone is explicitly rejected as the detection rule. For the coach memo, the top advice is "do not chase a 3–5 point run — wait for a structural reset," the direct translation of this mistake. It also motivates reporting the model's false-positive rate (runs flagged that never became breaks/sets) as a limitation metric.
