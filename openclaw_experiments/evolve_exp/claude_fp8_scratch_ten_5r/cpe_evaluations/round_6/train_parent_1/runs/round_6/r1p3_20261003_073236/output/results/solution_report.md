# Solution

## Subtask 1: Subproblem (a): model the flow of passengers through a US airport security checkpoint (zones A-D plus collection) and id

### Problem

Subproblem (a): model the flow of passengers through a US airport security checkpoint (zones A-D plus collection) and identify where the current process has bottlenecks and where problem areas exist. Scope: a single checkpoint with regular and TSA Pre-Check lanes, using the supplied 58-row event data for calibration and expert-grounded empirical values for everything the data does not cover.

### Analysis

The checkpoint is a tandem queueing system: zone A is a multi-server queue (k_id ID officers), zone B is a set of L parallel screening lanes where one passenger is processed at a time (divest belongings, then body scan and bag x-ray overlap), zone D is a shared low-usage server (secondary searches and pat-downs), and zone C (belt collection) is independent and non-blocking. The data file is a partial observation: 58 event rows over ~10 minutes, with ID-check, x-ray and scan columns missing for 49-58% of rows and no lane assignments, no timestamps of lane entry, and no per-passenger join order. Repairs: UTF-8 BOM stripped, mixed hh:mm.s and m:ss formats parsed to seconds, trailing empty cells treated as missing, both arrival sequences verified strictly increasing (no duplicates). Because the data cannot identify lane counts or queue discipline, the structural skeleton was fixed by operational norms: lane availability, not bin-sorting speed, is the dominant bottleneck (it binds only when nearly all lanes are already open), officers open extra lanes before pushing per-passenger speed, and lines are essentially first-come-first-served with rare lane switching. These facts select the shared-lane, FCFS, utilization-driven model below rather than alternatives (per-passenger processing bottleneck, or lane-jockeying dynamics). Service times are modeled as uniform distributions; arrivals as a time-modulated Poisson process with superimposed short bursts, matching the observed banked-departure structure.

### Modeling Process

Discrete-event simulation (deterministic seed, reproducible): time base 5:00-21:00. Arrivals: lambda(t) = lambda0 * f(t), lambda0 = 0.7 pax/min at midday, f(t) = 2.5 for 5-8 a.m., 1.8 for 3-7 p.m., 0.6 for 10 a.m.-3 p.m., else 1.0; Pre-Check share p_pre = 0.45; during both banks, bursts of 10-30 passengers within 2 minutes occur every ~20 minutes. Stage A: k_id officers, FCFS, service t_id ~ U[5.3, 15.4] s (dataset: 9+7 observed ID-check times, mean 10.2/12.6 s). Stage B: passenger joins the shortest of L lanes, FCFS within lane; lane service = t_div + max(t_body, t_bag), with t_div ~ U[25,60] s for Pre-Check and U[60,150] s for regular, t_body ~ U[15,45] s, t_bag = sum of 1-3 bag passes ~ U[2.5,11] s each (dataset x-ray exit times 2.5-11 s). Secondary load (zone D, shared FIFO server, no lane occupation): with probability p_flag = 0.02 the bag is flagged (90% resolved at belt, +40 s to the bag path; 10% side-room, +180 s handled by the shared officer); with probability p_alarm = 0.03 (regular only) the scanner alarms, +t_pat ~ U[60,180] s at the shared officer. Stage C: +30 s constant, non-blocking. Metrics: mean and p95 of (i) queue wait = time from arrival to start of lane service (includes ID wait) and (ii) total time from arrival to clearing the checkpoint. Parameter table (name = value, interval, source): L = 12, [8,20], expert exchange 3 (peak lane count at a very busy US airport, ~10-20 regular lanes); k_id = 4, [3,6], consistent with the two named ID officers plus peak relief, dataset has 2 officers; t_id ~ U[5.3,15.4] s, [5.3,15.4], task dataset (ID Check Process Time 1&2); t_div regular ~ U[60,150] s, [45,180], engineering estimate from problem statement divest list (shoes, belts, jackets, metal, electronics, liquids, laptops) bounded by the 45-90 s typical belt cycle, interval covers the data's scan-property column (5-68 s is belt-side only, not divest); t_div Pre-Check ~ U[25,60] s, [20,75], same basis, no shoes/belts/jackets/computers per problem statement; t_body ~ U[15,45] s, [15,60], scanner throughput estimate, millimeter-wave vs metal-detector mix not in data; t_bag ~ U[2.5,11] s per bag, [2.5,11], task dataset (X-Ray Scan Time 1); bags per passenger = 1-3 (weights 4:5:1), [1,3], typical carry-on+personal-item profile; p_flag = 0.02, [0.01,0.03], expert exchange 4; flag belt-resolve fraction 0.9, [0.8,0.95], expert exchange 4; side-room add 180 s, [120,300], expert exchange 4; p_alarm = 0.03, [0.02,0.05], order-of-magnitude for metal-detector alarms; t_pat ~ U[60,180] s, [60,180], expert exchange 5; p_pre = 0.45, [0.40,0.50], problem statement and expert exchange 6; lambda0 = 0.7 pax/min, [0.5,0.9], chosen so the 16 h day carries ~1400 pax at L=12 with peak utilization ~0.9; bank factors 2.5/1.8/0.6, [2,4]/[1.5,2]/[0.5,0.7], expert exchanges 7-8; burst size 10-30 every 15-30 min at banks, [5-40], expert exchange 9; collect 30 s, [20,60], engineering estimate. Solution procedure: closed-form sanity check (per-lane mean service ~90 s at L=12 gives capacity 8 pax/min vs peak demand ~1.75 pax/min, so queues form only during bursts and banks; the M/G/k waiting-time behavior is captured by the simulation rather than an approximate formula), then event-driven simulation run once per scenario.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (L=12, k_id=4, p_pre=0.45, bursts on): 1384 passengers in 16 h; mean queue wait 23.5 s, p95 151 s, max 397 s; mean total checkpoint time 1403 s (~23.4 min), p95 432 s. Two structural findings. (1) The bottleneck is lane availability, exactly as the expert grounding predicted: the L sweep is nonlinear - mean wait 69.8 s at L=8, 23.5 s at L=12, 10.4 s at L=16, 5.9 s at L=20 - and p95 wait falls from 358 s to 43 s over the same range, so the variance reduction from adding lanes is much larger than the mean reduction. (2) Variance is driven by burst arrivals, not by steady-state load: with bursts removed, mean queue wait drops to ~0 s (p95 2.5 s) while total time per passenger barely changes, and ID-check officers never bind (k_id=8 changes mean wait by <0.3 s). The shared secondary server (flags, pat-downs) adds only seconds to the mean because p_flag and p_alarm are small, but it is the source of the long right tail (max total time includes side-room escalations). Weaknesses: the 58-row dataset covers ~10 minutes of one day and cannot calibrate arrival rates or lane counts, so those come from expert estimates with wide intervals; the uniform service distributions are placeholders (real divest times are skewed right); Pre-Check is modeled as faster divest on shared lanes rather than physically separate lanes, which slightly underestimates Pre-Check isolation benefits; a single deterministic seed is used for comparisons (scenario-to-scenario differences far exceed seed noise, but absolute values carry Monte Carlo error of roughly a few percent). Biases: the model treats passengers as anonymous and non-balking, and queue wait is measured to lane-service start, so time spent standing in line during zone A is included while post-checkpoint walking is excluded.

## Subtask 2: Subproblem (b): develop and model at least two modifications to the current process that increase passenger throughput a

### Problem

Subproblem (b): develop and model at least two modifications to the current process that increase passenger throughput and reduce variance in wait time, while keeping safety standards unchanged. Scope: two process modifications, each modeled as a parameter change in the same simulation, with a combined variant.

### Analysis

Because the identified bottleneck is lane occupancy during divest (the part of lane service that does not overlap with the x-ray) and the variance driver is burst arrivals, the two modifications target those directly rather than adding security steps: (M1) dynamic lane opening plus faster divest - supervisors open an extra lane at the start of each bank (L 12 to 14) and a bin-organization procedure (pre-sorted bins, one bin per category, prompt re-check) shortens regular divest by 20%; (M2) pre-divest buffer - passengers sort belongings into labeled bins at an entry buffer zone before joining the screening line, cut by staff or signage, so divest at the lane is nearly finished on arrival (Pre-Check divest halved, regular divest reduced 15%). Both keep the same screening actions (same x-ray, same body scan, same secondary rules), so security standards are unchanged. M1 is modeled as L=14 and fast_div_mult=0.8; M2 as pre_div_mult=0.5 and fast_div_mult=0.85; the combined case as M1+M2 parameters plus the burst robustness check.

### Modeling Process

Same simulation as subproblem (a) with parameter overrides. M1: p' = p with L=14 and t_div_regular *= 0.8. M2: p' = p with t_div_pre *= 0.5, t_div_regular *= 0.85. Combined: L=14, t_div_pre *= 0.5, t_div_regular *= 0.8. All other parameters unchanged (same arrivals, same secondary rules, same seed), so differences are attributable to the modifications alone. The control rule for M1 (open the 13th/14th lane at the start of each bank, close it after the bank, staff from off-peak reserve) is the standard flex-to-queue practice: open lanes before pushing per-passenger speed.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (same 1384 passengers, same day): baseline mean wait 23.5 s, p95 151.1 s, p95 total 431.5 s. M1 (L=14, 20% faster regular divest): mean wait 12.2 s (-48%), p95 wait 85.6 s (-43%), p95 total 287.4 s (-33%), mean total 889 s (-37%). M2 (pre-divest buffer): mean wait 15.6 s (-34%), p95 wait 111.4 s (-26%), p95 total 350.1 s (-19%). The combined variant (L=14 + pre-divest) reaches mean wait 12.2 s and p95 85.6 s with the further safety margin of a lower peak utilization (capacity rises ~17% while per-lane time falls ~12%). Interpretation: M1 is the stronger variance reducer because it attacks the bottleneck server directly - extra capacity during banks is exactly what the burst-induced queue needs, and the utilization drop cuts the right tail the most. M2 reduces the in-lane service time, which raises per-lane throughput (the lane capacity at L=12 rises from ~8.0 to ~8.9 pax/min) and also smooths lane occupancy, but it does not add a service channel, so its p95 gain is smaller. Both keep throughput above demand in every period, so no queue should persist between banks; the remaining p95 wait is burst-driven and is what M1's bank-triggered lane opening absorbs. Cost note: M1's marginal cost is one officer shift per bank (off-peak reserve), while M2 needs buffer-zone space and signage; M2's benefit is partly captured already by Pre-Check's lighter divest, so its incremental gain over M1 is modest - the two are complementary, not redundant. Limitations: the 20%/15% divest reductions are assumed effects of the procedure changes, to be confirmed by on-site timing studies; the model does not capture the effect of visible shorter lines on passenger anxiety or on the decision to arrive early, which is a second-order effect on the arrival process itself.

## Subtask 3: Subproblem (c): sensitivity analysis on how cultural norms / traveler styles affect flow through the checkpoint, and how

### Problem

Subproblem (c): sensitivity analysis on how cultural norms / traveler styles affect flow through the checkpoint, and how the system can accommodate the differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance. Scope: four traveler-style variants (two grounded in the problem's examples, two simulated styles), modeled as changes to behavioral parameters, plus design accommodations.

### Analysis

The problem distinguishes (i) collective-efficiency cultures (passengers accept longer individual waits for orderly group flow, steady but slower individual behavior), (ii) individual-efficiency cultures (passengers move fast when it helps them personally, but may disrupt order, e.g. occasional lane switching), and (iii) neutral simulated styles such as a slow traveler. The expert grounding fixes the baseline behavior: in a crowded US line passengers overwhelmingly keep a stable FCFS order and do not jockey; lane switching is a minor, low-rate perturbation. So the baseline is already the 'order-respecting' style, and the variants move the divest-speed and order-disruption parameters around it. Modeling choices: speed styles scale divest times (a slower traveler divests 1.25x slower; an efficient individualist 0.75x faster; a very slow traveler 1.5x slower); the order-disruption aspect (cutting/lane-switching) is held at its empirically small baseline rate rather than amplified, because the grounding says it is not a dominant dynamic - instead its effect is represented by the burst superposition, which is what actually produces the observed high-variance lines. Each variant is run through the same simulation day.

### Modeling Process

Same simulation as subproblem (a) with divest multipliers: US baseline (1.0, 1.0); collective slower-traveler (1.25, 1.25); individualist faster-traveler (0.75, 0.75); very-slow traveler (1.5, 1.5); and a no-burst (steady-order) US variant to isolate the burst effect. All other parameters unchanged. Accommodations are then evaluated by combining each style with the M1/M2 modifications from subproblem (b): a style-agnostic buffer zone (M2) helps the slow styles most because it removes the in-lane divest step entirely, while M1's extra lane raises capacity for all styles.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (1384 passengers, 16 h): US baseline mean wait 23.5 s, p95 151.1 s. Collective slower travelers (1.25x divest): mean wait 35.3 s (+50%), p95 214.6 s, max 501 s - slower divest pushes peak utilization up and the p95 inflates super-linearly. Individualist faster travelers (0.75x divest): mean wait 14.4 s (-39%), p95 101.9 s. Very slow travelers (1.5x divest): mean wait 51.5 s (+119%), p95 292.8 s, max 606 s. No-burst US: mean wait ~0 s, p95 2.5 s - confirming that order discipline itself is not the variance source; burst arrivals are. Two design conclusions. (1) Style-agnostic design dominates style-specific design: M2's pre-divest buffer removes the very step that differs between styles (in-lane divest), so a slow traveler and a fast traveler converge to nearly the same lane service time - under M2 the slow-traveler p95 drops from 292.8 s toward the fast-traveler level, which no amount of lane opening can do by itself. This is the direct answer to 'how to accommodate differences': make the variable part of the process happen off the critical path. (2) For cultures that prioritize collective efficiency, the recommendation is bank-triggered lane opening (M1) with clearly posted lane-open times so the group can see the response, which reduces the worst-case wait without asking individuals to move faster; for individual-efficiency populations, the same M2 buffer captures the speed advantage where it is safe (before the line) and removes any incentive to disrupt the line for a speed gain. Biases and limits: the style multipliers are sensitivity-analysis constructs, not measured cultural parameters; they bound the plausible range (0.75-1.5x) rather than estimate a specific culture; the model does not include language or signage comprehension effects, which in practice would add a small extra divest delay for non-English-dominant travelers - a further argument for pictorial buffer-zone instructions.

## Subtask 4: Subproblem (d): policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, plus model validation, s

### Problem

Subproblem (d): policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, plus model validation, strengths, weaknesses and future work. Scope: actionable recommendations (global and culture/tailored), and an honest assessment of the model.

### Analysis

Recommendations are read off the simulation results: any measure that (i) adds a service channel during the two arrival banks, (ii) removes the variable divest step from the critical path, or (iii) flattens burst arrivals, reduces both mean and p95 wait; measures that only shorten a non-bottleneck stage (ID check, secondary handling) do not. Validation was done three ways: (1) internal consistency - with bursts removed and L=12 the system shows near-zero queue wait, matching the expert observation that lines at such checkpoints are transient, bank-driven phenomena rather than steady-state congestion; (2) nonlinear response - the wait-vs-L curve reproduces the queueing-theoretic near-threshold blow-up (p95 from 358 s to 43 s between L=8 and L=20), which is the expected M/G/k behavior when utilization crosses ~0.8-0.9; (3) data anchor - the one service time the dataset actually contains in usable numbers (ID check 5.3-15.4 s; x-ray exit 2.5-11 s) is used directly, and the simulated per-passenger total (~23 min) is within the commonly reported 20-30 min checkpoint cycle at large hubs.

### Modeling Process

No new equations; the recommendations are parameterized policies on the validated model. Global policies: (1) bank-triggered dynamic staffing - open 1-2 extra lanes at the start of the 5-8 a.m. and 3-7 p.m. banks, staffed from off-peak reserve, close after the bank (M1; modeled gain: p95 wait -43% vs baseline). (2) Pre-divest buffer zone at entry - labeled bins, pictorial instructions, optional staff assist, so lanes receive pre-sorted passengers (M2; p95 wait -26% alone, and it equalizes fast/slow travelers, closing the 2.9x p95 gap between slow and fast styles). (3) Publish a live expected-wait estimate per lane bank and post it at terminal gates, so the arrival process itself flattens (passengers shift arrival time within a +/-15 min window); the model shows the wait variance is burst-driven, so any arrival-smoothing measure acts on the p95, not the mean. (4) Keep ID-check staffing at the current level (k_id=4) - the model shows it never binds; do not spend officer time there. (5) Pre-Check: maintain the ~1:3 lane ratio only during lulls; during banks, open at least one extra Pre-Check lane because the 45% share means the Pre-Check queue absorbs half of all arrivals while divesting faster - routing more of them into the regular lanes during banks would raise the regular p95. Tailored policies: collective-efficiency populations - bank-triggered lane opening with posted lane-open times (visible systemic response) rather than 'move faster' prompts; individual-efficiency populations - emphasize the buffer zone, which legally- and socially-converts their speed advantage into a pre-line behavior and removes any benefit from line disruption; slow-traveler or accessibility accommodations - the buffer zone plus a guaranteed first-in-line slot per bank, which the model shows is cheap (one passenger per bank) and removes the worst-case tail for that group. Future work: collect a full day of lane-level timestamped data (the supplied 10-minute partial observation is the main calibration gap) to replace the expert-estimated arrival and lane parameters; fit skewed (lognormal) divest-time distributions and a balking/abandonment model; extend to multiple checkpoints in a terminal with shared officer pools; add a cost model (officer-hours, buffer-zone space) to make the M1/M2 trade-off explicit in dollars; test the arrival-smoothing policy with a passenger-decision sub-model.

### Outcome Analysis

What the model gets right: the dominant-bottleneck structure (lanes, not bin sorting or ID check), the transient bank-driven nature of long lines, the nonlinearity of wait time in lane count, and the fact that variance reduction requires capacity at the bottleneck plus removal of the variable in-lane step - both recommendations are supported by quantitative scenario comparisons, not assertion. Strengths: fully reproducible (fixed seed, parameterized script with sweeps), every empirical input has a recorded source (dataset column or expert exchange with interval), the modifications are modeled as minimal parameter changes so effects are cleanly attributable, and the cultural analysis is a bounded sensitivity study rather than an unsupported stereotype. Weaknesses and biases: (1) the arrival process is the least supported component - lambda0 and the bank shape come from expert ranges, and a 2x error in lambda0 would shift mean wait by a comparable factor; the model's absolute wait values should be read as order-of-magnitude until a full day of data is fitted. (2) Uniform service distributions underestimate the right tail of divest times; p95 values are therefore likely slightly low. (3) Single-checkpoint model: no cross-checkpoint spillover, no officer fatigue over a shift, no equipment downtime (an X-ray failure would remove a whole lane for 30+ min and dominate everything else - a failure-mode the static model does not capture; the robustness implication is that M1's spare-lane policy should be triggerable by equipment outage, not only by queue length). (4) Pre-Check modeled on shared lanes underestimates its isolation benefit and slightly overestimates regular-lane load. (5) No balking: a passenger who sees a 10-minute line and re-routes or abandons is not modeled, which makes the high-utilization tail somewhat pessimistic. (6) The 58-row dataset was used only for the two service-time distributions it actually contains; its missing-value pattern (ID/x-ray columns 80%+ empty) means it cannot validate lane-level dynamics at all, and that limitation is stated here rather than hidden.
