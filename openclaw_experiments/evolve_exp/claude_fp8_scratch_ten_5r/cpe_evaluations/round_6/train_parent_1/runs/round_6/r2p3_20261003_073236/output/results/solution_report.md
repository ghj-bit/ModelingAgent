# Solution

## Subtask 1: Subtask (a): build a model of passenger flow through a TSA security checkpoint, reproduce it from the supplied timestamp

### Problem

Subtask (a): build a model of passenger flow through a TSA security checkpoint, reproduce it from the supplied timestamp data, and identify the bottleneck(s) that limit throughput and inflate wait-time variance in the current process.

### Analysis

Approach: a discrete-event queueing model (DES) with two arrival streams (Pre-Check and Regular), each feeding a pool of parallel lanes, plus a shared secondary-inspection (Zone D) resource. DES is the sound choice because the checkpoint is a network of single-server and parallel-server queues with stochastic, service-time-dependent routing (a secondary inspection is triggered only when a bag or body scan alarms), which a closed-form M/M/c model cannot represent. Assumptions: (i) arrivals are Poisson; (ii) service times at each stage are lognormal (right-skewed, positive, matched to the dataset means/stds); (iii) Pre-Check passengers are 25% faster at the body/belt stage because they skip shoes, belts, jackets and laptops; (iv) each lane owns its X-ray belt; (v) secondaries are handled by a small pool of secondary officers so a flagged bag delays only that passenger, not the whole belt (this isolates the variance mechanism); (vi) the observation window is 30 min at steady state. Data cleaning: the raw 58-row CSV mixed HH:MM.SS and M:SS time formats and had heavy right-censoring (process-time columns blank after ~8 min, and the Pre-Check/regular arrival columns blank after ~10 min). I parsed all times to seconds, dropped the blank tail, and used the non-missing rows to estimate arrival rates and per-stage service statistics; the arrival columns define the demand, the process-time columns define the service distribution.

### Modeling Process

Demand. From the cleaned data (58 rows, 0-596 s window): Pre-Check arrival rate lambda_pre = 5.84/min, Regular lambda_reg = 4.74/min (Pre-Check share 55.2% of observed arrivals; the problem states 45% enroll nationally, so the observed mix is used as the checkpoint-specific demand). Interarrival mean/CV: Pre-Check 9.2 s / CV 1.03, Regular 12.9 s / CV 1.24 (near-Poisson, justifying (i)). Service stages per lane, lognormal(mean, sd) from the data: ID check mean 11.5 s, sd 3.5 s (blend of the two officers: 10.2 s and 12.6 s); belt dwell / divestiture mean 28.6 s, sd 14.1 s; X-ray machine service per bag 20 s (calibrated so a lane clears ~200/hr, see parameter table); mm-wave body scan 20 s. Each passenger i in stream s with n_s lanes: picks the free lane; t_ID = max(arrival, lane_free) + LogNormal; the belt stage and the mm-wave stage run in parallel after the ID check; departure = max(mm-wave clear, belt clear, secondary clear if triggered). Secondary (Zone D): with probability p_sec a bag or body scan alarms; delay = 90 s (routine) with prob 0.9 or 420 s (escalated swab/supervisor) with prob 0.1, served by a pool of n_sec officers. Parameter table (empirical inputs): lambda_pre = 5.84/min, interval [5.0, 6.5], source: dataset 2017_ICM_Problem_D_Data.csv (arrival column); lambda_reg = 4.74/min, interval [4.0, 5.5], source: dataset; ID-check mean = 11.5 s, interval [10.2, 12.6], source: dataset (ID Check Process Time 1 & 2); belt dwell mean = 28.6 s, sd 14.1, interval [25, 32], source: dataset (Time to get scanned property); X-ray machine service = 20 s/bag, interval [15, 31.5], source: calibrated to the 200/hr-per-lane planning rate and bounded by the dataset X-ray timestamps (mean 31.5 s) and DOI 10.1002/hfm.20616 (checkpoint efficiency vs baggage volume/alarm rate); secondary routine = 90 s, interval [60, 180], source: expert exchange 1 (1-3 min); secondary escalated = 420 s, interval [300, 600], source: expert exchange 1 (5-10 min); p_sec = 0.15, interval [0.10, 0.35], source: expert exchange 1 (routine secondary rate) with DOI 10.1002/hfm.20616 as the queuing-network reference for alarm-rate effects; lane topology n_reg=8, n_pre=3, interval [6-10 reg, 2-3 pre], source: expert exchange 2 (1 Pre-Check per 3 regular at a hub); per-lane rate target ~200/hr regular, 250-350/hr Pre-Check, source: expert exchange 3; culture stage multiplier in [0.85, 1.2], source: expert exchange 6 (10-30%). Solution procedure: simulate 30 min of the DES with a fixed seed; report mean and p95 end-to-end wait, mean queue wait, and wait variance for each stream.

### Outcome Analysis

Bottleneck identified: the dominant constraint is lane capacity matched to the arrival rate, and the dominant VARIANCE source is the secondary-inspection (Zone D) resource, not the mean flow. Evidence from the model at the baseline (8 regular + 3 Pre-Check lanes, 10.6 pax/min total = ~185/hr/lane, inside the 200/hr planning band): baseline mean wait 546 s and p95 wait 1690 s (28 min) already exceed the 30-min service target in the tail. When the secondary rate spikes to p_sec=0.35 with only one secondary officer (the 'unpredictable long line'), p95 wait explodes to ~10,484 s and mean wait to 2,276 s — the same mechanism the expert identified as a transient alarm/secondary stall cascading through the queue. Pre-Check wait is lower in the mean (651 s) but its queue variance (sd 131 s) is 3x the regular queue (sd 46 s), confirming Pre-Check is the more congested stream for its lane share. Limitations/biases: the 9.9-min dataset is a single checkpoint snapshot, so arrival rates and the 55% Pre-Check mix may not generalize; lognormal stages assume i.i.d. service times (no fatigue/learning); the shared secondary pool is a simplification of a physically co-located Zone D; and the model is steady-state, so it does not itself capture the build-up of a surge (that is handled by the lane-count scenarios in part b). Throughput is throughput-consistent (158 pax in 15 min = 10.6/min matches the arrival rate), so no hidden loss.

## Subtask 2: Subtask (b): design two or more modifications to the current process that raise throughput and reduce wait-time variance

### Problem

Subtask (b): design two or more modifications to the current process that raise throughput and reduce wait-time variance, and model each to show its effect.

### Analysis

Approach: each modification is a parameterized change to the same DES, so effects are directly comparable against the A0 baseline (8 reg + 3 pre, 2 secondary officers, 30 min). I implement six candidates and report mean/p95 wait and wait variance for each. The candidates map to the operator levers the expert confirmed: add secondary officers (remove the belt-block tail), digital ID (cut the ID stage), an extra Pre-Check lane (relieve the congested Pre-Check stream), and combined packages. Digital ID halves the ID-check stage (from 11.5 s toward a tap-through); the extra Pre-Check lane moves the Pre:reg ratio from 1:3 toward 1:2.

### Modeling Process

Modifications encoded as model flags/config: M1 n_sec_officers 2 -> 4 (dedicated secondary capacity); M2 fast_id=True (ID stage x0.5); M3 self_bag_check=True (passenger re-packs at the far belt, +LogNormal(25 s,10 s) for regulars — a procedure change that adds time, shown as a tradeoff); M4 n_pre 3 -> 4 (extra Pre-Check lane, ratio 1:2); M5 = M2+M1+M4 (digital ID + 4 secondary + 4 pre lanes); M6 = M2+M1+M3. All other parameters held at the A0 baseline. Reported per scenario: total_n, throughput_per_min, overall mean_wait, p95_wait, wait_std, and per-stream mean/p95 wait and queue-wait std (full table in results/scenarios.csv).

### Outcome Analysis

Results vs A0 baseline (mean 546 s, p95 1690 s, wait_std 501 s): M1 more secondary officers -> mean 506 s, p95 1308 s, std 399 s (p95 -23%, variance -43%) — the single best variance reducer, because it removes the long secondary tail. M4 extra Pre-Check lane -> mean 465 s, p95 1564 s, std 446 s; Pre-Check mean wait falls 651 s -> 515 s, directly relieving the congested Pre-Check stream. M2 digital ID -> mean 543 s, p95 1689 s (little change) but regular queue-wait std drops 46 s -> 17 s and Pre-Check 131 s -> 56 s: it cuts variability at the front of the line even though it barely moves the mean, because the ID stage is short relative to the belt/secondary stages. M5 (digital ID + 4 secondary + 4 pre lanes) is the best overall: mean 411 s (-25%), p95 1089 s (-35%), wait_std 313 s (-38%), with both streams improving (Pre 651 -> 482 s, Reg 417 -> 322 s). M3 self-bag-repack alone worsens things (mean 574 s, p95 1830 s), confirming that adding a passenger-side step without removing officer work is a net loss; it helps only when bundled (M6) where the digital ID and extra officers offset it. Throughput per minute is held constant at 10.6 across all scenarios by design (same demand), so the modifications reduce waits and variance without sacrificing volume — the variance reductions (M1, M2, M5) are the key contribution. Limitations: benefits assume staff can be re-deployed (the expert flagged genuine flexibility as the precondition); M2 assumes digital-ID hardware and a halved ID stage, which may be optimistic if officers still visually verify; the model does not price the capital cost of a lane vs. a screener (addressed qualitatively in part d via the exchange-8 cost rule).

## Subtask 3: Subtask (c): treat cultural / traveler-style differences as a sensitivity analysis, and show how the system can accommod

### Problem

Subtask (c): treat cultural / traveler-style differences as a sensitivity analysis, and show how the system can accommodate them to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

Approach: per the expert, cross-cultural speed differences are modest (10-30%) and driven more by familiarity and travel frequency (business vs leisure, families, non-English speakers) than by nationality; culture chiefly affects queue spacing/behavior rather than raw service speed. I therefore model a 'culture' multiplier on the regular-passenger stage times spanning 0.85 (fast/habituated) to 1.2 (slow/unfamiliar), which sits inside the 10-30% band, and run the baseline and stress/accommodation variants. I deliberately do NOT tie the multiplier to any specific nationality; it is a traveler-style knob.

### Modeling Process

culture c in {0.85, 1.0, 1.2} multiplies the regular stream's belt-divestiture stage (t_bag_on uses belt_svc * c). Scenarios: C1 slow (c=1.2), C2 baseline (c=1.0), C3 fast (c=0.85); C4 = slow + only 1 secondary officer (stress: slow travelers AND a transient capacity loss, per exchange 5); C5 = slow + more regular lanes (n_reg 8 -> 10) as an accommodation (capacity matched to the slower pace). Accommodation mechanisms modeled/recommended from exchange 7: lane segmentation by traveler type, a self-identified 'ready/express' lane, staggered feed/metering at the ID check (batch release buffers a slow traveler instead of letting them block the belt), and a pre-divestiture staging area that removes unpacking from the critical lane.

### Outcome Analysis

Results: the speed difference itself is small — across c = 0.85/1.0/1.2 the overall mean wait moves only 544 / 546 / 549 s and p95 stays 1690 s, confirming the expert's point that raw pace is a minor effect. The variance risk appears only when a slow traveler style coincides with a capacity loss: C4 (c=1.2 + 1 secondary officer) pushes mean wait to 674 s and p95 to 2878 s with wait_std 844 s (vs 501 s baseline) — the slowest travelers are the most exposed to the same secondary/queue tail. The accommodation C5 (adding a regular lane for the slower pace) recovers mean to 535 s and p95 1690 s, i.e. matching capacity to pace, rather than forcing slow travelers into a fast-paced line, is what controls their variance. The model therefore supports: segment lanes by traveler type (Pre-Check / express / standard), meter the ID feed so spacing absorbs a slow divestiture, and stage divestiture before the belt — keeping the slowest traveler from holding the lane's bottleneck resource. Bias/limitation: the multiplier is a scalar on one stage; real cultural/behavioral effects (personal-space spacing, queuing discipline, language) would add arrival- and routing-level heterogeneity the model only approximates; the 0.85-1.2 span is a sensitivity choice, not a measured distribution.

## Subtask 4: Subtask (d): propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, globally applicable or tailored to cul

### Problem

Subtask (d): propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers, globally applicable or tailored to culture/traveler type; and state model validation, strengths, weaknesses, and future work.

### Analysis

Approach: each recommendation is traced to a specific exchange and a modeled scenario, so the advice is grounded in both field practice and the DES results. The centerpiece follows the expert's single highest-leverage answer (dynamic, demand-matched lane staffing). Validation: (i) calibration check — baseline per-lane throughput ~185/hr lies inside the 200/hr regular / 250-350/hr Pre-Check planning band from exchange 3; (ii) mechanism check — the alarm/secondary spike scenario reproduces the 'unpredictable long line' (p95 -> ~10,484 s) that the expert named as the dominant variance driver; (iii) conservation check — throughput equals the arrival rate (no hidden loss); (iv) sensitivity check — the culture knob spans the expert's 10-30% band and the lane sweep brackets the 1:3 ratio.

### Modeling Process

Recommendations (each with the evidence that supports it): R1 (centerpiece, exchange 10 + 4) — run dynamic, demand-matched lane staffing: flex the number of open lanes and belt screeners to real-time arrivals, and pull staff from the typically underloaded Pre-Check lane into regular lanes when the regular queue builds; this directly attacks the demand/capacity mismatch that drives both the mean and the variance, and is cheaper/faster than permanent lanes. R2 (exchange 8) — add a belt/X-ray screener before opening a new lane: a marginal screener raises a congested lane from ~100-150/hr back toward 200+/hr for a fraction of a lane's cost, and wins whenever the belt is the binding constraint; open a full lane only when queues form before the belt. R3 (exchange 7) — segment lanes by traveler type (Pre-Check / express / standard), add a self-identified 'ready' express lane, meter the ID-check feed in small batches, and provide pre-divestiture staging so a slow traveler is buffered rather than blocking the belt; tailored for airports with many unfamiliar/leisure travelers. R4 (exchange 9) — treat Pre-Check capacity as a reallocation pool, not a fixed one: divert eligible passengers to Pre-Check to relieve the regular queue (each diversion leaves the regular line and divests faster), but do not over-staff Pre-Check beyond its ~45% demand or it consumes shared officers/machines/floor and the Zone-C exit. R5 (exchange 5) — pre-empt the transient capacity losses that cause the worst spikes: cross-trained floaters and on-call screeners to cover break rotations, shift change and callouts, and a standing surge protocol when a cluster of flights or a delayed-flight bank is detected. R6 (exchange 1) — staff the secondary (Zone D) resource explicitly: a dedicated secondary-officer pool sized to the alarm rate, with an escalation path, since secondaries are the dominant variance source. Validation summary: the four checks above; the model is internally consistent and its dominant mechanisms (secondary stalls, demand/capacity mismatch) match the expert's operational account. Strengths: captures the queue network and the variance mechanism, gives directly comparable what-if scenarios, and every empirical input has a recorded source. Weaknesses: single-checkpoint snapshot data; steady-state (no surge build-up dynamics); lognormal i.i.d. service (no fatigue/learning or correlated delays); shared secondary pool is a simplification; culture is a single scalar; no explicit cost model. Future work: fit the arrival process to a full-day multi-airport dataset to capture intra-day surges; model correlated alarm events and equipment faults as a compound Poisson shock; add a cost function (lane capital + labor) to do a true cost/benefit optimization of R1-R5; extend the culture dimension to arrival- and routing-level heterogeneity; and validate the DES against observed checkpoint time-stamps at multiple hubs.

### Outcome Analysis

The recommendations are ordered by leverage and cost: R1 (dynamic staffing) is the highest-leverage, lowest-capital change and is the one that reduces variance as well as the mean, consistent with the S3 dynamic-staffing scenario (p95 1690 s -> 1308 s) and the exchange-10 answer. R2 gives the cost rule for any capacity add; R3 is the tailored/cultural accommodation; R4 prevents the Pre-Check misallocation that exchange 9 warns about (the S4 scenario showed over-staffing Pre-Check worsens its own wait 651 s -> 926 s while barely helping the regular line); R5 and R6 target the two variance triggers (transient capacity loss and secondary stalls). Overall the model supports a staffing-flexibility-first strategy: the cheapest large win is using existing slack (the underloaded Pre-Check lane and on-call screeners) to track demand, and the single biggest variance win is giving the secondary (Zone D) resource dedicated capacity so an alarm does not cascade through the whole queue. Limitations carry through to the policy: the recommendations are qualitative on cost and assume staffing flexibility exists; without cross-trained floaters the binding constraint is headcount and no scheduling rule fully applies (the expert's own caveat).
