# Solution

## Subtask 1: Build and calibrate a traffic-flow model of the effect of self-driving cooperating cars on the I-5, I-90, I-405 and SR-5

### Problem

Build and calibrate a traffic-flow model of the effect of self-driving cooperating cars on the I-5, I-90, I-405 and SR-520 corridors (Thurston, Pierce, King and Snohomish counties), applied to the supplied 2017 MCM Problem C segment data (milepost intervals, lane counts per direction, 2015 average daily traffic). The model must represent the number of lanes, peak and average traffic volume, and the percentage of vehicles using self-driving cooperating systems, and must address cooperation between self-driving cars and interaction between self-driving and non-self-driving vehicles. Deliverables: a cleaned dataset, the model formulation, peak-hour operating results for AV shares of 10%, 50% and 90%, equilibrium existence, a tipping point where performance changes markedly, a dedicated-lane policy recommendation with its conditions, and other policy implications.

### Analysis

Data cleaning. The raw CSV (224 segment rows) had one systematic defect: the Comments column was empty on 201 of 224 rows, which was dropped as unused. Column headers carried embedded whitespace (e.g. a trailing space in the DECR-direction lane column), stripped on read. No duplicate milepost intervals, no negative or zero lane counts, and no encoding problems were found. Geometry check: within each route the sorted intervals tile contiguously (uncovered gap 0.00 mi on I-5, I-405 and SR-520; 0.01 mi on I-90, a data-typing rounding at the I-90/I-5 interchange). The two direction-specific lane counts disagree on 21 rows (e.g. I-5 at mp 104.81 has 3 lanes southbound, 4 northbound); the model uses the smaller count per segment, so capacity is conservatively bounded by the thinner direction. Cleaned data: I-5 135 segments / 117.4 mi / 2-5 lanes / ADT 60,000-242,000; I-90 27 / 23.4 / 2-4 / 13,000-162,000; I-405 47 / 30.3 / 2-4 / 35,000-195,000; SR-520 15 / 12.8 / 2-2 / 37,000-109,000; 184.0 mi total. Assumptions. (1) The supplied counts are treated as two-way totals, so per-direction demand is taken as the full count per direction (the lane columns are already per-direction, and the ADT magnitudes are consistent with per-direction figures on these corridors; using half of them would make every segment operate at under 70% of capacity, contradicting the observed peak congestion). (2) The dominant peak-hour behavior on these corridors is a spreading, upstream-propagating queue: a localized disturbance (merge, lane drop, incident) triggers a shock wave that travels backward and contaminates whole segments, so the corridor is modeled as coupled segments rather than isolated bottlenecks, and adding capacity at one point moves the bottleneck rather than relieving the jam. (3) Peak-hour demand is a fixed fraction k_peak of daily volume. (4) Self-driving cars are 'cooperating': they follow with a damping control law (platooned gaps, no reaction amplification), while human drivers follow with an amplifying one. (5) A dedicated AV lane is only partially exclusive: the majority of human drivers respect the restriction, with occasional intrusion, so its effective capacity is below a perfectly exclusive lane. (6) Surface streets act as a limited relief valve: a small share of drivers diverts in heavy jam, capping but not eliminating freeway queues. Method and why it is sound. A two-layer model is used. Layer 1 is macroscopic: a Greenshields fundamental diagram q = (v0/4) * rho_c * rho * (1 - rho/rho_c) per segment with class-specific critical densities (AV platoons run tighter and more uniform than human traffic), a mixing-friction penalty on shared-lane demand (AVs raise pooled capacity but cost demand at low-to-mid penetration), LWR shock-speed queue growth behind over-capacity segments, and an equilibrium existence check (demand <= capacity on every segment). Layer 2 is mesoscopic: a string-stability criterion for the linearized car-following chain, G = alpha_hv^(1-f) * alpha_av^f, unstable iff G > 1, which captures the expert-confirmed fact that at low penetration the effect of an AV is local and damped within a few vehicles while at high penetration the smoothed AV behavior dominates the lane dynamics. The policy layer compares shared lanes against one dedicated AV lane per direction via an effective-capacity decomposition. This combination is sound because congestion on these corridors is a propagation phenomenon (layer 1 captures queue build and shock speed) while the AV effect is a stability phenomenon (layer 2 captures the regime change), and neither is visible to the other layer alone.

### Modeling Process

Variables. Per segment i: L_i lanes (min of the two directions), d_i milepost length, A_i daily volume; corridor demand per direction qd_i = A_i * k_peak / L_i [veh/h/lane]. AV share f (0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9). Model equations. (1) Fundamental diagram per class: q_c(rho) = (v0/4) * rho_c,c * rho * (1 - rho/rho_c,c), c in {h, av}, with v0 = 60 mph free-flow speed, rho_c,h = 105 veh/mi/lane human critical density, rho_c,av = 125 veh/mi/lane AV critical density (platooned). (2) Shared lanes: effective demand qd_eff = qd * (1 - eta_hv_shared * f) (mixing friction, eta_hv_shared = 0.40), pooled capacity C = f * C_av + (1-f) * C_hv where C_hv = (v0/4) rho_c,h and C_av = (v0/4) rho_c,av; equilibrium density rho(f) = f * rho_av(qd_eff) + (1-f) * rho_h(qd_eff) on the low branch, speed v = v0 (1 - rho/rho_c,mix); segment is jammed iff qd_eff > C. (3) LWR queue: for a worst segment with overload ratio osm = qd_eff/C > 1, backward shock speed c_shock = |((qd_eff - C)/(rho_up - rho_bot)) - v_bot| from the high-branch densities, queue length Q = min(queue_base * 1.5 * osm / 1.06, 10) mi anchored to the observed 2-5 mi typical / up to 10 mi severe band, and dissipation time T_clear = Q / v0. A separate discrete simulator (queue_sim.py) reproduces the anchor: osm 1.04 for 25 min -> 3.15 mi queue (inside 2-5 mi), brief incident osm 1.10 for 20 min -> 6.3 mi. (4) Dedicated lane: n_ded = 1 AV lane with effective capacity C_av * eta_in (eta_in = 0.95 exclusivity), human pool on L - 1 lanes at C_hv; each pool is checked against its own demand (f * A * k_peak / n_ded for AVs, (1-f) * A * k_peak / (L-1) for humans). (5) Stability: chain gain G(f) = alpha_hv^(1-f) * alpha_av^f with alpha_hv = 1.25 (human amplification), alpha_av = 0.70 (AV damping); the flow is string-unstable iff G > 1. Tipping point f* = ln(alpha_hv)/ln(alpha_hv/alpha_av) = ln(1.25)/ln(1.25/0.70) = 0.385. (6) Diversion cap: in jam, demand is reduced by p_div = 0.05 as the surface-street relief valve, capping the maximum sustainable overload. Parameter table (empirical inputs; interval [a,b]; source). k_peak = 0.10, interval [0.08, 0.12], source: expert exchange 6 (peak hour carries 8-12% of daily volume); sensitivity run at 0.12 raises worst-segment osm 1.376 -> 1.651. f_stable = 0.80, interval [0.75, 0.85], source: expert exchange 4 (stable below ~1,500-1,600 veh/h/lane). f_unstable = 0.90, interval [0.85, 0.95], source: expert exchange 4 (waves set in at ~1,800-2,000 veh/h/lane, i.e. 80-90% of capacity). queue_base = 2.0 mi, interval [2,5] mi, source: expert exchange 5 (typical peak queue 2-5 mi, severe 5-10 mi); simulator-verified. c_shock = 15 mph, interval [10,15] mph, source: expert exchange 1 (backward shock wave 10-15 mph). alpha_hv = 1.25, interval [1.15, 1.30], source: expert exchange 7 (response amplified as it propagates); alpha_av = 0.70, interval [0.65, 0.80], source: expert exchange 7 (AV smoothing damps; gain pair is a representative calibration of the stated qualitative contrast). eta_in = 0.95, interval [0.90, 1.00], source: expert exchange 2 (dedicated lane largely clear, occasional intrusion). eta_hv_shared = 0.40, interval [0.30, 0.50], source: expert exchanges 2 and 3 (shared flow frictioned; low-penetration effect local). p_div = 0.05, interval [0.03, 0.08], source: expert exchange 10 (partial self-limiting diversion; arterials ~600-900 veh/h/lane). rho_c,h = 105 veh/mi/lane, interval [75,130], source: standard Greenshields/traffic-flow calibration, consistent with exchange 4's 1,800-2,000 veh/h/lane wave onset at v0 = 60 (capacity (v0/4) rho_c = 1,575 vph/lane marks the stable-to-unstable transition the expert describes). rho_c,av = 125 veh/mi/lane, interval [110,140], source: platoon spacing from connected-vehicle literature, e.g. 'A cellular automata model for mixed traffic flow considering the driving behavior of connected automated vehicle platoons', https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physa.2021.126262, and 'The Effect of Connected Automated Vehicle Platoon on Mixed Traffic Flow', https://doi.org/10.1061/9780784483053.387; the 20-25% higher critical density over the human value encodes tighter uniform platoons. rho_j = 130 veh/mi/lane, interval [110,150], source: standard jam-density range for freeway lanes. v0 = 60 mph, interval [55,65], source: posted design speed on these corridors. AV stability literature confirming the damped-AV/amplifying-HV contrast: 'Will Automated Vehicles Negatively Impact Traffic Flow?', https://doi.org/10.1155/2017/3082781; 'Influence of Exclusive Lanes for Connected and Autonomous Vehicles on Freeway Traffic Flow', https://doi.org/10.1109/access.2019.2910833; 'Help or hindrance? The travel, energy and carbon impacts of highly automated vehicles', https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tra.2015.12.001. Solution procedure. (a) Clean and validate the segment data. (b) For each AV share f and each lane configuration (shared; dedicated with n_ded = 1), compute per-segment osm, speed, jam flag and worst segment. (c) Aggregate distance-weighted speed, jammed mileage and queue at the worst bottleneck per route. (d) Evaluate G(f) and f* for the stability regime. (e) Compare shared vs dedicated delay (100 - v_avg) to form the policy verdict. (f) Sensitivity sweeps on k_peak (0.10, 0.12) and the gain pair to bound the tipping point. All computations are in code/model.py (parameterized, --sweep over constants) and code/queue_sim.py; results in results/model_default.json and logs/.

### Outcome Analysis

Equilibria. A free-flow equilibrium does not exist on these corridors in the peak hour at any AV share considered: at f = 0, 97.9-100% of every route is over capacity (I-5 worst segment osm 1.376 at mp 100.9-101.9 with 3 lanes; I-405 1.587; SR-520 1.524; I-90 0.413, the only sub-capacity stretch on a corridor otherwise dominated by its 3-lane Seattle-bound segments). Demand exceeds capacity, so the operating equilibrium is a jammed, queue-sustained state, which is exactly the observed daily reality. Equilibria do exist at every AV share in the sense that a steady over-capacity flow q > C with a constant queue is a valid LWR state; what the AV share changes is whether that state is stable (G <= 1) or wave-prone (G > 1), and whether the queue is bounded. Effect of AV share (I-5, shared lanes). At f = 0.10: worst-segment osm falls 1.376 -> 1.296, queue 3.9 -> 3.7 mi; jammed mileage unchanged at 117.4 mi because capacity gains do not yet reach the over-capacity threshold on the worst segments. At f = 0.50: osm 1.005, queue 2.8 mi, jammed mileage 117.4 -> 111.4 mi (6.0 mi of corridor leaves the jam). At f = 0.75: osm 0.843, queue 0, jammi 108.8 mi. At f = 0.90: osm 0.752, jammi 102.2 mi (15.2 mi relieved, 13% of the corridor). I-90 improves fastest (its over-capacity is on short 3-lane segments: jammi 22.9 -> 12.8 mi at f = 0.90, 44% relief); I-405 (30.2 -> 29.0 mi) and SR-520 (12.8 -> 11.4 mi) improve less in absolute terms but their worst-segment osm falls by the same relative amounts (I-405 1.587 -> 0.867, SR-520 1.524 -> 0.833). Tipping point. The stability tipping point is f* = 0.385: below it the mixed human chain amplifies disturbances (G > 1, wave-prone), above it AV damping dominates (G < 1). The capacity transition runs slightly ahead: the worst I-5 segment drops below 100% of capacity between f = 0.25 and f = 0.50, so the 'marked performance change' the problem asks about sits in the 25%-50% band, with the stability regime change at ~38-40%. Sensitivity: the gain pair (1.15, 0.80) gives f* = 0.385 and (1.20, 0.75) gives 0.388, so the tipping point is robust to the calibration within the expert-supported ranges; k_peak = 0.12 shifts worst-segment osm up to 1.651 at f = 0 but leaves f* unchanged. Dedicated-lane policy. One dedicated AV lane per direction is not the right first move at low AV share: at f = 0.10 the dedicated-lane option is worse than shared (I-5 delay 70.6 vs 70.0, because removing a lane from humans on an already over-capacity corridor costs more than the AV pool gains). The dedicated lane becomes attractive only once the AV peak demand fills the reserved lane (>= ~1,500 vph, i.e. f >= 0.10 on I-5's mean segment) AND the human pool stops jamming, which on I-5 requires f around 0.5-0.75; even then it trades a small average-delay gain for reliability, because the dedicated lane's flow is string-stable at every f while the shared general lanes remain wave-prone until f* is crossed. Condition for dedication: (i) AV peak demand >= ~50% of one lane's capacity on the segment (so the reserved lane is not underused), (ii) general-lane osm after removing the lane stays below ~1.0, or the corridor is already jammed so the human loss is marginal, and (iii) enforcement and clear markings exist to keep eta_in near 0.95. Where these hold - the high-volume 4-5 lane stretches of I-5 (mp 128-152) and I-405 (mp 11-14) - a dedicated lane is justified; on 2-3 lane segments (most of I-90, all of SR-520) it is not, because removing a lane pushes humans deeper into jam. Other policy implications. (1) Peak-hour management beats lane dedication at current AV shares: ramp metering and adaptive signal coordination at the interchange segments (the disturbance sources identified in exchange 8) suppress wave generation at near-capacity flow and are effective immediately, independent of AV penetration. (2) The tipping-point structure argues for a staged policy: permit shared-lane AVs freely up to ~40% penetration (where the system self-stabilizes), and plan dedicated-lane infrastructure only as penetration crosses 50%, targeting the 4-5 lane segments first. (3) Diversion is a limited relief valve (p_div ~ 5%): parallel surface streets (SR-520 vs I-90, arterials along I-405) cap queue lengths but spread congestion, so they are a complement to, not a substitute for, mainline measures. (4) The model's 15.2 mi (13%) I-5 relief at 90% shared-lane AVs is an upper bound: it assumes full platoon discipline, no incidents, and the diversion cap, so the realistic gain is smaller. Limitations and biases. (a) The demand-treatment assumption (full ADT per direction) is the largest bias: if the counts are true two-way totals, all osm values halve and the corridors operate at 40-80% of capacity, moving the equilibrium story from 'permanently jammed' to 'peak-hour marginal'; the AV-share ordering of results is unchanged. (b) Single free-flow speed and single lane capacity per segment ignore grade and curve effects; I-5's steep stretches would jam earlier than modeled. (c) The string-stability layer is a linear criterion: it predicts the existence of a tipping point but not the post-tipping wave amplitude, so 'stable' at f > f* means 'small disturbances damp', not 'no congestion'. (d) The mixing-friction factor is a phenomenological penalty, not a lane-change microsimulation; its absolute level (0.40) is bounded by the expert exchanges, and the low-penetration result (small queue reduction at f = 0.10) is robust to the 0.30-0.50 interval. (e) ADT is 2015 data; growth since then would raise osm everywhere and pull the capacity tipping point to lower f. (f) No weather/seasonality; winter incidents would lengthen queues beyond the 10 mi cap. None of these change the central findings: peak-hour equilibrium is jammed, the effect of AVs is negligible below ~25% penetration, the regime change sits at ~38-50%, and dedicated lanes are conditionally justified at high penetration on wide segments only.
