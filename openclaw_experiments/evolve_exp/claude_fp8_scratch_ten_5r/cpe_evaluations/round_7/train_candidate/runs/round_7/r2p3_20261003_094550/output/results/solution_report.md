### Problem

Develop a model that captures the flow of play as points occur and applies it to the 2023 Wimbledon final (Alcaraz vs Djokovic, match 2023-wimbledon-1701) and other matches. The model must identify which player is performing better at a given time and how much better, and provide a visualization (match-flow curve) depicting the match flow. The server's structural edge in winning points/games must be factored in.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) A 'point' is the atomic unit of play; the flow of play is a sequence of point outcomes. (2) The dominant mechanism of short-term form is serve dominance: the serving player wins first-serve points far more often than second-serve points, so raw point counts over-credit the server and under-credit the returner. (3) 'Momentum' is the deviation of a player's recent point performance from the serve-specific expectation, i.e. the part of their output not explained by who is serving. (4) Form is a short-memory phenomenon: it reflects roughly the last 5-15 points (about one to two games); shorter runs are indistinguishable from chance and longer spans are set-level trends. (5) A momentum lead is 'real' only once it exceeds the noise window; a 2-3 point cushion is not a lead. Method and why it is sound: an exponentially-weighted, serve-adjusted momentum metric. For each point we score the server's outcome against the serve-specific point-win probability (first-serve vs second-serve), producing a standardized residual; we then accumulate this residual over recent points with exponential decay so recent points weigh more. Standardizing by serve removes the known server confound (the problem explicitly warns that the server has a much higher probability of winning), leaving a clean 'who is actually playing better' signal. Exponential decay with a half-life of about 4 points matches the short memory horizon of form. The metric is bounded, signed (positive favors player 1, negative favors player 2), and monotone in the strength of the deviation, so it simultaneously says who is better and how much better (magnitude). The match-flow visualization is the time series of this metric; a local sign reversal with magnitude on both sides marks a swing in the flow of play.

### Modeling Process

Data cleaning: read Wimbledon_featured_matches.csv (7284 points, 30 men's matches, 46 columns). Repairs: converted numeric and categorical columns, parsed elapsed_time HH:MM:SS to seconds, confirmed no full-row duplicates, no missing values except 752 missing speed_mph (second-serve points have no serve speed, as expected), verified elapsed_time is non-decreasing within each match, and verified p1_points_won/p2_points_won are consistent with point_victor (one minor off-by-one flagged, not material). Scores use 0/15/30/40/AD for games and 0,1,2,... for tiebreak points; 99 tiebreak points were excluded from serve-rate calibration because their score encoding differs.

Serve calibration (from data, excluding tiebreaks): first-serve server point-win rate E1 = 0.755; second-serve server point-win rate E2 = 0.529. Per-match rates ranged 0.63-0.85 (first) and 0.39-0.61 (second).

Momentum metric. Let point i have server s(i) in {1,2}, serve number q(i) in {1,2}, and outcome o(i) = 1 if the server won the point else 0. The standardized residual for the server is z(i) = o(i) - E(q(i)), where E(1)=0.755, E(2)=0.529. Attribute to player 1: z1(i) = z(i) if s(i)=1 else -z(i). The serve-adjusted momentum for player 1 at point i is an exponential moving average: M(i) = (1-lambda) * sum_{j<=i} lambda^(i-j) z1(j), with lambda = 0.85 per point (half-life ~4 points, inside the 5-15 point form window). For visualization a 5-point centered moving average M~(i) is used. M is positive when Alcaraz (player 1) is outperforming his serve-adjusted expectation, negative when Djokovic is; |M| is how much better. A swing is a zero-crossing of M~ at which both the preceding peak and the following peak reach |M| > thr (thr = 0.08), which discards sub-threshold noise flickers.

Applied to 2023-wimbledon-1701 (Alcaraz=p1, Djokovic=p2), the metric reproduces the match flow: set 1 (Djokovic 6-1) momentum ends the set negative (M~-0.155, Djokovic); set 2 (Alcaraz 7-6) swings to positive (M~0.166); set 3 (Alcaraz 6-1) strengthens positive (M~0.251, the largest player-1 momentum of the match); set 4 (Djokovic 6-3) swings negative (M~-0.281); set 5 (Alcaraz 6-4) is the closest (mean |M| smaller). Nine momentum sign-swings were detected, including the three major set-to-set reversals. The per-set max |M| (0.214, 0.173, 0.311, 0.281, 0.273) quantifies how dominant each set's winner was. The full series is saved to results/match_flow_1701.csv (columns: t_sec, momentum_p1, momentum_smooth, set_no, server, point_victor).

### Outcome Analysis

The model identifies the leader and its strength at every point. In the featured match it correctly shows Djokovic commanding set 1, Alcaraz seizing sets 2 and 3 (with set 3 the strongest player-1 momentum), Djokovic retaking set 4, and a tight set 5 that Alcaraz closes out. Limitations: (a) the metric oscillates at game boundaries because serve alternates, so the raw series has a slow component tied to who serves; the 5-point smoothing and the dual-peak swing test mitigate but do not remove this, which is why set 5 (won by Alcaraz 6-4) still reads slightly negative at the very end. (b) Magnitudes are in standardized units, not a probability of winning the next point; they rank players but are not calibrated win-probabilities. (c) The metric uses only point outcomes and serve number, not shot quality, so it cannot distinguish a lucky point from a high-quality one. Bias: it is symmetric in the two players and treats a second-serve win as only weakly positive, which under-weights aggressive second-serve play.

## Subtask 2: A tennis coach claims momentum does not play a role and that swings in play and runs of success are purely random. Use t

### Problem

A tennis coach claims momentum does not play a role and that swings in play and runs of success are purely random. Use the model/metric to assess this claim.

### Analysis

The coach's null hypothesis is that point outcomes are independent draws (a run of success is just chance). Two tests are run on the data. Test 1 (run lengths): for each match, take the sequence of point winners, compute the longest run of consecutive wins by one player and the number of runs of 5+ points, and compare against the length a fair random process would produce (for n points, the expected longest same-outcome run is about log2(n), and the expected number of 5+ runs is about n/32). Test 2 (serve-degradation structure): if swings were random, a player's serve quality would be unrelated to whether they hold serve; we instead test whether hold rate depends systematically on double faults and break-point pressure within the service game. Assumption: a 'run' means consecutive points won by the same player regardless of who served; this is the object a commentator perceives as a streak.

### Modeling Process

Test 1: for each match compute longest run L and count C of runs of >=5 from the point_victor sequence; compare L to log2(n) and C to n/32. Test 2: for all 1188 service games across the 30 matches, group by double faults in the game (0/1/2/3+) and by break points faced (0/1/2/3+), and compute hold rate and break rate per group. If random, hold rate would be flat across these groups.

### Outcome Analysis

Test 1: observed longest runs (5-13) and 5+ run counts sit within, and are generally below or at, the random expectations (e.g. featured match: longest run 7 vs random ~8.4; 5+ runs 9 vs random ~10.4). Short runs of success are largely consistent with chance, supporting the coach at the level of brief streaks. Test 2: hold rate is strongly non-flat. Across all matches the hold rate falls from 87.3% (0 double faults) to 72.4% (1 DF) to 55.9% (2 DF) to 25% (3+ DF); and break rate jumps from 1.4% when no break point is faced to 63% when one is faced (51-54% at 2-3+). This systematic dependence shows that game-level flow is driven by serve degradation and break-point conversion, not by random chance. Conclusion: the coach is partly right and partly wrong. Point-to-point streaks of a few points are mostly random, but the larger swings in play (lost service games, set changes) are not random: they are caused by identifiable, repeatable mechanisms (serve slipping, break points). Momentum exists as a real, measurable, cause-driven phenomenon at the game/set scale even though it is not a mysterious force at the single-point scale.

## Subtask 3: Coaches want indicators that predict when the flow of play is about to change from favoring one player to the other. Usi

### Problem

Coaches want indicators that predict when the flow of play is about to change from favoring one player to the other. Using at least one match, develop a model that predicts these swings. Identify the most related factors, give advice for a player facing a different opponent, test on other matches, report prediction quality, identify factors missing when it performs poorly, and assess generalizability to women's matches, other tournaments, surfaces, and other sports.

### Analysis

A 'swing' in flow is operationalized as a break (a service game lost to the returner), because that is the discrete event that flips the advantage. The model predicts, before a service game is played (or as it unfolds), whether it will be broken. Factors tested, from expert guidance: (1) break points faced - the pressure point where the server tightens and the returner swings freely; (2) double faults in the service game - the signature of serve degradation; (3) first-serve fraction of the service game - low first-serve % forces attackable second serves. The training match is the featured final; test matches are five others with varied break counts (4 to 17 breaks) so the model is not fit to a single break rate.

### Modeling Process

For each service game compute, from the point rows: server, number of points n, first-serve fraction first_s, double faults df, break points faced bp_faced, and whether the game was broken (game_victor != server and game_victor > 0). Training on 2023-wimbledon-1701 (46 service games, 10 breaks) shows the factor separations: broken games averaged first_s 0.611 vs 0.666 held, df 0.500 vs 0.139, and bp_faced 2.600 vs 0.222 - break-point pressure is by far the most separated predictor. Prediction rule (combined): predict a break if bp_faced >= 1 OR df >= 2. This combines the pressure indicator (a break point has appeared) with the serve-degradation signal (2+ double faults, the expert-identified 'clear signal'). The first-serve fraction is used as a secondary/confirmation feature. Evaluation: accuracy, precision (of predicted breaks, how many were real), recall (of real breaks, how many were predicted) against the always-hold baseline (accuracy = 1 - base break rate).

### Outcome Analysis

Across 10 test matches (471 service games, 86 breaks, base rate 18.3%, always-hold accuracy 81.7%), the combined rule gives accuracy 86.4%, precision 57.9% (a predicted break is 3.2x more likely than the base rate), and recall 94.2% (it catches almost every real break, flagging it as soon as a break point appears or the serve degrades). Per-match accuracy ranged 81.4%-91.5%; the model is strongest on high-break matches (1310: prec 73.9%, rec 100%) and weakest on low-break matches where a single flagged break is a large fraction (1407: prec 30%). Most related factors: break-point pressure first (break rate 63% once a break point is faced vs 1.4% with none), then double faults (hold 87.3% at 0 DF vs 55.9% at 2 DF). Missing factors when it performs poorly: the rule uses in-game state only; it does not use (a) player-specific baseline tendencies (a server who is 40% up on first serves is already fragile before a DF), (b) score/match urgency (a break at 4-4 or for the set behaves differently), (c) serve speed and placement (speed_mph, serve_width, serve_depth are in the data but not used), (d) rally length and unforced-error rate as leading signs of a rally collapsing, and (e) fatigue/time (elapsed_time). Adding opponent-specific priors and match-state would raise precision on low-break matches. Advice for a player facing a new opponent: before the match, profile the opponent's first-serve percentage and double faults per service game and their break-point conversion from past matches - a rival with a low first-serve % or a DF-prone serve is structurally vulnerable and should be pressed on second serves; in the match, watch the opponent's first-serve % and second-serve count over the last game or two, because the turn is signaled by the opponent's own service game slipping, not by your own rise. Generalizability: the mechanism (serve dominance, serve degradation, break points, the 5-15 point window) is sport- and gender-independent in structure, so the model transfers to women's matches, other tournaments and surfaces, and rally sports like table tennis; however the empirical parameters - first- and second-serve point-win rates, the break base rate, and the length of the noise window - must be re-estimated on the new population, because women's tour serving is less dominant (more frequent breaks, higher rally variance) so the same structure produces more and noisier swings. It is not generalizable by reusing the calibrated numbers; it is generalizable by re-fitting them.
