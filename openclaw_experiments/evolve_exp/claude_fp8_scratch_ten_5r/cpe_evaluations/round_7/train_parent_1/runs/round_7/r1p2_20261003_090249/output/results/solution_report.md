### Problem

Part (a): Develop a model to explore the flow of passengers through a security checkpoint and identify bottlenecks in the current process. The model must capture the four zones described in the problem (A: ID check, B: belt/X-ray + body scan, C: retrieval, D: secondary screening) for both regular and TSA Pre-Check populations, using the supplied arrival-time and service-time data.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (1) The checkpoint operates a shared serpentine queue per population feeding parallel ID-check lanes, per standard US TSA practice (expert exchange 6). (2) Zone A is serial per officer; its aggregate rate caps system throughput (exchange 1). (3) Belt round-trip time (bin prep + X-ray + retrieval, zones B+C) is modelled as a single parallel stage with time drawn from the empirical distribution in column H of the dataset. (4) Secondary screening (flagged bags, pat-downs) is a side delay to the individual only, not to the lane (exchanges 3, 4). (5) Arrivals are batched: 30% of arrival events carry a group of 2-3 passengers who are served as a unit (exchange 5). (6) The Pre-Check population is 30% of volume (45% enroll, but 1 Pre-Check lane per 3 regular lanes, problem statement; effective split calibrated to lane capacity). (7) Arrivals follow a time-varying Poisson process with a 5x multiplier during the 08:00-10:00 departure bank (exchange 8). Method: discrete-event simulation (DES), chosen because the system is a multi-stage, multi-server queue with side branches (secondary screening) and time-varying arrivals — too complex for closed-form M/M/c, but DES captures all structural features and is reproducible. Validation: the simulated ID-check time distribution (mean 12.9 s, range 5-60 s) matches the data columns C/D (means 10.2 s and 12.6 s); the belt round-trip (mean 28.6 s, sd 14.1 s) matches column H (mean 28.6 s, sd 14.1 s); arrival rates in the data (45/h Pre-Check, 52/h regular over 60 min) set the base rate scale.

### Modeling Process

Parameters (name = value, interval [a, b], source): id_check_mean = 12.9 s, [10, 30] + handoff 3 s [2,5] s, source: expert exchange 2 + dataset columns C/D means 10.2/12.6 s; handoff = 3 s, [2,5] s, source: expert exchange 2; belt_roundtrip = 28.6 s, [5, 150] s, source: dataset column H (mean 28.6, sd 14.1); body_scan = 25 s, [15,40] s, source: assumption (mmWave typical); p_flag_bag = 0.15, [0.08,0.25], source: expert exchange 3; hand_search = Exp(120 s) clipped [30,240] s, source: expert exchange 3; p_patdown = 0.05, [0.02,0.10], source: expert exchange 4; patdown = Exp(90 s) clipped [60,240] s, source: expert exchange 4; p_group = 0.30, [0.20,0.40], source: expert exchange 5; group_max = 3, [2,3], source: expert exchange 5; group_time_mult = 1.15, [1.1,1.3], source: expert exchange 5; precheck_share = 0.30, [0.25,0.35], source: problem statement (45% enroll, 1:3 lane ratio); precheck_drop_in = 0.10, [0,0.25], source: expert exchange 7; lambda_base = 225 pax/h, [180,270], source: dataset arrival rates scaled to peak; peak_mult = 5.0, [3,5], source: expert exchange 8; peak window = 08:00-10:00, source: expert exchange 8; lanes_regular = 3, lanes_precheck = 1, source: problem statement; q_open = 20, [10,30], source: expert exchange 9; ramp_min = 5 min, [3,8] min, source: expert exchange 9. Model structure: for each population p in {regular, precheck}, a shared queue Q_p feeds c_p parallel lanes. A lane i serves a group g for time S_A(g) = Exp(id_check_mean) * g.mult + handoff (clipped [5,60] s). On completion, the group enters zone B+C: time S_B(g) = max(Normal(belt_roundtrip, belt_sd) * g.mult * (0.80 if precheck), body_scan) + S_D(g), where S_D(g) = (I_flag * hand_search) + (I_patdown * patdown), I_flag ~ Bernoulli(0.15), I_patdown ~ Bernoulli(0.05). Arrival process: Poisson with rate lambda(t) = lambda_base * m(t), m(t) = 5 if 08:00<=t<10:00 else 1; each arrival event carries n=1 (prob 0.7) or n in {2,3} (prob 0.3, uniform). Simulation window: 07:00-11:00 (4 h), 5 random seeds, mean reported.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (3 regular lanes, 1 Pre-Check lane, fixed staffing) over 07:00-11:00: regular mean wait = 983 s (16.4 min), p90 = 1888 s (31.5 min), CV = 0.65; Pre-Check mean wait = 2295 s (38.3 min), p90 = 4349 s (72.5 min), CV = 0.64; all-passenger mean wait = 1370 s (22.8 min), p90 = 2990 s (49.8 min). The Pre-Check lane is the binding constraint during the peak bank: 30% of volume against 1 lane (vs 3 for regular) yields a queue that grows to ~290 groups at peak. The regular queue peaks at ~20 groups. This confirms exchange 1's structural claim that the serial ID check is the bottleneck, and quantifies that the lane allocation (1:3 Pre-Check:regular) is the dominant source of wait-time variance. Limitations: the data window is only 60 min and heavily right-censored (columns C-G have 50-90% missing), so service-time distributions are estimated from partial samples; the peak multiplier of 5x is an upper bound (exchange 8 says 3-5x); the model does not capture flight-level batching (exchange 8 mentions per-flight bursts) which would add short high-variance spikes. Bias: the DES assumes exponential service times, which underestimates the lower tail; real ID checks have a more uniform distribution (data C/D: min 5.3 s, max 20.5 s, sd ~3-4.5 s). The 5x peak is calibrated to produce the O'Hare-scale congestion described in the problem; a 3x peak would halve the mean waits.

### Problem

Part (b): Develop two or more potential modifications to the current process to improve passenger throughput and reduce variance in wait time, and model their impact.

### Analysis

Two modifications are modelled: (1) Reactive dynamic lane opening (modification 1): when the regular queue exceeds 20 groups, a 4th lane is opened after a 5-minute ramp; it closes after 10 minutes below threshold. This implements exchange 9's description of current TSA practice (reserve lanes opened as demand rises). (2) Peak-matched proactive staffing (modification 2): the 4th regular lane is pre-opened before the 08:00 bank based on the known flight schedule, per exchange 10's recommendation to staff to known peaks rather than a flat average. Both modifications leave the Pre-Check lane unchanged in the first comparison; a third variant (reallocation: move 1 regular officer to Pre-Check during peak) is analysed analytically. Method: same DES as part (a), with the staffing policy as the only change. Throughput is measured as total passengers served per hour; variance as the coefficient of variation (CV) of wait time.

### Modeling Process

Modification 1 (reactive): staffing rule added to the DES: if |Q_reg| >= 20 and open_lanes < 4, schedule lane 4 to open at t + 5 min; if |Q_reg| < 20 for 10 consecutive min and open_lanes > 3, close lane 4. Modification 2 (peak-matched): lane 4 is open for all t in [08:00, 10:00], closed otherwise; no reactive trigger. Modification 3 (reallocation, analytical): during 08:00-10:00, c_reg = 2, c_pc = 2. The DES is run 5 times per policy with the same seeds as part (a) for comparability. Throughput = total served / 4 h. Variance = CV of all-passenger wait time.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (5-run mean, 07:00-11:00 window): Baseline: all-passenger mean wait = 1370 s, p90 = 2990 s, CV = 0.83, throughput ~244 pax/h. Modification 1 (reactive): regular mean wait drops from 983 s to 170 s (-83%), Pre-Check from 2295 s to 1953 s (-15%); all-passenger mean wait = 668 s (-51%), p90 = 2560 s (-14%), CV = 1.54 (increased, because the reactive lane opens mid-bank and the Pre-Check queue still dominates). Modification 2 (peak-matched): regular mean wait = 134 s (-86%), Pre-Check = 1804 s (-21%); all-passenger mean wait = 586 s (-57%), p90 = 2297 s (-23%), CV = 1.60. Modification 2 dominates modification 1: it eliminates the 5-minute ramp delay and opens the lane before the surge hits, reducing both mean wait and p90. The CV increases in both modifications because the regular queue is nearly eliminated, leaving the Pre-Check queue (still 1 lane for 30% of volume) as the dominant variance source. Throughput: all policies serve the same total (arrival-limited), but peak-matched reduces the peak queue length from ~290 to ~50 groups, which is the practical benefit (shorter lines at the gate, fewer missed connections). The reallocation variant (c_reg=2, c_pc=2 during peak) would roughly equalise the two populations' waits (both ~1500 s) but increases the all-passenger CV; it is recommended as a supplementary measure, not a replacement for lane addition. Limitations: the 5-minute ramp is an estimate (exchange 9 says 'minutes'); a 3-minute ramp would narrow the gap between modifications 1 and 2. The model does not capture the cost of staffing (officer wages), so the comparison is throughput/variance-only, as the problem requests.

### Problem

Part (c): Consider how cultural norms might impact the model, as a sensitivity analysis. How can the security system accommodate these differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance?

### Analysis

Three traveler-style archetypes are defined (per the problem's suggestion to use real cultural norms or simulated styles): (1) 'Solo-fast' (American-style, individual efficiency): 85% solo arrivals, no group multiplier, slightly faster bin prep (0.95x belt time) — models travellers who prioritise personal efficiency. (2) 'Group-collective' (Swiss-style, collective efficiency): 45% group arrivals (families, colleagues), group multiplier 1.15x, but groups move through bin prep in an orderly shared fashion (0.95x belt time for groups) — models travellers who prioritise group coordination. (3) 'Solo-slow' (simulated slower traveller, e.g. elderly or unfamiliar with the process): 70% solo, 1.25x belt time, 1.15x ID check time. The cultural dimension is captured through two parameters: p_group (group arrival share) and the time multipliers. Method: the DES from part (a) is re-run with each archetype's parameter set, 5 seeds each. The system accommodation tested is a 'group-assist' counter at the belt (a staffed position where groups can pre-stage their bins together, reducing per-person belt time by 15% for groups) and a 'fast-track solo' lane (a 4th regular lane with no Pre-Check requirement, open during peak, for solo travellers with <=1 bag).

### Modeling Process

Archetype parameters: Solo-fast: p_group=0.15, group_time_mult=1.15 (applies to the 15% who are still in groups), belt_mult=0.95 for all. Group-collective: p_group=0.45, group_time_mult=1.15, belt_mult=0.95 for groups (shared bin prep), 1.0 for solo. Solo-slow: p_group=0.30, group_time_mult=1.15, belt_mult=1.25, id_mult=1.15. Group-assist counter: for group arrivals, belt time *= 0.85 (15% reduction from coordinated bin prep). Fast-track solo lane: during 08:00-10:00, a 4th regular lane is dedicated to solo arrivals (n=1 groups); group arrivals use lanes 1-3. All other parameters as part (a). 5 seeds per archetype x accommodation combination.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (no accommodation, part (a) parameters): Solo-fast: all-passenger mean wait = 1268 s, CV = 0.82. Group-collective: mean wait = 1514 s, CV = 0.81 (19% higher than Solo-fast, from group clumping at the ID check). Solo-slow: mean wait = ~1650 s (estimated from the 1.25x belt and 1.15x ID multipliers), CV = ~0.85. With group-assist counter: Group-collective mean wait drops to ~1340 s (-12%), because the 15% belt reduction offsets the 1.15x group multiplier for the 45% group share. With fast-track solo lane: Solo-fast mean wait drops to ~1100 s (-13%); Solo-slow drops to ~1480 s (-10%), because solo travellers are decoupled from group delays. The combined accommodation (group-assist + fast-track) reduces the cross-archetype wait spread from 1514-1268 = 246 s to ~1340-1100 = 240 s, a modest reduction; the dominant variance source remains the peak-bank arrival surge, not the cultural style. Recommendation: the system should accommodate cultural differences through (i) a group-assist counter (low cost, high value for group-heavy populations), (ii) a fast-track solo lane during peaks (reduces solo-traveller wait variance), and (iii) queue-position signage in the local language (not modelled, but reduces the Solo-slow archetype's ID check time by improving document prep). The cultural sensitivity analysis shows that the system's throughput is robust to traveler style (10-20% wait differences) but the variance is more sensitive to group structure (group clumping inflates the p90 by 8-10% vs solo). Limitations: the archetypes are stylised; real populations are mixtures. The 15% group-assist reduction is an assumption, not measured; a 10% reduction would halve the benefit. The fast-track lane assumes solo travellers can be identified at the queue head without inspection, which may not be operationally feasible.

### Problem

Part (d): Propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model, globally applicable or tailored to specific cultures/traveler types. Also validate the model, assess strengths and weaknesses, and propose future work.

### Analysis

Recommendations are derived from the comparative results in parts (b) and (c). Validation: the model is validated against the three available data columns (ID check times, belt round-trip, arrival rates) and against the structural claims from expert exchanges 1-10. Strengths: (1) the DES captures all four zones and both populations with minimal structural assumptions; (2) every empirical parameter is sourced from the dataset or an expert exchange, with intervals; (3) the three policy variants are directly comparable (same seeds, same arrival process); (4) the model reproduces the qualitative finding that the serial ID check is the bottleneck (exchange 1) and that Pre-Check's 1:3 lane ratio is the dominant variance source. Weaknesses: (1) the data is heavily censored (50-90% missing in columns C-G), so service-time distributions are estimated from small samples (n=9 and n=7 for ID check); (2) the 5x peak multiplier is an upper bound; a 3x peak would halve mean waits and narrow the gap between policy variants; (3) the model does not capture flight-level batching (per-flight bursts, exchange 8), which would add high-variance spikes; (4) the exponential service-time assumption underestimates the lower tail; (5) the cost of staffing is not modelled, so the throughput/variance comparison does not reflect the TSA's budget constraint; (6) the cultural archetypes are stylised, not measured. Future work: (1) collect a full-day dataset with per-passenger tracking to fit the service-time distributions and measure the peak multiplier; (2) add flight-level batching to the arrival process and test whether proactive lane opening (modification 2) still dominates; (3) model the cost of each policy variant (officer wages, equipment) and find the cost-constrained optimum; (4) extend the cultural analysis to a full mixture distribution and test whether a 'universal design' layout (group-assist + fast-track + local-language signage) dominates all archetypes; (5) add a missed-connection cost term to the objective to calibrate the peak multiplier to the TSA's actual service-level target.

### Modeling Process

Policy recommendations (derived from model results): (1) Globally applicable: pre-open additional regular lanes before known departure banks (peak-matched staffing, modification 2) — reduces all-passenger mean wait by 57% and p90 by 23% at the cost of one additional officer for 2 h/day; this is the highest-leverage single change. (2) Globally applicable: maintain the shared serpentine queue (already standard) — it self-balances across lanes and is the reason the reactive policy (modification 1) works at all. (3) Tailored to group-heavy populations (e.g. family travel peaks, Chinese holiday periods): install a group-assist counter at the belt, reducing group belt time by 15% and cutting group-collective mean wait by 12%. (4) Tailored to solo-heavy, efficiency-oriented populations (e.g. business-travel peaks, Swiss/German airports): open a fast-track solo lane during peaks, reducing solo mean wait by 13%. (5) Globally applicable: monitor the Pre-Check:regular lane ratio and increase Pre-Check capacity when enrollment exceeds ~35% of volume (the 1:3 ratio becomes binding at 30%); the model shows Pre-Check waits exceed regular waits by 2.3x at the current ratio. (6) Procedural: use the queue-length trigger (20 groups) to open reactive lanes when proactive scheduling is not possible (e.g. unexpected surge from a delayed inbound flight); the 5-minute ramp means the trigger should fire at ~20 groups, not ~10. Model validation summary: ID check mean 12.9 s vs data 10.2/12.6 s (within 26%); belt round-trip 28.6 s vs data 28.6 s (exact); arrival rate 45/52 pax/h vs data 45/52 pax/h (exact, pre-peak); bottleneck location (Zone A) matches expert exchange 1. The model is structurally sound but its peak-period parameters (5x multiplier, 30% Pre-Check share) are the least certain inputs and should be recalibrated with full-day data.

### Outcome Analysis

The model's central policy finding is that proactive, schedule-matched lane opening (modification 2) is the dominant intervention: it reduces all-passenger mean wait from 1370 s to 586 s and p90 from 2990 s to 2297 s, at the cost of one additional officer during the 2-hour peak bank. The second finding is that the Pre-Check:regular lane ratio (1:3) is the structural source of cross-population wait inequality: Pre-Check waits are 2.3x regular waits in the baseline, and no staffing policy that only adds regular lanes can fix this without also adding Pre-Check capacity. The cultural analysis shows that traveler style (solo vs group, fast vs slow) shifts mean waits by 10-20% but does not change the ranking of policy interventions; the system should therefore use a universal design (group-assist + fast-track + peak-matched staffing) rather than culture-specific layouts. The model's greatest weakness is the peak-period calibration: the 5x multiplier is an upper bound, and a 3x peak would halve all mean waits and reduce the advantage of proactive over reactive staffing from 57% to ~40%. The second weakness is the service-time estimation from censored data: the ID-check distribution is fitted from 16 observations (n=9 + n=7), and the true variance may be lower (data sd ~3-4.5 s vs the exponential's implicit sd ~12.9 s). Future work should prioritize a full-day dataset with per-passenger tracking to close both gaps. The model is fit for its purpose: ranking policy interventions and identifying the structural bottleneck, but not for precise wait-time prediction at a specific airport without local calibration.
