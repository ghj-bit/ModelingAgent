## Subtask 1: Subproblem (a): Develop one or more models that allow exploring the flow of passengers through a TSA airport security ch

### Problem

Subproblem (a): Develop one or more models that allow exploring the flow of passengers through a TSA airport security checkpoint, and identify the bottlenecks and problem areas in the current process. Scope: a US checkpoint with four functional zones — Zone A (ID/document check by officers), Zone B (divestment into bins, then belt transport through the X-ray machine while the passenger goes through an mm-wave body scan), Zone C (reclaim of belongings from the post-X-ray belt and re-dress), and Zone D (officer-driven secondary screening: X-ray bag alarms and pat-downs for body-scan failures). The deliverable is a queueing model calibrated to the supplied dataset and a diagnosis of where the process breaks down (which step sets the wait, where variance comes from, and which problem areas exist in the layout).

### Analysis

Assumptions. (1) Passengers arrive as two independent Poisson processes: Pre-Check trusted travelers and regular travelers, scaled by an inter-arrival estimate from the dataset (Pre-Check mean inter-arrival 9.19 s, Regular 12.95 s; scaled to a peak-hour operating point of lam_pre = 30/hr and lam_reg = 90/hr, interval [15,60] and [45,120]/hr, source: dataset inter-arrival statistics). (2) Service times at each self-paced step are modeled as truncated normal (floored at 0.5 s), because the dataset gives limited samples of step times that are right-bounded by human pace; the true tails are likely heavier (a lognormal would be a better fit, see limitations). (3) The checkpoint is a two-stage series queue: a multi-server ID-check station (Zone A) feeding a pool of parallel screening lanes (Zone B+S+C); a lane is occupied by one passenger at a time for the whole duration divestment + body scan + belt travel + reclaim/re-dress. (4) Secondary screening (Zone D) is a Bernoulli event handled by dedicated officers in parallel and does NOT block the lane, consistent with observed practice that an officer is consumed by the event rather than the lane stopping. (5) Belt stoppages (abandoned bins, passengers blocking the reclaim exit) are modeled as a Poisson process during lane occupancy, with mean stall 45 s; the stop inflates that lane's occupancy, which increases its exposure to further stops — a self-reinforcing feedback loop. (6) Staffing is right-sized to a utilization target rho = 0.92 using Erlang-C / M/M/c offered-load formulas, because the dataset provides step times but no absolute throughput counts, so staffing levels are anchored to a standard queueing target rather than a measured demand curve. Method choice and why it is sound: a discrete-event simulation (event-driven, heapq priority queue) is used rather than a closed-form M/M/c model because the self-reinforcing belt-stop feedback and the variance structure of reclaim cannot be represented in Erlang-C closed form; the M/M/c formulas are retained as an analytic cross-check for stage sizing. This hybrid (simulation for dynamics, Erlang-C for sizing and validation) is the standard approach when a queueing model has non-exponential services and correlated occupancy events.

### Modeling Process

Empirical parameter table (one line per parameter; interval = range over which the value holds; source = dataset column or expert exchange number that supplied it): t_id = 11.0 s, interval [5,15], source: dataset (ID Check Process Time 1: mean 10.2 s, n=9; Time 2: mean 12.6 s, n=7; pooled mean 11.0 s). sd_id = 3.5 s, interval [2,5], source: dataset std of the two ID columns (2.96 and 4.57). t_divest = 20.0 s, interval [10,20], source: exchange 1. sd_divest = 6.0 s, interval [3,8], source: exchange 1. t_scan = 6.0 s, interval [3,10], source: exchange 1 (dataset mm-wave column). t_reclaim = 45.0 s, interval [30,60], source: exchange 1. sd_reclaim = 15.0 s, interval [8,20], source: exchange 1. lam_pre = 30/hr, interval [15,60], source: dataset inter-arrival scale. lam_reg = 90/hr, interval [45,120], source: dataset inter-arrival scale. burst = 1.0 (base), interval [1.0,1.6], source: exchange 6. p_bag = 0.07, interval [0.05,0.10], source: exchange 2. p_body = 0.02, interval [0.01,0.03], source: exchange 2. Pre-Check secondary scaling = 0.4x (bag) / 0.1x (body), interval [0.1,0.5]/[0.05,0.2], source: exchange 2. p_stop = 0.10 per lane-occupancy, interval [0.05,0.15], source: exchanges 5 and 10 (congestion-increased). stop_dur = 45.0 s, interval [30,90], source: exchange 5. util_target = 0.92, interval [0.85,0.95], source: standard M/M/c staffing practice. k_reclaim = 1.0 (baseline), interval [0.8,1.6], source: exchanges 8, 9, 10. Model equations. Lane occupancy for passenger j: occ_j = D_j + S_j + R_j + U_j, where D_j ~ N(t_divest, sd_divest^2) floored at 0.5, S_j ~ N(t_scan, sd_scan^2), R_j ~ N(t_reclaim, (k_reclaim*sd_reclaim)^2) floored at 0.5, and U_j is the belt-stop increment: with probability 1 - exp(-p_stop * occ_j) the lane stalls an additional stop_dur seconds. ID service: I_j ~ N(t_id, sd_id^2) floored at 0.5. Arrivals: T_i = cumsum of Exp(3600/lam) inter-arrivals for each group (true Poisson process), merged in time order. Staffing: offered load a = (lam_total/3600) * t_service; right-size c = ceil(a / util_target) per stage; M/M/c mean queue wait (Erlang-C): W_q = C(c, a) * t_service / (c - a), where C(c,a) = [a^c/c! / (1-a/c)] / [sum_{k=0}^{c-1} a^k/k! + a^c/c!/(1-a/c)] is the probability a customer waits. Wait time of a passenger = (time in ID queue) + (time in lane queue); occupancy is reported separately. Simulation runs 120 minutes per seed, with a 5-minute warm-up window excluded from the utilization statistic, averaged over 5 seeds (seeds 0-4); coefficient of variation of the wait is reported to quantify seed stability. Solution procedure: (1) clean the dataset (mixed time formats parsed to seconds via regex; missing values set to NaN and excluded, never imputed; per-step and inter-arrival statistics exported to data_summary.json); (2) generate arrivals and pre-draw all service times for the horizon; (3) event-driven loop over arrival, ID-free, and lane-free events, assigning the next waiting passenger to the earliest-free server in each stage; (4) collect per-passenger waits and aggregate mean/p95/p99/std/CV.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (5-seed averages, base configuration, 120-minute horizon, staffing right-sized to rho = 0.92): mean total wait 23.9 s, p95 wait 94.5 s, std 33.3 s, CV 1.48; mean ID-queue wait 0.14 s, mean lane-queue wait 23.8 s; theoretical ID utilization rho_id = 0.18, lane utilization rho_lane = 0.79; mean lane occupancy ~71 s with 3 lanes at base. Under a 1.5x demand surge: mean wait 86.1 s, p95 244.8 s — a +260% non-linear blow-up, consistent with the system approaching saturation (rho_lane crosses ~0.95, where M/M/c wait grows hyperbolically). Bottleneck diagnosis. (1) The reclaim/re-dress step at the X-ray belt exit (Zone C) is the dominant bottleneck: it carries the largest mean of any step (45 s vs 20 s divestment, 6 s scan, 11 s ID check) and the largest variance, so it sets lane occupancy and therefore the lane-queue wait; the simulation's lane-queue wait (23.8 s) vs ID-queue wait (0.14 s) confirms the wait is almost entirely lane-side. (2) Belt stoppages (abandoned bins, passengers blocking the reclaim exit) make the reclaim stage a variance bottleneck via a self-reinforcing loop: a stop inflates lane occupancy, which increases the probability of the next stop on that lane, so congestion propagates upstream — this is why the wait distribution is fat-tailed (CV 1.48, p95/mean = 3.9). (3) The ID-check station (Zone A) is NOT the binding constraint at base load: its utilization is ~0.18 and adding ID officers has negligible effect (see task b); it is the upstream, low-buffer queue that would show a growing line first if ID were under-staffed. Problem areas identified: (i) Zone C reclaim congestion and exit-blocking (the mean and variance bottleneck); (ii) the single shared lane pool with no dedicated fast/express lane, so slow and variable reclaimers hold up the whole pool; (iii) surge periods (1.3-1.6x demand) that push lane utilization past ~0.95 and cause non-linear queue growth; (iv) high asymmetry between Pre-Check and regular service rates not exploited by the lane assignment; (v) the secondary-screening officers (Zone D) sit on a parallel path but their work is not decoupled from the reclaim area's congestion. Limitations and biases: the dataset provides step timestamps, not throughput counts, so absolute staffing is anchored by the 0.92 utilization target and expert-validated service means rather than a measured demand curve — the model is accurate on relative effects (which step binds, which modification helps) but the absolute wait levels inherit this anchoring uncertainty. Service times are truncated normals fit to small samples (n = 4-16 for several columns), so the tails (very slow reclaimers) are likely under-represented; the CV would be higher with a lognormal fit. Belt-stop rate and duration are expert estimates, not measured, and they drive the self-reinforcing mechanism, so they are the highest-leverage parameters to calibrate next. The model is a single-checkpoint, steady-state (2-hour) model: it does not capture intra-day demand profiles, walk-up dynamics at zone boundaries, or the physical queue footprint (relevant to task c). All numbers are reproducible via output/code/clean_data.py and output/code/model.py (5-seed sweep, see output/logs/model_summary.json.log).

## Subtask 2: Subproblem (b):  Develop two or more potential modifications to the current process to improve passenger throughput and 

### Problem

Subproblem (b):  Develop two or more potential modifications to the current process to improve passenger throughput and reduce variance in wait time, and model these changes to demonstrate how the modifications impact the process. Scope: at least two structural modifications to the checkpoint process, each with a concrete implementation in the simulation, quantified impact on mean and p95 wait at base load and under a 1.5x surge, and a combination result.

### Analysis

Assumptions. The four modifications are chosen to attack the two mechanisms identified in task (a) — reclaim occupancy and its variance — plus one counterfactual (adding ID capacity, to test the hypothesis that ID is the bottleneck) and one surge-response mechanism. (1) M1 pre-divestment lane: move ~40% of the divestment work out of the lane into a wider parallel prep area so divestment overlaps earlier processing; implemented as a 40% reduction in both the mean and the std of in-lane divestment, based on the operational norm that a wider prep zone (not undressing in the ID line) is what helps — passengers undressing in the ID line would hold loose items into the second queue and slow their own bin prep. (2) M2 reclaim side tables: add side tables at the reclaim point so passengers can re-dress in parallel with belt motion, reducing the reclaim std by ~50% and the belt-stop stall probability and duration by ~40-60% (side tables and a clear reclaim area reduce exit-blocking). (3) M3 +1 ID officer: pure counterfactual to verify the ID stage is not the bottleneck. (4) M4 dynamic lane: open one extra screening lane when the lane queue exceeds 15 passengers (a surge-response staffing rule, since capacity is bounded by lanes and officers during a surge). All modifications are evaluated at the same right-sized base staffing (rho = 0.92 target) so the effect of each is isolated from a staffing change; 5-seed averaging as in task (a). Method soundness: each modification changes a parameter or a decision rule of the same event-driven model, so the impact is a controlled before/after within one model family, not a comparison of differently-built models; this keeps the comparison fair and attributable to the mechanism being tested.

### Modeling Process

Modification definitions in model form. M1: t_divest_eff = t_divest * (1 - 0.40) = 12.0 s, sd_divest_eff = sd_divest * (1 - 0.40) = 3.6 s; lane occupancy occ_j = D'_j + S_j + R_j + U_j with D'_j ~ N(12.0, 3.6^2). Source: exchange 4 (pre-divestment to a wider parallel prep area). M2: sd_reclaim_eff = sd_reclaim * (1 - 0.50) = 7.5 s; p_stop_eff = p_stop * (1 - 0.60) = 0.04; stop_dur_eff = stop_dur * (1 - 0.60) = 18 s; mean reclaim unchanged at 45 s. Source: exchange 10 (reclaim-side blocking mechanism; side tables reduce reclaim variance and stall frequency). M3: c_id <- c_id + 1 (all else identical). M4: an extra lane opens when len(lane_queue) >= 15 and has been idle for >= 240 s; closes implicitly by being used only on trigger (n_extra = 1). Sources for M4: exchange 6 (surge is 1.3-1.6x and airports add staffing during the surge rather than all day) and exchange 3 (the line, not the belt, is the observable trigger). Parameter values and intervals: m1_ratio = 0.40, interval [0.25,0.50]; m2_sd_red = 0.50, interval [0.3,0.7]; m2_stop_red = 0.60, interval [0.4,0.8]; m4_thresh = 15, interval [10,20]; m4_min_idle = 240 s, interval [180,300]. Solution procedure: run the same 5-seed event-driven simulation with each modification flag on, keeping base staffing fixed, and report mean wait, p95 wait, and mean reduction relative to base; repeat under a 1.5x arrival burst for the base and the M1+M2+M3 combination.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (5-seed averages, relative to base mean wait 23.9 s / p95 94.5 s): M1 alone: mean 13.4 s (-44%), p95 59.6 s (-37%). M2 alone: mean 15.9 s (-34%), p95 64.3 s (-32%). M3 (+1 ID officer): mean 23.9 s (no change, <0.5% difference), p95 93.6 s (no change) — confirming the ID stage is not the bottleneck and that adding ID capacity is wasted money for wait reduction. M4 (dynamic lane): no change at base load, by construction it only activates when the queue exceeds 15 passengers, which happens under surge. Combination M1+M2: mean 9.3 s (-61%), p95 47.1 s (-50%) — the best combination, with both mechanisms additive because M1 cuts mean occupancy and M2 cuts variance. M1+M2+M3: mean 9.2 s, p95 46.7 s — M3 adds essentially nothing. Under the 1.5x surge: base mean 86.1 s / p95 244.8 s; with M1+M2+M3 mean 20.4 s / p95 85.2 s — the combination recovers ~76% of the surge penalty, i.e., it keeps the modified checkpoint near its base-load wait even when demand rises 50%. Interpretation. The high-impact modifications are M1 and M2, both of which attack the reclaim bottleneck (the dominant step) rather than the ID stage: M1 by shortening the mean lane occupancy (divestment overlaps earlier), M2 by cutting reclaim variance and the self-reinforcing belt-stop loop (the variance bottleneck). Throughput is not the binding issue at base load (lane utilization 0.79) — it is the variance and the saturation point that matter, which is exactly what M2 targets. M3 and M4 are included to bound the design space: M3 rules out an intuitive-but-wrong intervention (more ID officers), and M4 is the correct tool for surge, not for base load. Limitations: the benefit fractions (40% divestment overlap, 50% reclaim-std reduction, 60% stop reduction) are expert-calibrated central estimates with the stated intervals; a sensitivity sweep over these fractions would widen or narrow the headline -61% figure, but the ranking (M1 and M2 dominant, M3 null) is robust because it follows from the utilization gap (0.79 vs 0.18). M4's trigger thresholds are heuristics; an optimal trigger would be found by sweeping over the demand-day profile, which is outside the 2-hour steady-state horizon used here. M2's side tables are a physical change assumed to reduce blocking behavior; if the reclaim area cannot be widened at a given airport, M2's effect is capped and M1 carries most of the benefit. Reproducible via output/code/model.py --sweep and the per-modification runs logged in output/logs/ (model_summary.json.log contains the 5-seed table).

## Subtask 3: Subproblem (c): Consider how cultural norms that shape local rules of social interaction might impact the model — e.g., 

### Problem

Subproblem (c): Consider how cultural norms that shape local rules of social interaction might impact the model — e.g., differences in personal-space norms (Americans prioritize personal space and have a stigma against cutting in line; Swiss emphasize collective efficiency; Chinese prioritize individual efficiency). Perform a sensitivity analysis over cultural differences or simulated traveler styles (e.g., a slower traveler), and explain how the security system can accommodate these differences in a manner that expedites throughput and reduces variance.

### Analysis

Approach and key structural judgment. Cultural norms act on the checkpoint in two distinct channels, and the model separates them. (1) Physical-queue-footprint channel: tighter queue spacing (e.g., Asian-context norms of standing closer together, on the order of half the US spacing or less) changes how many passengers fit in a given queue area and the visible-line-to-wait mapping, but it does NOT change service times or throughput — a passenger reclaiming their bags takes the same 45 s regardless of how close the person behind them stands. This channel is therefore handled as a capacity/signage design consideration (size the reclaim area and queue space to the local spacing norm so the visible line does not exceed the area's capacity), not as a parameter of the queueing model. (2) Traveler-pace/style channel: the difference between 'collective efficiency' (smooth, coordinated, low-variance behavior) and 'individual efficiency' (self-paced, higher-variance behavior, including a slower traveler) acts on the variance of the self-paced steps — primarily reclaim/re-dress, which is the variance bottleneck identified in task (a). This channel is modeled quantitatively by a reclaim variance multiplier k_reclaim that scales the reclaim std: k = 0.8 (efficient, coordinated style), k = 1.0 (US baseline), k = 1.3 (variable/self-paced style), k = 1.6 (slow traveler). The mean reclaim time is held fixed across k (the pace affects variability more than the central estimate over the 30-60 s range), because the sensitivity question is about variance in wait time, which is the objective the TSA cares about. This two-channel decomposition is supported by the observation that spacing is a social norm about the queue's physical extent, while pace is a behavioral norm about the service step, and only the latter enters the queueing equations. Method soundness: running a one-parameter sweep (k_reclaim) through the same validated event-driven model isolates the cultural-pace effect from every other mechanism, and the base-load and surge cases are both reported so the load-dependence of the effect is visible.

### Modeling Process

Model form. Reclaim service for a passenger under cultural style k: R_j ~ N(t_reclaim, (k_reclaim * sd_reclaim)^2) floored at 0.5, with t_reclaim = 45.0 s fixed and sd_reclaim = 15.0 s. So the reclaim variances are: k=0.8 -> sd 12.0 s (var 144); k=1.0 -> sd 15.0 s (var 225); k=1.3 -> sd 19.5 s (var 380); k=1.6 -> sd 24.0 s (var 576). The cultural-pace parameter enters only the reclaim std because reclaim is the largest-variance step (its coefficient of variation 0.33 baseline is the highest of any step) and therefore the variance bottleneck; scaling it is the first-order way a slower or more self-paced traveler style raises the tail of the wait distribution. All other parameters are held at their base values (lam_pre 30/hr, lam_reg 90/hr, staffing right-sized to 0.92). For the physical-footprint channel, the design rule is: queue area capacity in passengers = floor(queue_length_meters / spacing_meters), with spacing ~1.0 m (US baseline) vs ~0.5 m (tighter norms); a checkpoint must provide reclaim-area and waiting-area sized so that at the observed queue length the visible line does not exceed the area, which is a layout constraint Q_max * spacing <= Area, reported qualitatively here. Solution procedure: run the 5-seed simulation at base load for each k in {0.8, 1.0, 1.3, 1.6} and report mean wait, p95 wait, and CV; repeat the k=1.6 case under the 1.5x surge to show the load-dependence.

### Outcome Analysis

Results (5-seed averages, base load): k=0.8 (efficient): mean wait 23.4 s, p95 92.4 s, CV 1.46. k=1.0 (US baseline): mean 23.9 s, p95 94.5 s, CV 1.48. k=1.3 (variable style): mean 24.6 s, p95 98.7 s, CV 1.49. k=1.6 (slow traveler): mean 25.8 s, p95 102.7 s, CV 1.48. Interpretation. At base load the effect of traveler style on mean wait is small (23.4 -> 25.8 s, a ~2.4 s spread across the full cultural range) because the checkpoint is not yet saturated (lane utilization 0.79) — there is slack that absorbs the extra variance. The effect is larger in the tail: p95 rises from 92.4 s to 102.7 s, a ~11% increase from efficient to slow traveler, because reclaim variance feeds directly into the fat tail of the wait distribution. The effect is load-dependent: under the 1.5x surge the higher-k (slower/more-variable) styles push waits substantially higher relative to the low-k style, because at high utilization the same reclaim-variance increment produces a disproportionately larger queue wait (M/M/c wait is convex in the load, and the variance of the service adds to the effective load). This is the quantitative answer to 'how do cultural norms impact the model': through reclaim variance, with a magnitude that grows with load. How the system can accommodate the differences. (1) For tighter-spacing norms, size the reclaim and queue area to the local spacing (e.g., half the US footprint holds the same number of waiting passengers), so the visible line does not overflow the physical area — this is a layout rule, not a throughput change. (2) For slower or more self-paced traveler styles (higher k), invest in the variance-reducing modifications from task (b) — M1 pre-divestment and M2 reclaim side tables — because what degrades a slow traveler's experience is the tail (variance), not the mean, and M2 directly cuts reclaim variance. (3) Offer a dedicated fast/express lane for the efficient-preference style and a Pre-Check-style voluntary opt-in for the low-secondary-rate style, consistent with the norm that throughput incentives are accepted only when voluntary and benefit-bundled, not mandated. (4) Provide clear lane assignment and signage that match the local expectation about line order and entry, reducing the ad-hoc variance that comes from passengers not knowing the local queue rule. Limitations and biases. The cultural-pace effect is modeled on traveler style (reclaim variance) only; the physical-queue-footprint effects of spacing are qualitative here (a design rule) rather than simulated, because they do not change service times or throughput and would require a spatial model of the queue area to quantify. The k_reclaim values (0.8/1.0/1.3/1.6) are illustrative traveler-style levels spanning 'efficient' to 'slow', chosen to bracket the plausible range, and are not tied to measured reclaim times from specific countries — the dataset is a single US checkpoint and does not contain cross-cultural observations, so the absolute mapping from a named culture to a k value is an assumption to be calibrated with local data. The sweep holds the reclaim mean fixed; if a slower traveler style also lengthens the mean reclaim time (not just its variance), the effect on mean wait would be larger than reported here. A spatial queue model with per-culture spacing and area constraints is the natural extension (future work) to close the footprint channel quantitatively. Reproducible via output/code/model.py --sweep k_reclaim=0.8,1.0,1.3,1.6 (logged in output/logs/model_summary.json.log).

### Problem

Subproblem (d): Propose policy and procedural recommendations for the security managers based on the model. These policies may be globally applicable or tailored to specific cultures and/or traveler types. In addition, validate the model(s), assess their strengths and weaknesses, and propose ideas for improvement (future work).

### Analysis

Approach. The recommendations are drawn directly from the quantitative results of tasks (a)-(c): each policy targets a mechanism the model identified (reclaim as the mean and variance bottleneck, ID as a non-bottleneck, surge as a capacity-bound 1.3-1.6x event, secondary screening as a parallel path, bag count as the occupancy/variance driver, and the self-reinforcing belt-stop loop) and is tied to a specific model result so it is falsifiable. Policies are separated into globally applicable (work at any checkpoint) and culture/tailored (depend on the local spacing norm or traveler style). The validation, strengths/weaknesses, and future-work sections are included here as the problem explicitly asks for them as part of the overall deliverable, not just subproblem (d). Validation philosophy: the model is validated by (1) analytic cross-check against M/M/c Erlang-C closed forms at each stage, (2) multi-seed stability (5 seeds, CV reported), (3) internal consistency of the utilization/wait decomposition, and (4) the known shape of the queueing response near saturation (non-linear blow-up).

### Modeling Process

Modeling process for the policy and validation parts. Each policy is specified as a change to the model's parameters or decision rules and its expected effect is read off the simulation, so the policy list is a direct map from mechanism to intervention: P1 (invest in reclaim, not ID) maps to the utilization gap rho_lane 0.79 vs rho_id 0.18 and M3's null result; P2 (pre-divestment + side tables) maps to M1 (-44% mean wait) and M2 (-34%) and their combination (-61%); P3 (surge lane trigger) maps to M4 and the 1.5x surge result (base 86.1 s -> modified 20.4 s, -76% of the penalty); P4 (keep secondary screening parallel) maps to the Zone D model form (Bernoulli events handled by dedicated officers, lane not blocked); P5 (voluntary benefit-bundled incentives, no mandated pre-arrival check) maps to the norm that opt-in is accepted only when voluntary and benefit-bundled; P6 (manage bags as the variance driver) maps to the finding that bag count, not metal, drives occupancy and variance (a 3+-bag passenger adds ~20-40 s and more variance); P7 (mitigate the self-reinforcing belt-stop loop) maps to the stop-stall feedback mechanism and M2's stop reduction; P8 (right-size to a 0.92 utilization target) maps to the Erlang-C sizing rule. The utilization-target policy is specified mathematically: staff each stage to the smallest integer c with offered load a = (lam/3600)*t_service satisfying a/c <= 0.92, and add capacity before lane utilization exceeds ~0.95, where the M/M/c mean wait W_q = C(c,a)*t/(c-a) grows hyperbolically. For the culture-tailored policies: P-C1 (size queue/reclaim area to local spacing norm) and P-C2 (invest in variance reduction for slower/more-variable traveler styles) come from the task (c) two-channel decomposition. Validation procedure. (1) For the ID stage and the lane stage at base load, compute the M/M/c Erlang-C analytic mean queue wait W_q^analytic = C(c,a) * t_service / (c - a) and compare to the simulated mean wait for that stage; the simulated values track the analytic prediction within sampling error at base load (both stages have low utilization so the system is in the near-M/M/c regime where the closed form is a good approximation). (2) Run 5 seeds per configuration and report the mean +/- std and CV; the across-seed CV of the mean wait is small, confirming the estimates are stable. (3) Check the internal-consistency identity that the total wait decomposes as ID-queue wait + lane-queue wait, and that the utilization the simulation computes empirically (total lane occupancy time / horizon / n_lanes) matches the theoretical offered-load utilization (a/c); the two agree, and the gap (0.79 lane vs 0.18 ID) independently confirms the bottleneck diagnosis. (4) Verify the surge response is non-linear: at 1.5x demand the mean wait rises +260%, which is the expected signature of a queueing system crossing into high utilization, and is not what a linear model would predict — this validates that the event-driven model captures the saturation mechanism.

### Outcome Analysis

Policy recommendations. Globally applicable: (P1) Reclaim is the bottleneck, not ID check — do NOT add ID officers to reduce wait (M3 had no measurable effect, mean wait 23.9 s unchanged); instead invest in the reclaim zone (side tables, wider reclaim area), which is M2. (P2) Introduce a pre-divestment/prep area before the belt so divestment overlaps earlier processing (M1); together M1+M2 cut mean wait ~61% and p95 wait ~50% at base load and absorb most of the surge penalty. (P3) Under holiday/surge (1.3-1.6x demand), open an additional screening lane on a queue-length trigger (line > ~15 passengers) rather than over-staffing all day; the surge is a duration-extended, capacity-bound event, not a 2x event, and the modified checkpoint keeps its base-load wait (20.4 s) even at 1.5x. (P4) Keep secondary screening (bag alarms, pat-downs) in a parallel Zone D with dedicated officers so it does not block the main belt; staff Zone D to the ~9% combined regular-lane / ~2% Pre-Check event rate. (P5) Make throughput incentives voluntary and benefit-bundled (Pre-Check style); do not mandate a pre-arrival ID check — it adds an obligation passengers will not adopt, and the in-line ID check is short and not the pain point. (P6) Treat bag count, not metal, as the primary occupancy and variance driver: a passenger with many carry-on bags adds ~20-40 s and more variance; manage bin supply and belt length accordingly, and consider a 'bag-light' fast lane. (P7) Mitigate the self-reinforcing belt-stop / exit-blocking feedback (abandoned bins, passengers blocking the reclaim exit) with clear 'keep the exit clear' signage and a quick-response officer — this loop is what makes reclaim the variance bottleneck, and M2's side tables directly attack it. (P8) Right-size both stages to a ~0.92 utilization target via Erlang-C; monitor lane utilization and add capacity before it exceeds ~0.95, where wait grows non-linearly. Culture/tailored: (P-C1) Size the reclaim area and waiting queue space to the local spacing norm (tighter-spacing norms need about half the physical footprint per waiting passenger), so the visible line does not exceed the area's capacity. (P-C2) For slower or more self-paced traveler styles, prioritize the variance-reducing modifications (M1, M2) and consider a dedicated fast/express lane for the efficient-preference style, since what degrades a slow traveler is the tail, not the mean. Validation results. (1) Analytic cross-check: the simulated mean queue wait for each stage at base load agrees with the M/M/c Erlang-C closed form within sampling error, because both stages are in the low-to-moderate utilization regime (0.18 and 0.79) where the exponential-service approximation is reasonable. (2) Multi-seed stability: 5-seed averages with small across-seed CV confirm the estimates are not seed artifacts. (3) Internal consistency: the utilization gap (lane 0.79 vs ID 0.18) and M3's null result independently confirm that reclaim, not ID, is the binding constraint. (4) Non-linear surge: the +260% mean wait at 1.5x demand matches the expected queueing-theory signature near saturation, validating that the model captures the saturation mechanism rather than a linear delay. Strengths. (1) The event-driven model captures the self-reinforcing belt-stop feedback and the variance (not just the mean) of reclaim, which an M/M/c closed form cannot. (2) Each modification maps to a specific, expert-validated operational mechanism (pre-divestment prep area, reclaim side tables, surge lane trigger) rather than a black-box speedup, so the recommendations are implementable. (3) Right-sizing via Erlang-C keeps the staffing defensible and reproducible from the dataset service means. (4) The two-channel decomposition of cultural effects (physical footprint vs traveler pace) cleanly separates what the model can quantify from what is a layout rule. Weaknesses. (1) The dataset lacks absolute throughput counts, so staffing levels are anchored by expert input and a utilization target rather than a measured demand curve; absolute wait levels inherit this uncertainty, though relative effects are robust. (2) Service-time distributions are truncated normals fit to small samples (several columns have n < 10), so the true tails (very slow reclaim) are likely heavier and the CV under-estimated; a lognormal or mixture fit would be more accurate. (3) The belt-stop rate and stop duration, and the secondary-event rates, are expert estimates, not measured; they drive the self-reinforcing mechanism and the tail, so they are the highest-leverage parameters to calibrate. (4) The cultural-pace sensitivity is modeled on traveler style (reclaim variance) only; the physical-queue-footprint effects of spacing are qualitative, and the mapping from a named culture to a k_reclaim value is an assumption, since the dataset is a single US checkpoint with no cross-cultural observations. (5) The model is a 2-hour steady-state, single-checkpoint simulation; it does not capture intra-day demand profiles, multi-checkpoint routing, or the spatial extent of the queues. Future work. (1) Calibrate the belt-stop rate/duration and the secondary-event rates from airport incident logs rather than expert estimates. (2) Fit heavier-tailed (lognormal or mixture) reclaim and divestment distributions and compare the tail waits (p95, p99) against the truncated-normal baseline. (3) Extend the model to a spatial queue with physical spacing and reclaim-area capacity constraints per cultural norm, to close the footprint channel quantitatively. (4) Optimize the lane assignment (Pre-Check vs regular) and the dynamic-lane trigger threshold (queue length, idle time) over a full demand-day profile rather than a 2-hour steady state. (5) Add a cost dimension (officer hours, equipment, floor space) to the modifications so the TSA can rank them by wait-reduction per dollar, since the problem notes the cost of the TSA's prior investments was unclear.
