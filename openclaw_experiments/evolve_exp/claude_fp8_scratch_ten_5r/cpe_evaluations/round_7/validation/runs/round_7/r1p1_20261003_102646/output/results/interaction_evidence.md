# Expert Interaction Evidence — Task 2002_C (Florida Scrub Lizard)

Ten exchanges, one question each. Each reply was turned into a model parameter,
constraint, or decision rule before the next exchange. Values are integrated into
the model; expert sentences are not copied into the submission.

| # | Branch | Question (short) | Expert answer (gist) | How it changed the work |
|---|--------|------------------|----------------------|------------------------|
| 1 | Structural | Is a patch's population self-sustaining or reliant on immigrants? | Mostly self-sustaining; only ~10% of juveniles migrate, adults don't; small/marginal patches can lean on neighbors. | Confirmed the per-patch viability logic: viability is set by local carrying capacity and local growth, with immigration only a modest supplement that rescues small patches. |
| 2 | Parameter | ~How many lizards in a patch to survive on its own? | No precise threshold (empirical): on the order of a few hundred, ~200–500 breeding individuals. | Adopted MVP = 200 lizards (low end) as the self-sustaining threshold for Task 5 viability. |
| 3 | Structural | ~How often are scrub areas actually burned? | Managed burns ~5–15 yr, target 8–12 yr; historically 5–20 yr; at 6%/yr a patch closes in ~15–20 yr. | Set the burning-policy target interval (8–12 yr) and the no-fire closure horizon (~15–20 yr) for Task 6. |
| 4 | Parameter | Does a normal-cycle fire wipe out the lizards? | No — a minority of the population; survivors recolonize in a season or two. Risk is wrong fire regime (too hot/frequent) or no unburned refuge. | Fire mortality kept as a minority loss, not a reset; policy must preserve unburned refugia. |
| 5 | Boundary | What if a patch never gets burned? | Population declines to extinction; patch closes in ~15–20 yr at 6%/yr; small patches go first. | Quantified the consequence of inaction; supports a burn interval well under the closure time. |
| 6 | Structural | Biggest driver of habitat loss? | Direct conversion to residential/commercial/agricultural development on the sought-after sandy uplands; fire suppression is the main *degradation* driver, second to conversion; fragmentation interacts. | Framed Task 1: conversion = loss, suppression = degradation; recommendations target both. |
| 7 | Parameter | What stands in the way of protecting patches? | Private ownership/property rights, cost of acquisition, fragmentation into small platted tracts, county-level growth politics, competing uses. | Task 1 obstacles list and the design of the acquisition/easement recommendation. |
| 8 | Structural | Does migration success fall off faster with distance? | Yes — steeper than linear, roughly exponential; each meter multiplies risk. | Confirmed the exponential survival model P(D) = 0.10·exp(−D/80.5 m) for Task 4. |
| 9 | Parameter | ~How often to burn a small isolated patch? | Same 5–15 yr cycle, lean to the shorter end, in small patchy rotating strips (create internal refuge). | Task 6 rule for small/isolated patches: shorter interval + patchy strips, never whole-patch. |
| 10 | Boundary | When to stop burning a patch? | When it has closed to dense oak/palmetto (hot fire, no sandy refuge), is small/isolated, population ≤ a few tens, or fuels are uncontrollable — use mechanical treatment first. | Task 6 exception rule: defer burning and restore open sand mechanically in those cases. |
