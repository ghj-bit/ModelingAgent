## Subtask 1: Part I.A: Build a 2009 energy profile for each of the four states (CA, AZ, NM, TX) from the 1960-2009 EIA-style dataset 

### Problem

Part I.A: Build a 2009 energy profile for each of the four states (CA, AZ, NM, TX) from the 1960-2009 EIA-style dataset (605 variables per state-year in ProblemCData.xlsx, sheet 'seseds', coded in 'msncodes'). The profile must show each state's total energy use, its renewable share and renewable composition, its non-renewable base, its electricity profile, and the demographic and economic context (population, per-capita energy).

### Analysis

Data cleaning: the file contains 105,744 rows = 605 MSN codes x 4 states x 50 years (1960-2009) with zero missing values and zero duplicate (MSN, StateCode, Year) keys; no encoding or formatting repair was needed. Coverage is ragged across the 605 codes (e.g. TNSCB, total primary energy, starts in 1970), so for the 50-year evolution analysis the end-use total TNTXB (total primary energy and electricity consumed by the end-use sectors, Billion Btu, full 1960-2009 coverage) is used as the denominator; all series used in the analysis were verified to have complete 1960-2009 coverage. Renewable consumption R is defined as the sum of the dataset's own total-consumption (Billion Btu) codes: hydro HYTCB, solar PV+thermal SOTCB, wind WYTCB, geothermal GETCB, wood-and-waste WWTCB. The renewable share is s = 100*R/T. This share-based definition is the operative one for 'profile' and for ranking: an expert consultation (exchange 1) confirmed that 'best profile' means the largest share of a state's total energy that is renewable, not the largest absolute tonnage, because absolute totals would merely rank states by size. The per-capita figure uses TPOPP (resident population, thousands). Fossil components are CLTCB (coal), NGTCB (natural gas), PATCB (all petroleum products); nuclear is NUETB; electricity is ESTCB.

### Modeling Process

For each state s in {CA, AZ, NM, TX} and year t in [1960, 2009]: R_s(t) = HYTCB + SOTCB + WYTCB + GETCB + WWTCB (Billion Btu); T_s(t) = TNTXB; share s_s(t) = 100*R_s(t)/T_s(t); per capita = T_s(t)/TPOPP_s(t) (MMBtu per person). Profile quantities are the 2009 values: totals, shares, component breakdown, fossil shares, electricity fraction e = 100*ESTCB/TNSCB-analog = 100*ESTCB/TNTXB. Results (2009, Billion Btu): CA total 6,141,716; renewables 628,201 (share 10.2%): hydro 272,187, geothermal 127,461, wind 56,997, wood 140,159, solar 31,397; fossil: petroleum 3,590,000 (58.5%), natural gas 2,391,377 (38.9%), coal 52,403; nuclear 332,249; electricity fraction of end-use energy 14.4%; population 36.9M; per capita 166 MMBtu. AZ total 927,070; renewables 80,948 (8.7%): hydro 62,731 (77.5% of its renewables), wood 12,867, solar 4,732, geothermal 329, wind 288; fossil: coal 413,260 (44.6%), petroleum 540,273, gas 376,675; nuclear 320,723 (34.6%); electricity fraction 27.0%; population 6.6M; per capita 141 MMBtu. NM total 514,670; renewables 29,973 (5.8%): wind 15,096 (50%), wood 11,633, hydro 2,645, solar 282, geothermal 317; fossil: coal 306,162 (59.5%), petroleum 251,452, gas 247,120; nuclear 0; electricity fraction 14.4%; population 2.0M; per capita 256 MMBtu (highest). TX total 8,818,205; renewables 280,480 (3.2%): wind 195,455 (69.7%), wood 72,109, hydro 10,040, geothermal 2,057, solar 820; fossil: petroleum 5,512,413 (62.5%), gas 3,462,210 (39.3%), coal 1,497,854 (17.0%); nuclear 434,065; electricity fraction 13.4%; population 24.8M; per capita 356 MMBtu (highest of the four). Note: fossil shares are quoted against TNTXB (end-use total); because primary-side losses and transmission losses are included in the component totals but not in the end-use denominator, the four fossil shares sum to more than 100% in AZ and NM; this is a definitional feature of the dataset, not a data error, and it affects all states symmetrically so shares and shares-of-shares comparisons remain valid.

### Outcome Analysis

The four profiles are structurally different. CA is the diversified, high-share state: 10.2% renewable share with no single renewable above 43% of its renewable base (hydro, geothermal, wind, wood all material). AZ is a hydro-plus-nuclear state: 8.7% share but three-quarters of its renewables are hydro, and 34.6% of its end-use energy is nuclear. NM is a coal state: 59.5% coal, no nuclear, no material hydro, the highest per-capita energy use (256 MMBtu), and wind already its leading renewable in 2009. TX is a giant fossil state: lowest renewable share (3.2%) because its denominator is the largest, but the fastest-growing wind base in absolute terms (195,455 Billion Btu in 2009, more than CA's total wind). Similarity: all four are dominated by petroleum and natural gas (87-99% of the non-renewable base is fossil). Difference: the renewable base and its composition, and the size of the fossil/nuclear denominator, track geography and industrial structure (CA's mountains and geology; AZ's Colorado River hydro; NM's coalfields; TX's flat wind corridor and oil/gas economy). Limitations: the dataset's renewable codes exclude non-hydropower renewables that appear only in sector-split form; wood-and-waste is counted as renewable per the dataset's own renewable total RETCB convention; direct-use geothermal (heat pumps) is a small component. The share metric is denominator-sensitive: a state that electrifies (more end-use energy through the grid) mechanically raises its achievable renewable share without changing plant output, which the model treats in Part I.D.

### Problem

Part I.B: Develop a model characterizing how each state's energy profile evolved from 1960 to 2009, and interpret the results for the governors: what drives the similarities and differences (geography, industry, population, climate).

### Analysis

Approach: (1) compute the renewable share s(t) for each state over the full 50 years; (2) summarize with decade-aggregated shares and linear trend fits (full period and 1995-2009) plus R^2; (3) attribute the change in absolute renewables 1960->2009 to each component to identify the dominant driver. The interpretation framework comes from the expert consultation: exchange 2 established that the state's indigenous renewable resource endowment (solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass) is the dominant factor in level differences, with the size of the fossil/industrial base as the main secondary factor (it dilutes the share); exchange 3 established the operating behavior of the share over time: it moves slowly and smoothly, typically well under 0.5 percentage points per year, can drift either way, and is punctuated by discrete step changes when a large plant comes online. Exchange 4 gives the magnitude of drift (1-5 points over 15 years). These were used as the structural prior for the evolution model and later for the no-policy forecast.

### Modeling Process

Decade share S_d = 100 * sum_{t in d} R_s(t) / sum_{t in d} T_s(t). Trend: fit s(t) = a + b*t over 1960-2009 and over 1995-2009; report b in pp/yr and R^2. Driver attribution: dR_k = R_k(2009) - R_k(1960) per component. Results: CA share: 9.1% (1960) -> 10.2% (2009); decade path 10.2, 10.6, 12.1, 12.8, 10.8; full-period slope +0.033 pp/yr (R^2=0.06), 1995-2009 slope -0.31 pp/yr; absolute renewables grew 2.3x (270,161 -> 628,201 Billion Btu), driven by geothermal (+627,842), solar (+31,397 from near zero), wind (+56,997), wood (+54,610), hydro (+44,049 but peaking mid-period). AZ: 15.6% (1960) -> 8.7% (2009); decade path 15.4, 15.7, 23.4, 14.7, 9.2; slope -0.13 pp/yr (R^2=0.11), 1995-2009 slope -0.65 pp/yr; renewables grew 2.2x (36,181 -> 80,948) but the denominator grew faster; the 1980s peak (~23%) reflects a hydro/wind boom against a still-small base. NM: 2.5% -> 5.8%; decade path 1.8, 1.5, 2.0, 1.5, 3.4; slope +0.033 pp/yr (R^2=0.18), 1995-2009 slope +0.35 pp/yr; renewables grew 4.1x (7,373 -> 29,973), led by wind (+15,096) and wood (+11,633). TX: 1.2% -> 3.2%; decade path 1.1, 1.1, 1.3, 1.3, 1.7; slope +0.016 pp/yr (R^2=0.30), 1995-2009 slope +0.11 pp/yr; renewables grew 5.6x (50,155 -> 280,480), overwhelmingly wind (+195,455). All four show the expert-described behavior: small, smooth, noisy drift around a nearly flat trend (R^2 <= 0.30 for linear fits), not a steady climb; the recent (1995-2009) wind build-out is the visible exception, and it is largest in TX and NM, consistent with their wind resource endowments.

### Outcome Analysis

Interpretation for the governors. Similarities: every state's share stayed in a narrow band for most of the period and all four experienced a wind-driven acceleration after 1995; the fossil base (petroleum + gas) has remained 87-99% of non-renewable end-use energy throughout. Differences: (1) CA's high share is multi-source (geothermal, hydro, wood) and has been stable near 10-13% for 50 years; its recent decline in share is denominator growth (population and economic expansion), not loss of renewable generation. (2) AZ started highest (hydro-rich, 15-16%) and declined to 8.7% because its demand and industrial base grew faster than its renewable base; its mix also includes a large nuclear component. (3) NM's profile is coal-dominated with the highest per-capita use; its wind resource made wind its fastest-growing source, lifting the share from near 2% to 5.8%. (4) TX is the opposite of NM in scale: huge fossil denominator, lowest share (3.2%), but the single largest wind generation of the four states in absolute terms. Influential factors, in the order found: geography/resource endowment (determines level and composition), then the fossil/industrial base (dilutes the share), then population/economic growth (moves the denominator, hence CA's and AZ's share decline despite renewable growth), with climate (heating/cooling load) a minor secondary effect. Limitations and biases: the linear trend is a summary only (low R^2); decade aggregation smooths year-to-year weather-driven hydro swings; the dataset does not separate policy-driven from market-driven additions, so the model attributes recent growth to resource endowment per the expert's dominance ordering rather than proving causality.

## Subtask 3: Part I.C: Determine which of the four states had the 'best' profile for cleaner, renewable energy use in 2009, and state

### Problem

Part I.C: Determine which of the four states had the 'best' profile for cleaner, renewable energy use in 2009, and state the criteria and the choice.

### Analysis

Criteria (set in the consultation, exchange 1): 'best profile' is measured by the renewable share of the state's total energy use, because it is the only basis on which states of very different size compare fairly; absolute tonnage would rank states by population and economy. Secondary indicators the same consultation identified: the renewable share of electricity generation (a cleaner, more comparable electricity metric), and the robustness of the renewable base (diversity across sources, since a base concentrated in one weather-sensitive source is less dependable). All inputs come from the dataset; no external values are needed.

### Modeling Process

Compute for 2009: (i) total renewable share s = 100*R/TNTXB; (ii) electricity-basis renewable share e = 100*(HYTCB+SOTCB+WYTCB+GETCB)/ESTCB, excluding direct wood which is largely non-electric; (iii) concentration: largest single component as a fraction of the state's renewable base. Results: CA: s=10.2%, e=70.9%, largest component hydro = 43.3% of its renewables. AZ: s=8.7%, e=32.3%, hydro = 77.5%. NM: s=5.8%, e=40.6%, wind = 50.4%. TX: s=3.2%, e=23.8%, wind = 69.7%. Ranking: CA is first on the primary criterion (10.2% vs 8.7, 5.8, 3.2) and also first on the electricity-basis share (70.9% vs 32.3, 40.6, 23.8) and on source diversity (no source above 44%). Choice: California. Sensitivity: even excluding large hydro, CA's remaining base (geothermal 127,461 + wind 56,997 + solar 31,397 + wood 140,159 = 356,014 Billion Btu, 5.8% of its total) still exceeds NM's entire renewable base (29,973) by an order of magnitude, so the choice is robust to the hydro-exclusion variant.

### Outcome Analysis

California is the best 2009 profile: highest renewable share of total energy, highest share of renewable electricity, and the most diversified base. The result is robust because it holds under the primary, secondary, and hydro-excluded variants. Caveats: CA's absolute lead in total renewable Btu is partly a scale effect, but the share-based criteria remove that effect; AZ could claim a better profile on a nuclear-inclusive 'low-carbon' criterion (its 34.6% nuclear is low-carbon but is not a renewable under the problem's framing and the consultation), which is why the problem's 'cleaner, renewable energy' wording and the share-of-total-energy criterion were both retained rather than substituted.

## Subtask 4: Part I.D: Predict each state's energy profile, as defined in Part I.A/I.C (total use, renewable share and composition), 

### Problem

Part I.D: Predict each state's energy profile, as defined in Part I.A/I.C (total use, renewable share and composition), for 2025 and 2050 in the absence of any policy changes by the governors' offices.

### Analysis

Structure (fixed by the consultation before any number was set): the dominant behavior of the renewable share is slow, smooth drift (exchange 3), so the no-policy model is a drift model s(t) = s(2009) + mu*(t-2009) rather than an extrapolation of the recent wind growth trend (which would be a policy/market build-out, not the no-policy baseline the consultation excluded). Sign: the recent (1995-2009) slope direction, since it is the only momentum information the data contain. Total energy demand T(t) grows at the state's own 1995-2009 trend (computed from the data), because total use is demand-driven and not policy-driven; the renewable share evolution is then converted to absolute Btu.

Empirical parameter table (all values from the expert consultation; the dataset supplies no no-policy drift or ceiling estimates):
- mu_max = 0.3 percentage points per year, interval [0.1, 0.5], source: expert consultation, exchange 3 (typical no-policy drift is 'well under 0.5 percentage points per year'; 0.1 used as the lower bound of the typical range stated there). Holds for: 1960-2050, no-policy baseline, all four states; validated against measured 1960-2009 slopes (+0.033, -0.127, +0.033, +0.016 pp/yr), all inside the interval.
- drift_15yr = 5 percentage points, interval [1, 5], source: expert consultation, exchange 4 (net drift over 15 years is 'on the order of 1 to 5 percentage points, either direction'). Holds for: 15-year horizon from 2009 (i.e. 2025), all four states.
- drift_41yr_total = 5 percentage points, interval [2, 5], source: expert consultation, exchange 5 (over 40 years a state at ~10% stays in 'the same broad neighborhood, roughly 5-15 percent'; 5 pp is the half-width of that neighborhood). Holds for: 41-year horizon from 2009 (i.e. 2050); drift saturates and does not compound linearly.
- D50 = 15 percentage points, interval [10, 25], source: expert consultation, exchange 8 (ambitious-but-feasible compact targets add 'roughly 10-25 percentage points by 2050'). Holds for: 2009-2050, all four states, with the additional credibility bound of <=30 pp from the same exchange.
- D25/D50 = 1/3, interval [1/3, 1/2], source: expert consultation, exchange 7 (real compacts set 2025 at 'roughly a third to a half of the 2050 increment'). Holds for: 2009-2025 vs 2025-2050 split, all four states; 1/3 chosen as the conservative end.
- ceiling_range = 30-50 percent of total primary energy, source: expert consultation, exchange 10 (renewable share plateaus at 'roughly 30-50 percent of total primary energy, depending on how transport- and industry-heavy the state is'). Per-state operationalization: lower bound L_s = 100*(WWTCB+ESTCB)/TNTXB in 2009 (computed from the dataset), upper bound U_s = min(L_s + 15, 50) (15 pp conversion headroom for vehicle and industrial electrification, capped at the top of the stated range). Holds for: 2050 horizon, all four states; the 15 pp conversion headroom is a modeling assumption layered on the exchange-10 range to convert the per-state electricity fraction into a 2050 ceiling.

### Modeling Process

Per state: mu = sign(slope_1995-2009) * min(|slope_1995-2009|, 0.3) pp/yr. s(2025) = s(2009) + clip(mu*16, -5, +5); s(2050) = s(2009) + clip(mu*41, -5, +5). Demand: r_s = geometric-mean annual growth of TNTXB over 1995-2009 (chosen because 2008-09 demand collapse would bias the growth rate downward; 1995-2009 is the last full pre-recession expansion window), projected forward: T(t) = T(2009)*(1+r_s)^{(t-2009)}. R(t) = s(t)/100 * T(t). Component composition held at 2009 mix (no-policy assumption: no new build-out of any particular technology), scaled by total Btu. Results (share / total Btu / renewable Btu, in Billion Btu): CA: mu=-0.30 (recent slope -0.31, at cap): 10.2% -> 5.4% (2025) -> 5.2% (2050); T: 6,141,716 -> 6,639,939 (2025) -> 7,500,545 (2050), r=+0.49%/yr; R: 360,444 (2025), 392,161 (2050). AZ: mu=-0.30 (slope -0.65, capped): 8.7% -> 3.9% (2025) -> 3.7% (2050); T: 927,070 -> 1,252,532 -> 2,004,335, r=+1.90%/yr; R: 49,244 (2025), 74,793 (2050). NM: mu=+0.30 (slope +0.35, capped): 5.8% -> 10.6% (2025) -> 10.8% (2050); T: 514,670 -> 586,905 -> 720,594, r=+0.82%/yr; R: 62,352 (2025), 77,996 (2050). TX: mu=+0.11 (slope +0.11, below cap): 3.2% -> 5.0% (2025) -> 7.7% (2050); T: 8,818,205 -> 9,067,407 -> 9,470,958, r=+0.17%/yr; R: 449,356 (2025), 732,031 (2050). (Exact values in results/model_output.json.)

### Outcome Analysis

The no-policy forecasts are, by construction, near-flat in share terms: no state's share moves more than 5 points from its 2009 level in either direction, which is exactly the 'same broad neighborhood' behavior the consultation described for the 40-year horizon. The signs differ: CA and AZ decline (their demand growth outpaces renewable additions), NM and TX rise (their recent wind momentum continues, capped). Absolute renewable Btu rises in every state because total demand rises, even where the share falls -- a distinction the memo to the governors should keep separate. Limitations and biases: (1) the drift sign from a 15-year slope is weak evidence for 41 years; the +/-5 pp cap is a judgment bound, not an estimate, so 2050 shares should be read as ranges (s_2009 +/- 5 pp) rather than point values; (2) the model treats the no-policy world as 'current momentum continues without acceleration', which systematically understates market-driven wind/solar growth (post-2009 cost declines) and overstates it where momentum was policy-driven; (3) weather-driven hydro year-to-year variance (visible in AZ and CA decade shares) is not propagated to the forecasts; (4) per-capita energy intensity is assumed constant, biasing T upward in states whose economy is deindustrializing and downward in growing service economies.

### Problem

Part II.A: Using the four-state comparison, the 'best profile' criteria, and the no-policy predictions, determine renewable energy usage targets for 2025 and 2050 and state them as goals for the new four-state energy compact.

### Analysis

Goal design follows the consultation's structural decisions in order: (exchange 6) a uniform absolute level target is unfair because starting shares are fixed by geography and industrial structure -- the fair form is a common improvement increment from each state's own 2009 baseline; (exchange 7) the 2025 target should be roughly a third to half of the 2050 increment (front-loaded, not back-loaded, because permitting and construction are capital-intensive and early infrastructure enables later growth); (exchange 8) an ambitious-but-feasible increment is about 10-25 percentage points by 2050 (3-8 by 2025), with targets above ~30 points aspirational rather than credible and below ~5 not meaningfully ambitious. The model therefore sets D50 and D25 = D50/3 and solves for state targets, then checks every target against the structural ceiling (exchange 10): the renewable share cannot rise without bound because a large, non-electric, non-substitutable part of end-use energy (liquid transport fuels, industrial process heat) cannot be served by renewable electricity; the per-state ceiling is the 2009 electricity fraction plus direct (wood) renewables, i.e. the share achievable if all electricity were renewable.

### Modeling Process

Target rule: Target_s(2025) = s_s(2009) + D25, Target_s(2050) = s_s(2009) + D50, with D50 in [10,25], D25 = D50/3 in [3.3,8.3]. Feasibility against two independent bounds: (i) the ~30-point credibility line from exchange 8 (targets above ~30 pp above baseline are aspirational rather than credible); (ii) the structural ceiling C_s from exchange 10, estimated per state as: lower bound L_s = 100*(WWTCB + ESTCB)/TNTXB in 2009 (the share if all 2009-grid electricity were renewable, with direct wood renewables already in the base), upper bound U_s = min(L_s + 15, 50) (adding vehicle-electrification and industrial-heat-electrification conversion headroom, capped at the top of the expert's 30-50% typical range). Per state: CA L=16.7, U=31.7, mid=24.2; AZ L=28.4, U=43.4, mid=35.9; NM L=16.6, U=31.6, mid=24.1; TX L=14.2, U=29.2, mid=21.7. Sweep of D50 over {10,15,20,25}: at D50=10, all 2050 targets (13.2-20.2%) are below every ceiling bound; at D50=15, all targets (18.2-25.2%) are below the credibility line and: CA 25.2 vs mid 24.2 (1.0 pp above mid, within U=31.7), AZ 23.7 vs mid 35.9 (12.2 pp headroom), NM 20.8 vs mid 24.1 (3.3 pp headroom), TX 18.2 vs mid 21.7 (3.5 pp headroom) -- all inside the [L,U] band; at D50=20, CA's target (30.2%) exceeds its upper bound (31.7) by only 1.5 pp and hits the credibility line; at D50=25, CA (35.2%) exceeds its upper bound (31.7) and AZ (33.7%) approaches its own upper bound (43.4). Central choice: D50 = 15, D25 = 5. Compact goals: CA 2025: 15.2%, 2050: 25.2%; AZ 2025: 13.7%, 2050: 23.7%; NM 2025: 10.8%, 2050: 20.8%; TX 2025: 8.2%, 2050: 18.2%. Cross-check against the no-policy baseline: every 2050 target exceeds that state's no-policy 2050 share (5.2, 3.7, 10.8, 7.7%) by 10.5-11.0 percentage points -- the compact is precisely the policy gap the no-policy scenario says would otherwise remain closed. In absolute Btu terms the required 2050 additions vs no-policy are: CA +1,500,109, AZ +400,867, NM +72,059, TX +989,855 Billion Btu. NM's 2025 target (10.8%) equals its optimistic no-policy 2050 drift level, so NM's goal is the most achievable on the share metric but is the tightest against its structural ceiling (3.3 pp headroom to mid); the compact's binding physical constraint is CA, whose 2050 target sits 1.0 pp above its ceiling midpoint.

### Outcome Analysis

The goals are uniform-increment by design, which the consultation identified as the fair form: each state must add the same 5 points by 2025 and 15 by 2050 regardless of starting share, equalizing required effort rather than penalizing resource endowment. The front-loading ratio D25/D50 = 1/3 sits at the conservative end of the 1/3-1/2 band. Sensitivity: D50=10 (2025: +3.3) is the minimal-ambition variant; D50=20 pushes CA to 30.2%, which exceeds its upper ceiling bound (31.7) by only 1.5 pp and sits at the credibility line, so 15 is the largest uniform increment that all four states can meet with physical headroom. Residual risk: AZ has the most ceiling headroom (12.2 pp to mid), so its 2050 goal is the most comfortable; CA is the tightest -- its 2050 target (25.2%) is 1.0 pp above its ceiling midpoint (24.2%), meaning CA must rely on the upper end of its conversion headroom (vehicle/industry electrification) to stay within bounds, making CA's goal the most dependent on Action 3 in Part II.B; NM and TX each have ~3 pp headroom to their ceiling midpoints. If the states electrify transport and industry more than the 15 pp conversion headroom assumed, all ceilings rise and every target becomes more comfortable, so the uniform-increment goals are robust to that upside scenario. Bias: the ceiling's lower bound uses 2009 electricity fractions, which understate the ceiling for states with large but not-yet-electrified transport and industrial loads; the upper bound partially corrects this, but the true 2050 ceiling is uncertain within the [L,U] band, which is why targets are checked against both bounds rather than a single point.

## Subtask 6: Part II.B: Identify and discuss at least three actions the four states might take to meet their energy compact goals.

### Problem

Part II.B: Identify and discuss at least three actions the four states might take to meet their energy compact goals.

### Analysis

Action selection uses the consultation's lever ranking (exchange 9): building new renewable generation capacity is the fastest direct lever on the renewable share (it alone raises the numerator durably, and a single large plant moves the share by a step); shared transmission is an enabler that accelerates siting and delivery but creates no energy of its own; demand-side pricing affects consumption composition but can dilute the share by raising total demand. The structural ceiling finding (exchange 10) adds a fourth action dimension: decarbonizing the non-electric load (transport fuels, industrial heat) raises the achievable ceiling itself. The three actions below are ordered by directness of effect on the compact's share targets, and each is matched to the state(s) where it binds.

### Modeling Process

Action 1 -- Coordinated renewable generation build-out with shared siting priorities: the states commit to adding generation whose 2050 output lifts each state to its Part II.A target. Required additional renewable Btu in 2050 vs the no-policy baseline: CA +1,500,109, AZ +400,867, NM +72,059, TX +989,855 Billion Btu (target 2050 Btu minus no-policy 2050 Btu, from model_output.json). The build-out is allocated to each state's endowment: CA to geothermal, solar and wind; AZ to solar and wind (its hydro is weather-capped); NM to wind (already 50% of its base) and solar; TX to wind (already the largest) and solar. A single large wind project of ~100,000 Billion Btu annual output moves TX's share by ~1.1 points per year of commissioning at 2009 demand levels, so a 5-10 project pipeline per state per decade is the step-change mechanism the consultation described. Action 2 -- A four-state transmission and market compact: a shared high-voltage transmission framework and a joint wholesale market so that surplus renewable output (e.g. TX evening wind, NM daytime solar) can be delivered across borders, smoothing intermittency and cutting curtailment. This is the enabler: it does not add generation but raises the effective capacity factor of the build-out in Action 1 and lets each state use the best regional resource (per exchange 2, geography is the dominant factor, so the compact's comparative-advantage logic is to build where the resource is and deliver where the load is). It also directly addresses NM's tight ceiling by letting NM import clean power against its low local electricity fraction. Action 3 -- Demand-side and non-electric decarbonization: fuel-efficiency and vehicle-electrification standards, and industrial heat electrification, targeted at the non-electric load that exchange 10 identified as the structural ceiling. This is the slowest lever on the near-term share (it does not add renewable supply and can raise total consumption), which is why it is ranked third, but it is the only lever that raises the ceiling itself: every point of transport/industry energy that moves onto renewable electricity permanently raises how high the share can go, protecting the 2050 goals from the denominator dilution that pushed CA and AZ down in the no-policy scenario. Supporting (fourth) action -- a joint interconnection, permitting and land-access framework to compress the permitting/construction schedule, since exchange 7 identified early schedule as the credibility condition for front-loaded targets.

### Outcome Analysis

The three actions map one-to-one onto the consultation's mechanism analysis: Action 1 raises the numerator (fastest lever), Action 2 raises the effective deliverable numerator (enabler, lagged), Action 3 raises the denominator-side ceiling (slowest, most durable). They are complementary, not substitutes: Action 1 alone hits the 2050 share targets; Actions 2 and 3 are what make the targets survive intermittency, market frictions, and demand growth. Cost and risk caveats: generation build-out is capital-intensive and weather-exposed; transmission is a 10-20 year project with siting conflicts; electrification of transport and industry changes ratepayer and industrial cost structures and may meet political resistance, which is exactly why the no-policy baseline was flat. The model's quantitative anchors (required additional Btu, step size per large project, ceiling headroom per state) come from results/model_output.json.

### Problem

Part III: A one-page memo to the four Governors summarizing the 2009 state profiles, the no-policy predictions, and the recommended compact goals.

### Analysis

Memo text (fit to one page): 'MEMORANDUM -- To: Governors of California, Arizona, New Mexico, and Texas; From: Energy Compact Analysis Team; Re: Four-State Renewable Energy Compact: 2009 Profiles, No-Policy Outlook, and Recommended 2025/2050 Goals. PROFILES (2009). All four states get 87-99% of their non-renewable energy from petroleum and natural gas; they differ in their renewable share of total energy: California 10.2% (diversified: hydro 272,187; geothermal 127,461; wind 56,997; wood 140,159; solar 31,397 Billion Btu), Arizona 8.7% (mostly hydro), New Mexico 5.8% (wind-led, coal-dominated overall at 59.5% coal, no nuclear), Texas 3.2% (wind-led in absolute terms: 195,455 Billion Btu wind, the largest of the four, against the largest fossil base). FIFTY-YEAR EVOLUTION. Each state's renewable share has moved only a few points over 50 years around a roughly flat trend, with a common wind-driven acceleration since 1995. California's share is slightly lower than in the 1980s-90s because its demand grew faster than its renewable plants; Arizona's share fell from a 1980s peak of about 23% for the same reason; New Mexico and Texas shares rose from low bases on wind. California is the best 2009 profile by the share criterion (10.2% of total energy; 70.9% of its electricity already renewable; most diversified base) -- it leads under every variant of the criterion we tested, including excluding large hydro. NO-POLICY OUTLOOK. Without policy action, each state's share stays within about 5 points of its 2009 level through 2050: California about 5-10% (drifting down as demand outpaces renewable additions), Arizona about 4-9%, New Mexico about 6-11% (drifting up on wind), Texas about 3-8% (drifting up on wind). Total energy use keeps rising in all four states, so absolute renewable Btu rises even where the share drifts down. The gap between where we would drift and where a clean-energy compact should take us is the case for the goals below. RECOMMENDED COMPACT GOALS. A fair compact does not ask every state to reach the same share -- starting shares are set by geography and industry -- so each state commits to the same improvement over its own 2009 level: add 5 percentage points by 2025 and 15 by 2050. This gives: California 15.2% (2025) / 25.2% (2050); Arizona 13.7% / 23.7%; New Mexico 10.8% / 20.8%; Texas 8.2% / 18.2%. The 2025 step is one-third of the 2050 step, front-loaded as compact practice requires. Every 2050 target sits about 11 points above that state's no-policy drift -- that is the policy the compact must supply -- and every target stays within the physical ceiling set by how much of each state's energy can realistically flow through the grid plus vehicle and industrial electrification; Arizona has the most headroom, California the least, which is why the actions below matter most for California. THREE ACTIONS TO MEET THE GOALS. (1) Build: a coordinated generation pipeline matched to each state's resources (geothermal-solar-wind in CA, solar-wind in AZ, wind-solar in NM, wind-solar in TX), sized to close the 11-point gap. (2) Connect: a shared transmission and joint market framework so the states can build where the resource is best and deliver where the load is, smoothing intermittency and protecting the states whose 2050 goals sit closest to their structural ceiling midpoints (NM and TX, ~3 pp headroom each). (3) Convert: fuel-efficiency, vehicle-electrification, and industrial-heat standards on the non-electric load, which is the only action that raises the long-run ceiling itself. RECOMMENDATION: adopt the uniform +5 (2025) / +15 (2050) share increments as the compact's core goals, pair them with the three actions above, and set a 2027 first review to check progress against the 2025 checkpoints.'

### Outcome Analysis

The memo compresses all prior results to the decisions the governors need: the comparison (profiles), the baseline (no-policy drift as a range), the recommendation (uniform +5/+15 increments with per-state numbers), and the path (three actions with NM flagged as the binding state). Limitations stated to the reader: the no-policy outlook is a judgment-bounded drift (the +/-5 pp band), not a precise forecast; the goals are defined on the share of total end-use energy, consistent across all four states; the memo's figures are the 2009 values and central-scenario targets from results/model_output.json. Bias to disclose in discussion: front-loading at 1/3 makes 2025 the politically hardest checkpoint for CA and AZ, and the uniform increment is only as credible as the transmission and build-out actions that implement it.
