# Interaction Evidence — MM-Bench 2020_D (Huskies passing network)

Data explored before consulting: 38 matches (13 W, 10 D, 15 L), 30 Huskies players
(10 F, 10 D, 9 M, 1 G), 10,435 Huskies passes / 23,429 total, 59,271 full events.
Record is heavily away-poor: 10 home wins vs 3 away wins; 58 goals conceded vs 44 scored.

## Exchange 1 — dominant passing mechanism (structural)
**Q:** What matters most in a good soccer team's passing: quick short links, or long balls forward?
**Reply (abridged):** Neither dominates; the mix matters and depends on game state and opponent. Successful teams are built on a high volume of short, quick, high-completion passes as the base (maintains possession, creates numerical superiority, progresses the ball reliably). Long balls forward are a lower-frequency, higher-variance tool — lower completion, cede possession when they fail — used selectively to bypass a high press, exploit space behind a slow defense, or relieve pressure. Short links are the default; long balls a situational complement.
**Became in model:** Sets the *shape* of the model. The passing network is weighted by pass length and completion proxy; the performance score rewards the high-volume short-pass foundation and treats long balls as a conditional complement. Concretely: (a) pass edges are classified short/mid/long by Euclidean field distance; (b) the "possession foundation" indicator uses short-pass volume and local clustering (triadic closure) as the primary signal, with long-ball share entering only as a state-dependent term, penalised when short-pass foundation is weak. Recorded interval: holds across normal competitive play, all game states.

## Exchange 2 — midfield hub role (structural, builds on E1)
**Q:** For a team built on short links, what role do midfield players really play in keeping play moving?
**Reply (abridged):** Midfielders are the connective hub: they receive from the back line and redistribute forward, carry the highest pass volume, and sit at the center of the passing network's betweenness. Threefold role: (1) link defense to attack — relay that turns a defender's outlet into an attacking entry; (2) create numerical superiority in the middle third by offering short, close options — what makes the short-link game work; (3) set tempo — recycling possession sideways/backward when no forward option is on, rather than forcing a low-percentage ball. In network terms: high degree and high betweenness, brokers between defensive and forward clusters; remove them and the short-link structure fragments.
**Became in model:** Defines the *structural-core* indicator. Compute degree centrality and betweenness on the Huskies passing network; the midfield hub metric is the mean betweenness of M-position players, and a "connectivity" indicator is the fraction of D→F attacking paths that pass through at least one M node. Teamwork score rewards high M-betweenness and high D–F-via-M path fraction. Constraint used: a healthy short-link network has its M nodes among the top-betweerness nodes; fragmentation (low D–F-via-M fraction) is a named failure mode. Interval: holds for the short-link foundation established in E1.

## Exchange 3 — local clustering + global spread (structural, builds on E1–E2)
**Q:** Do strong teams play in tight small groups of three to four players, or do they spread across the whole field?
**Reply (abridged):** Both, at different scales — not in conflict. Locally tight and globally spread: in the attacking phase they form small close clusters of three to four players (ball-carrier plus two/three short options) creating numerical superiority and quick combination play — these local triangles are the triadic configurations that make the short-link game work. Simultaneously the whole team occupies full width and depth, stretching the opponent's block to create the space those clusters exploit. Tight small groups are the mechanism; whole-field spread is the structure. Only-tight gets compressed/easy to defend; only-spread has no short options. Successful teams alternate — clustering near the ball, spreading away from it.
**Became in model:** Splits the structural indicators into a micro/macro pair. (1) *Triadic closure* — count closed triads (A–B, B–C, A–C all present) on the Huskies passing network and normalise by potential triads; this is the "local tightness" indicator, expected to be elevated for winning matches. (2) *Field spread* — mean and coefficient of variation of pass origins in x (width) and y (depth); a low-spread match is a named failure mode. The teamwork model treats triadic closure and spread as two additive structural terms that can offset only in the wrong direction (both high = healthy). Interval: holds in the attacking/possession phase per E1.

## Exchange 4 — tempo is dynamic and state-dependent (dynamic, builds on E1–E3)
**Q:** During a match, does a team's passing tempo usually stay steady, or does it change as the game goes on?
**Reply (abridged):** It changes; steady tempo across a match is not typical. Tempo varies with game state and fatigue: highest early in each half and after restarts; drops as players tire — pass counts and ball speed per minute fall in later stages, most visibly in the last 15-20 minutes. Shifts with scoreline: a chasing team speeds up and plays more directly; a team protecting a lead slows down, recycles possession, takes fewer risks. Opponent pressing raises tempo; a compact deep opponent can slow it. Tempo is a dynamic state-dependent quantity, tracked minute-by-minute, not averaged.
**Became in model:** Fixes the *temporal* design of the dynamical model. (1) Tempo indicator = passes per minute computed in 5-minute bins within each half (1H/2H from MatchPeriod, EventTime in seconds), giving a per-match tempo profile rather than a single average — per the explicit "minute-by-minute, not averaged" instruction. (2) Fatigue term: compare early-half (0-15 min) vs late-half (>75 min of period) tempo; a large drop is the fatigue signature. (3) State dependence: match Outcome/OwnScore-OpponentScore at each 15-min mark is used to condition whether the Huskies were chasing or protecting, so "directness" (share of long balls) is interpreted relative to scoreline, matching E1's "long balls are situational." Constraint: tempo is modelled as a function of (minute-in-half, game-state, opponent press), not a constant. Interval: holds for normal competitive halves of ~52-57 min.

## Exchange 5 — contribution is moderately concentrated with redundancy (structural, builds on E2)
**Q:** In a successful team, is passing effort spread evenly among players, or carried by a few key players?
**Reply (abridged):** Neither extreme; moderately concentrated. Pass distributions in good teams are skewed: a handful of players — central midfielders, a deep-lying playmaker, plus goalkeeper and center-backs as build-up outlets — account for a disproportionate share, while forwards/wide players touch the ball far less. But not extreme: a team funneling nearly everything through a single player is easy to shut down by marking him, and the structure collapses when he's unavailable. Successful teams have several high-volume hubs — load is concentrated but redundant, multiple players can play the connective role. Unevenly distributed, carried by a few hubs, but no single player indispensable.
**Became in model:** Defines the *contribution-distribution* and *redundancy* indicators. (1) Compute the outbound pass-degree share per player; measure concentration via the Gini coefficient of the out-degree vector and the top-3 players' share. Healthy range: skewed (Gini meaningfully above uniform) but top-3 share below a single-player dominance threshold. (2) *Hub redundancy*: count players whose betweenness exceeds a fraction (0.5×) of the max betweenness; a value of 1 (only the top hub) is the "single point of failure" failure mode named in the reply. (3) Positional prior: expect the high-volume hubs to be M / G / D, with F carrying a smaller share; a forward-dominated pass distribution is flagged as atypical. Interval: holds for the short-link foundation of E1 and the midfield hub role of E2.

## Exchange 6 — diagnosis gate: effort/execution before tactics (analytic, builds on E4)
**Q:** After a bad away result, what is the first thing a soccer coach usually looks for before changing tactics?
**Reply (abridged):** First looks at effort and execution, not tactics — whether the team lost its duels and second balls and made unforced errors. Tactics are worth changing only if the players carried out the existing plan. Reviews video for non-tactical causes first: intensity/work rate, individual mistakes/turnovers, set-piece and transition defending, whether beaten physically or mentally. A bad away result is often travel, hostile crowd, poor pitch, or a slow start rather than a flawed system. Only if the team executed the plan well and still lost does the coach blame tactics. Sequence: check effort, discipline, errors first; change tactics only when those are ruled out.
**Became in model:** Becomes the *diagnostic gate* in the advice/recommendation model (subproblem 3). Before attributing a loss to a structural/tactical flaw, the model first checks the execution proxies available in fullevents.csv: duel outcome and count (Duel events), unforced-error proxy (possession turnovers = pass to opponent or out), and first-15-min intensity (early-tempo from E4). A loss is classified as "execution" (keep tactics, fix intensity) vs "tactical" (change structure) depending on whether execution proxies are poor. Away matches are given a prior penalty for non-tactical causes (travel/crowd/slow start) — matching the strong home/away split found in the data (1.26 vs 0.63 pts/match). Interval: applies to post-match review logic, all matches.

## Exchange 7 — adapt within identity, not abandon it (dynamic, builds on E1, E2, E5)
**Q:** When an opponent changes their shape to stop a team's usual pattern, what does a strong team do differently?
**Reply (abridged):** Adapts rather than repeats the blocked pattern — but within its identity, not by abandoning it. When the usual option is taken (e.g., pressing the central midfield hub, packing the middle to kill short combos): (1) switches the point of attack — moves the ball laterally/backward to the free side, forcing the block to shift, overload on the opposite flank; (2) changes the entry route — if short central links are cut, goes longer or wider (over the press, into the channels, via full-backs), using the long ball as the situational complement it always was; (3) changes tempo and rhythm — slow to draw the opponent out, or speed up with quick one-touch to exploit before the block resets; (4) uses different players as the hub — if one connector is marked, another (center-back stepping up, wide player tucking in) takes over; this is the redundancy that makes the team hard to shut down. A strong team treats the opponent's adjustment as a cue to vary route, side, and tempo while keeping the same principles. A weak team keeps forcing the blocked pattern and stalls.
**Became in model:** Defines the *adaptability* indicator and the counter-strategy conditionality (subproblem 2). Adaptability = within-match variability in route/side/tempo while holding identity: (1) lateral re-orientation — count of direction switches in the passing sequence (pass to left then right, or backward then forward), normalised; (2) route diversity — Shannon entropy over pass-length buckets conditional on the ball being in the central third (variety of entry routes when short central links are cut); (3) tempo variability — std of the 5-min tempo bins (rhythm change), distinct from the mean tempo; (4) hub redundancy from E5 — number of near-max betweenness players, so "a different player takes over the hub" is measurable. The model treats these as the mechanism by which a team defeats an opponent's counter-strategy, so the effectiveness of a structural strategy is reported *conditional* on adaptability, answering "universally effective or dependent on counter-strategies." Interval: holds in possession when under organized press.

## Exchange 8 — edge case: final minutes of a close game (boundary, builds on E4)
**Q:** In the last minutes of a close, one-goal game, how does a team's play usually change from the rest of the match?
**Reply (abridged):** Changes in two opposite directions depending on which side of the scoreline the team is on, both departures from the normal pattern. Protecting the lead: slows down, more conservative — low-risk sideways/backward passes, plays into corners/away from own goal, fewer forward risks, defensive substitutions; the passing network contracts toward its own half and concentrates among defenders/midfielders. Chasing the goal: speeds up, more direct — higher tempo, more forward/longer passes, more crosses and set-piece deliveries, more players committed forward, less patience; the network stretches toward the opponent's goal, shape more open, raising the risk of conceding on the counter. In the final minutes, tempo, directness, and territorial spread diverge sharply between the two teams — the game becomes less symmetric than at any other point.
**Became in model:** Defines the *domain of validity / edge case* of the dynamical model. The model's state-dependence (E4) is extended to a terminal-game-state regime: for matches that enter the final 10 minutes (both halves, last ~600s of 2H) with a 1-goal margin, the tempo/directness/spread indicators are interpreted as divergent, not as a drift of the base mechanism. The model therefore (1) splits each match's time series into "normal" vs "endgame" windows; (2) in the endgame window, conditions all indicators on the team's sign of (OwnScore - OpponentScore) at that point — a +1 team's contracted network (low forward share, low x-centroid, low tempo) is the *expected healthy* response, not a failure; a -1 team's stretched network (high long-ball/cross share, high x-centroid) is expected but flags counter-attack risk. This is the boundary where the "steady short-link foundation" (E1) assumption is known to break, so the teamwork score excludes endgame windows from the structural terms and treats them separately. Interval: final ~10 minutes with a 1-goal margin.

## Exchange 9 — generalization: structural ideas carry over, sport-specific do not (subproblem 4)
**Q:** Of what you learned about how soccer teams coordinate, which ideas would carry over to a business or research team?
**Reply (abridged):** The structural ideas carry over, not the sport-specific ones. (1) Short, frequent, low-risk exchanges as the base — a team that communicates often and cheaply (quick check-ins, small handoffs) maintains shared context; long high-stakes one-shot communications are the situational complement. (2) Connective brokers matter — the midfield-hub maps to the coordinator/integrator who links specialized subgroups; remove them and the team fragments. (3) Local tightness plus global spread — small close-knit subgroups (3-4) doing the actual work, within a team spanning the full problem space. (4) Uneven but redundant contribution — a few hubs carry disproportionate load but successful teams have several, so no single person is indispensable. (5) Adaptation within identity — switch route/tempo/hub when the environment blocks the usual pattern, without abandoning principles. (6) Dynamic state-dependent tempo — coordination intensity shifts with pressure, deadlines, stakes; track over time, not averaged. What does NOT carry over cleanly: the fixed rules, roles, and shared physical space that make soccer coordination measurable.
**Became in model:** Directly structures the subproblem-4 generalization. The teamwork model is decomposed into a *transferable structural core* (six items above, each already an indicator in the model: short-pass foundation E1, betweenness hubs E2, triadic closure + field spread E3, degree-Gini/hub-redundancy E5, adaptability E7, tempo-profile E4) and a *non-transferable sport-specific shell* (fixed 11-player roles, on/off-ball positions, the bounded 100x100 field, discrete scoring). The generalized model of team performance keeps the structural core as the invariant and lists what must be added for other domains to make the shell measurable: an explicit interaction graph with typed edges (communication/handoff events with timestamps and content), a definition of "shared context" and a completion/receipt signal (the analogue of pass completion, absent in most organizational data), and a bounded task-space so "spread" is defined. This is the answer to "what other aspects of teamwork would need to be captured." Interval: applies to any team with observable pairwise interaction events.

## Exchange 10 — boundary: what pass/event data cannot capture (limitations)
**Q:** When a coach studies a team using only pass and event data, what important thing about the team is missing?
**Reply (abridged):** Everything that happens without the ball — and the causal context that gives the passes meaning. Specifically absent from pass/event data: (1) off-ball movement and positioning — runs, decoy runs, dragging defenders, creating space, defensive shape; the data records who passed to whom, not why the option existed. (2) Defensive and pressing behavior — interceptions, tackles, blocks, pressing intensity appear only indirectly (as failed passes by the opponent), so defensive coordination is largely unmeasured. (3) Physical and mental state — fatigue, injuries, motivation, confidence not captured. (4) Intent and decision quality — a completed pass and a missed opportunity look different in outcome but the data cannot tell whether the right option was chosen. (5) Context — game state, weather, pitch, crowd, referee decisions absent or only partially inferable. So pass/event data describes the on-ball, successful part of coordination — the visible skeleton — while the off-ball structure that makes it possible, and the reasons behind each choice, remain hidden.
**Became in model:** Fixes the *domain of validity / limitations* stated in subproblem 4 and in the outcome analysis. The model's indicators are explicitly restricted to the on-ball, successful subset of coordination: (a) the passing network measures only completed pass links, so "why the option existed" (off-ball runs, decoys, space creation) is unmodeled — the triadic-closure and spread indicators describe the skeleton, not the generative mechanism. (b) Defensive/pressing coordination is proxied only indirectly via opponent event share and duel counts, not directly measured. (c) Fatigue is proxied by the late-half tempo drop, not by a physiological signal. (d) Intent/decision quality is unmeasured — a pass that completes is not scored higher for being the right choice. (e) Context (weather, pitch, crowd, referee) is absent except for home/away, which is the only context variable retained. These are recorded as the model's systematic blind spots and biases: the teamwork score is an on-ball, possession-side measure and will understate teams whose advantage is defensive or off-ball. Interval: applies to all indicators in the model.
