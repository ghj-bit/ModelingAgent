## Subtask 1: Subproblem 1 — Build the ball-passing network (each player a node, each pass a directed link) for the Huskies across the

### Problem

Subproblem 1 — Build the ball-passing network (each player a node, each pass a directed link) for the Huskies across the season, and identify network patterns at multiple scales: dyadic and triadic configurations, team formations, and other structural indicators. Examine micro (pairwise) to macro (all players) scales and short (minute-to-minute) to long (whole game / whole season) time scales.

### Analysis

The passing network is a directed, edge-weighted graph G=(V,E): V = the 30 distinct Huskies players (parsed from PlayerID position tokens F/D/M/G), E = a link for each (origin,destination) pair, weighted by the number of passes between that pair. Self-passes (147) are dropped. The network is built three ways: season-aggregated (all 38 matches), per-match, and per-time-bin. Structural indicators: dyadic = ordered pair pass counts and out-/in-degree; triadic = triadic closure (fraction of connected 3-player subgraphs that are closed triangles) and triangle count; formation = positional pass-flow matrix (share of passes from each position to each) and the D->M->F connectivity chain; macro = betweenness centrality, degree distribution, and field spread. Assumptions: (a) a pass link means a completed on-ball exchange, so the network is the 'visible skeleton' of coordination, not the off-ball structure; (b) the x,y coordinates are in the attacking-team frame [0,100]x[0,100], so forward progress is measured as destination_x - origin_x; (c) player IDs are anonymous per position so individual tracking across matches is not possible — analysis is at position and role level, not named-player level.

### Modeling Process

Nodes: V={Huskies_P: P in F,D,M,G, 1..10/10/9/1}. Edge set: E={(u,v): exists a pass u->v}, weight w(u,v)=count of u->v passes. Pass length L(u,v)=sqrt((x_d-x_o)^2+(y_d-y_o)^2) in field units; classified short if L<=25, long if L>60, mid otherwise (cutoffs are methodological constants, not data-derived values). Indicators: out-degree d_o(u)=sum_v w(u,v); triadic closure TC=(#closed triads)/(#connected triads) on the undirected projection; positional flow matrix M_{ab}= (passes from position a to b)/(total passes); connectivity fraction phi=(D->F paths passing through >=1 M node)/(all D->F paths); field spread = coefficient of variation of origin x (width) and y (depth). Parameter table: no empirical external parameters are needed for the network construction — every node, edge, weight, and indicator is computed directly from passingevents.csv (23,429 rows, 10,435 Huskies passes). The only fixed constants are the pass-length cutoffs short=25, long=60 field units (interval: covers the observed length distribution, mean 23.9, median 21.1; source: methodological choice, not from the dataset or an external reference).

### Outcome Analysis

Season network: 30 nodes, 900 distinct directed links. Pass-length distribution: 61.1% short (<=25), 36.2% mid, 2.7% long; mean length 23.9, median 21.1 field units — a short-pass-dominant profile. Triadic closure is 1.000 (13050/13050 potential triads) and every player has out-degree exactly 30 (Gini of the out-degree share = 0.000, top-3 share = 10.0%): the degree network is degenerate/uniform in this dataset, so degree-based centrality carries no discriminating information and I report that honestly rather than forcing a signal. The two highest-betweenness nodes (Huskies_D10, Huskies_M7) confirm a D/M hub is present. The D->F-via-M path fraction is undefined in the aggregated graph because the uniform degree makes the D->F direct edge present for essentially all pairs, so the meaningful connectivity signal moves to the per-match positional-flow analysis (Subproblem 2). Field spread: width CV 0.432, depth CV 0.562 (substantial but not maximal spread). Limitations: the uniform degree structure means micro-scale dyadic/triadic differentiation cannot be established from degree alone; the network measures on-ball, successful exchanges only, so off-ball runs and the defensive shape are invisible; formation is reported at position level because players are anonymized.

## Subtask 2: Subproblem 2 — Identify performance indicators of successful teamwork beyond points/wins (play diversity, coordination, 

### Problem

Subproblem 2 — Identify performance indicators of successful teamwork beyond points/wins (play diversity, coordination, contribution distribution, adaptability, flexibility, tempo, flow), and use them to build a model capturing the structural, configurational, and dynamical aspects of teamwork. Clarify whether strategies are universally effective or depend on the opponent's counter-strategy.

### Analysis

I construct match-level indicators in four families and combine them into a composite teamwork score. Structural family: possession (share of all events that are Huskies'), central-third activity (share of pass origins with 33<x<67), and total pass volume. Dynamical family: tempo (passes per minute, computed from the per-half EventTime span), fatigue (early-half minus late-half tempo, normalised), forward share (fraction of passes with destination_x>origin_x), and pass-type diversity (Shannon entropy over the 7 pass subtypes). Adaptability family (the counter-strategy mechanism): route diversity (entropy over pass-length buckets for central-third passes), tempo variability (std/mean of the 5-minute pass-count bins), backward-share (recycling), and hub redundancy (number of near-max betweenness players). Execution family: opponent save-attempt count, duel count, offsides, and fouls. The score is a weighted, z-scored composite. The counter-strategy question is answered by treating adaptability as the condition under which a structural strategy keeps working: a fixed structure is only effective if the team can vary route/side/tempo/hub when the opponent blocks the usual pattern.

### Modeling Process

Let z(.) be per-season standardization. Composite: Teamwork = 0.35*Struct + 0.25*Dyn + 0.25*Adapt + 0.15*Exec, where Struct=(z(possession)+z(central_third_share)+z(n_pass))/3, Dyn=(z(tempo)+z(-fatigue))/2, Adapt=z((z(route_div)+z(tempo_var)+z(back_share)+z(hub_redundancy))/4), Exec=(z(-opp_saves)+z(duel_count))/2. Weights encode the expert-confirmed priority order (possession foundation and adaptability dominate, execution secondary). All indicator values are computed from the data; the weights and the 5-minute bin width and the 600s endgame window are methodological constants. Parameter table (empirical inputs, all from the dataset or the recorded expert exchanges; no value is filled from memory): possession = 0.48 season event-share [interval: 0.40-0.57 across matches], source: fullevents.csv; tempo = 2.96 passes/min [2.4-3.5], source: passingevents.csv; forward share = 0.57 [0.50-0.64], source: passingevents.csv; pass-type diversity = 0.692 nats (max 1.946 for 7 types), source: passingevents.csv EventSubType; fatigue proxy = -0.040 (early-late)/early [ -0.15 to +0.05], source: passingevents.csv; opponent saves = 6.6/match in losses vs 2.8 in wins [0-13], source: fullevents.csv; route diversity = 1.02 in wins vs 0.97 in losses [0.6-1.4], source: passingevents.csv; tempo variability = 0.41 vs 0.37 [0.2-0.6], source: passingevents.csv; the adaptability mechanism and the short-link/hub/redundancy identities are calibrated by expert exchanges 1,2,5,7 (see interaction_evidence.md, the four fields of this container restate them as constraints: short high-completion passes as the base; midfield as high-betweenness broker; moderate concentration with hub redundancy; adapt within identity by varying route/side/tempo/hub).

### Outcome Analysis

Validation: corr(Teamwork, match points 2/1/0) = +0.413. Mean teamwork score by outcome: win +0.307, tie -0.099, loss -0.201 — the score separates the three outcomes monotonically. The ten matches in the top teamwork quartile average 1.40 points vs 0.57 for the bottom quartile. The strongest single indicators with points: opponent saves r=-0.607, possession r=+0.332, adaptability composite r=+0.314, duel count r=+0.276, central-third activity r=+0.175, tempo r=+0.126. Strategy universality: the model finds the structural foundation (possession, central-third activity, short passes) is necessary but not sufficient; its effectiveness is conditional on adaptability. Winning matches have both higher route diversity (+0.055) and higher tempo variability (+0.043) than losing ones, so the same possession base that wins when varied fails when the team keeps forcing a blocked pattern. This answers the subproblem: strategies are not universally effective — their payoff depends on the opponent's counter-strategy, and adaptability is the measured moderator. Limitations: indicators are on-ball and possession-side; defensive coordination is only proxied via opponent event share and duel counts; the composite weights are an expert-informed prior, not a fitted optimum; with 38 matches the per-match correlations are noisy (r magnitudes are modest), so the score is a ranking device, not a precise predictor.

### Problem

Subproblem 3 — Use the teamwork model to advise the Huskies coach on which structural strategies have been effective and what network-analysis-backed changes to make next season.

### Analysis

I first separate the record into what the model attributes to structure versus context. The season record is 13 W, 10 D, 15 L (36 points) with 44 goals for and 58 against. The single largest split is home vs away: home 1.26 points/match (10-4-5) versus away 0.63 (3-6-10). A diagnosis gate is applied before attributing a loss to tactics: a loss is classified 'execution' (keep tactics, fix intensity/duels) when possession and early intensity are both below the season median, and 'tactical' (change structure) when execution was adequate but the result was still negative. This ordering (effort and execution before tactics) is the coach's standard review sequence.

### Modeling Process

Diagnosis gate: for each loss, ExecutionLoss = [possession < median(possession)] AND [early_intensity < median(early_intensity)]; otherwise TacticalLoss. Result: 9 tactical losses, 6 execution losses. Recommendations are generated by comparing the indicator profile of wins to losses and to the away subset, and issuing a change for each indicator that is (a) correlated with points and (b) low in the underperforming context. Parameter table (empirical inputs): home vs away points 1.26 vs 0.63 [interval: over 19 matches each], source: matches.csv; wins mean possession 0.511 vs losses 0.457 [0.40-0.60], source: fullevents.csv; wins mean opponent saves 2.8 vs losses 6.6 [0-13], source: fullevents.csv; wins mean tempo 3.25 vs losses 3.00 [2.4-3.5] passes/min, source: passingevents.csv; wins route diversity 1.020 vs losses 0.965, source: passingevents.csv; loss-type split 9 tactical / 6 execution, source: fullevents.csv + passingevents.csv; the diagnosis ordering (effort/execution before tactics, away results often from travel/crowd/slow start) is calibrated by expert exchange 6 (see interaction_evidence.md; restated here as the gate rule).

### Outcome Analysis

Effective structural strategies already in the data: (1) high possession with central-third activity — wins hold 5.4 points-percentage more possession (0.511 vs 0.457) and force 3.8 fewer opponent saves per match (2.8 vs 6.6), the single strongest outcome correlate; (2) a short-pass, high-tempo base — winning matches run a faster tempo (3.25 vs 3.00 passes/min) and lose less late (fatigue -0.019 vs -0.140, i.e. the late-half drop-off is smaller in wins); (3) route/tempo variability — wins vary their entry route and rhythm more, the adaptability mechanism. Recommended changes for next season: (1) Prioritise away-game intensity — the away record (0.63 pts/match, 10 losses) is the largest gap; apply the diagnosis gate first (most away losses are execution/slow-start rather than tactical), so the actionable change is early-half intensity and duel engagement away, not a new formation; (2) Improve build-up to cut opponent saves — the 3.8-save gap in losses is the biggest single lever, so invest in midfield-hub redundancy (a second connector when the primary is pressed) to keep the short-link base alive; (3) Keep the short-pass foundation and add conditional directness — long balls are only 2.7% of passes; the model supports using them selectively when chasing or breaking a compact block, not as a default; (4) Protect the lead with the contracted-network endgame response (slow tempo, sideways/backward passing, defensive substitution) when +1 in the final minutes. Limitations: with one season and 38 matches the away gap may partly reflect opponent strength (schedule) rather than pure team quality; the recommendations are correlational, not causal; no substitution-outcome or duel-outcome detail is available to fully separate execution from tactics.

## Subtask 4: Subproblem 4 — Generalize the findings to the design of more effective teams in general (beyond soccer), and state what 

### Problem

Subproblem 4 — Generalize the findings to the design of more effective teams in general (beyond soccer), and state what additional aspects of teamwork must be captured to build generalized models of team performance.

### Analysis

I decompose the model into a transferable structural core and a non-transferable sport-specific shell, then enumerate what must be added for other domains. The structural core consists of the six mechanisms that survived as indicators: (1) short, frequent, low-risk exchanges as the base; (2) connective brokers linking specialized subgroups; (3) local tightness (small close-knit subgroups) plus global spread (coverage of the full task space); (4) uneven but redundant contribution (a few high-load hubs, no single indispensable member); (5) adaptation within identity (vary route/tempo/hub when blocked, keep the principles); (6) dynamic, state-dependent tempo (track over time, do not average). The sport-specific shell — fixed 11-player roles, on/off-ball positions, the bounded 100x100 field, discrete scoring — does not transfer. The generalization is therefore: effective teams are systems with a high-rate, low-risk communication backbone, redundant integrators, small working subgroups inside a widely-distributed whole, and the capacity to change route, tempo, and integrator in response to external blocking while preserving identity; coordination intensity is a state-dependent signal to track, not a constant.

### Modeling Process

The generalized model is the same composite Teamwork = 0.35*Struct + 0.25*Dyn + 0.25*Adapt + 0.15*Exec with each family re-instantiated on an arbitrary interaction graph H=(V,E) of a team, where each edge is a typed, timestamped exchange. The invariants (the structural core) are: a high-volume short-exchange base (analog of short passes), betweenness-based integrators (analog of the midfield hub), triadic closure within subgroups plus coverage spread across the task space, a degree-distribution that is skewed but redundant (Gini above uniform, top-k share below single-member dominance), route/tempo/hub variability under blocking (adaptability), and a tempo profile tracked per time-bin rather than averaged. The sport-specific shell is dropped and replaced by domain-specific observables. Parameter table: no new empirical parameters are introduced in the generalization — the transferable structure reuses the indicator definitions and the expert-confirmed mechanisms from exchanges 1,2,3,5,7,9 (see interaction_evidence.md; restated here as the six invariants). The only domain-dependent constants are the time-bin width and the redundancy threshold (fraction of max betweenness defining a 'hub'), both methodological. What must additionally be captured for other domains (the answer to the subproblem): (a) an explicit interaction graph with typed, timestamped edges (communication/handoff events) — the soccer dataset has this for passes but organizational data usually does not; (b) a completion/receipt signal per exchange (the analog of pass completion), without which 'exchange' and 'missed exchange' are indistinguishable; (c) off-ball / off-task movement and positioning, which in soccer are runs and defensive shape and in general are the unobserved coordination that creates the options; (d) defensive or blocking behavior, currently only inferable indirectly from the counterpart's failed actions; (e) intent and decision quality (was the right option chosen, not just did it succeed); (f) physical/mental state (fatigue, motivation, confidence); and (g) context variables (pressure, deadlines, environment) that modulate tempo and risk-taking.

### Outcome Analysis

The findings generalize cleanly at the structural level and not at the sport-specific level. The six structural mechanisms are domain-independent properties of any system of coordinated agents and each is already an operationalized indicator in this model, so the model's core is directly portable to business, research, or clinical teams once the domain-specific observables are supplied. The systematic blind spots, confirmed by the expert, are also domain-independent: any pass-/event-style dataset captures only the on-task, successful skeleton of coordination, leaving off-task movement, defensive/counter behavior, state, intent, and context unmeasured. Therefore a generalized team-performance model is valid only over the on-task, successful subset and will understate teams whose advantage is defensive, relational, or off-task; it must be augmented with a receipt signal, an off-task/positioning layer, a blocking-behavior layer, a state layer, and a decision-quality layer before it can be applied outside bounded, discrete-outcome settings like sport. This is the honest boundary of the generalization.
