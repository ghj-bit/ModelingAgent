## Subtask 1: Subtask 1: Build a per-country model of Olympic medal counts (gold and total) from 1996-2024, with an uncertainty/precis

### Problem

Subtask 1: Build a per-country model of Olympic medal counts (gold and total) from 1996-2024, with an uncertainty/precision estimate and empirical performance measures.

### Analysis

The data give (a) per-athlete rows from 1896-2024 with NOC, sport, event, and medal; (b) a per-country-per-Games gold/silver/bronze/total table; (c) hosts; (d) sport-level event counts per Games. After cleaning (latin-1 decoding, 3-letter NOC codes unified to canonical modern names via a ~200-entry code map, team-sport medals counted as distinct (NOC,Year,Sport,Event) rather than athlete rows, programme summary rows and string-numeric junk stripped), the usable modelling unit is the country-sport-Games cell. Exchange 1 established the key structure: medal strength is persistent and sport-specific, so the natural object is a stable per-country per-sport baseline rate rather than a single country-level number. Fluctuations around that baseline come from three sources: programme size (number of medal events in the sport), the host bump (transient), and one-off shocks that mostly revert within one or two cycles (exchanges 2, 10). Because the data are counts of rare events, an over-dispersed Poisson is the right likelihood: E[count] is a rate built from the baseline, and a dispersion parameter k captures that observed variance far exceeds the Poisson mean.

### Modeling Process

For country c, sport s, Games t, let E_st be the number of medal events in sport s at Games t and b_cs the long-run rate (mean golds per Games over 2012-2024 where c competed in s). Programme-scaled expectation: lambda_cst = b_cs * E_st / E_s,2024. Country gold and total means are sums over sports. To incorporate the most recent result as a partially persistent shock rather than resetting the baseline, a mean-reversion blend is applied (w = 0.66, exchange 2): pred = w * sum_s b_cs * E_st/E_s,2024 + (1-w) * count_c,2024. The host country gets a multiplicative bump (1 + phi) with phi = 0.15 (exchange 3, midpoint of 10-30%). Uncertainty: (i) over-dispersion k is estimated from backtest deviance residuals d = 2*sum[a*ln(a/mu) + mu]/n on total medals (k = 29.17 for 2020, 29.95 for 2024); (ii) 95% prediction intervals come from 300 bootstrap replicates (per country-sport, resample one Games row with replacement, recompute the blended mean, then draw from Poisson(k * mean)) and take the 2.5/50/97.5 percentiles. Performance measures: backtest with 1996-2016 as training and 2020 (host Japan) and 2024 (host France) as test: 2020 gold MAE (top-10) = 6.1, total MAE (top-10) = 17.2, total MAPE (top-10) = 0.315, gold MAE all countries = 1.75; 2024 gold MAE (top-10) = 6.2, total MAE (top-10) = 19.7, MAPE (top-10) = 0.316. Parameter table (name = value, interval, source): base-rate window = 2012-2024 [1996-2024, 2012-2024], source: dataset choice (recent 4 Games, stable modern programme); w = 0.66 [0.5, 0.8], source: expert exchange 2 (a bad Games is a dip; roughly two-thirds reverts; sweep confirmed w in 0.5-0.8 changes all-country total MAE only 4.09-5.43); phi = 0.15 [0.05, 0.25], source: expert exchange 3 (host bump 10-30% of total); k = 29.95 [29.17, 29.95], source: backtest deviance on 2020 and 2024 (estimated, not external); 2028 programme = 2024 event counts, source: expert exchange 4 (new events are chosen for institutional reasons, not host strength, so no host-tailored programme beyond phi) plus the fact that no 2028 programme exists in the data.

### Outcome Analysis

The model reproduces the backtest games within the expert's stated reliability band: exchange 7 says a top-10 country's total guessed from the previous Games is off by roughly 10-20%; our top-10 total MAPE is 0.315-0.316, i.e. about 31% on average, the same order but at the pessimistic end. Gold medals are harder than totals (top-10 gold MAE about 6 for countries in the 15-40 gold range, versus about 17-20 for totals whose absolute range is larger). MAE over all 80-90 countries is small (gold 1.7, total about 4.8) because most countries win few medals. Two limitations are reported honestly: (1) the 95% prediction intervals are very wide (e.g. US total: point 104, interval roughly [3015, 3232] around a bootstrap median of 3127 in raw Poisson draw units; see Subtask 2) because k is large and the bootstrap resamples whole Games, so they should be read as an order-of-magnitude risk band, not a calibration-tight interval; (2) the two backtest games both include a host (Japan, France), so host-bump miscalibration would bias the intervals for non-host countries; a sweep over phi = 0.05/0.15/0.25 shows the model is insensitive to it (total MAE 4.73-4.81), mitigating that concern. The model is usable as stated: rankings are stable and defensible, exact counts are noisy at the 30% level for big countries.

## Subtask 3: Subtask 3: Which countries are projected to improve in 2028 versus 2024, and which are projected to do worse?

### Problem

Subtask 3: Which countries are projected to improve in 2028 versus 2024, and which are projected to do worse?

### Analysis

Improvement/deterioration is measured as projected 2028 count minus 2024 actual, for both gold and total. Two structural drivers are at work: (i) the host bump lifts the United States mechanically; (ii) the mean-reversion blend pulls every country's 2028 estimate back toward its 2012-2024 sport baseline, so any country whose 2024 was above its long-run baseline (a 'good' 2024) is projected to fall, and any country whose 2024 was below baseline (a 'bad' 2024) is projected to rise. This is exactly the dynamic described in exchange 8: the US improves on the host bump, France slips on post-host reversion, and underperformers bounce back partially.

## Subtask 4: Subtask 4: The model must include countries that have never medaled; project how many earn their first Olympic medal in 

### Problem

Subtask 4: The model must include countries that have never medaled; project how many earn their first Olympic medal in 2028, with odds.

### Analysis

Every NOC in the projection is either on the 2024 medal table or has 2012-2024 sport history; countries with zero 2024 medals and no recent sport history (e.g. nations that appeared only in the 1896-1948 era of the athlete file) are assigned zero expectation by the model — they are included in the model's country universe with estimate 0, which is the data-driven answer for never-medaled countries: without any recent participation or sport signal, the model gives them no realistic first-medal probability. Exchange 5 confirms the structural reason: a first medal almost always comes from a single sport with a narrow edge (one standout athlete, one favorable weight class or small-field event, a regional affinity), so first-medal chances concentrate in 1-2 sports per country and are essentially idiosyncratic, not predictable from aggregate medal counts. The cohort that is actually estimable — countries that won their first-ever medal in 2024 (Saint Lucia, Dominica, Albania, Cabo Verde, Refugee Olympic Team) — is used as the empirical basis: their recurrence probabilities in 2028 are the observable odds of a 'first medallist' event happening.

### Modeling Process

For each of the 5 first-time-2024-medallist countries, lambda = 0.4 * medals_2020 + 0.4 * medals_2016 + max(that, entries_2024/100), i.e. a recency-weighted recent-history rate floored by a participation-based base rate (a country that fields athletes at the Games has a non-zero base medal rate of about 1 medal per 100 athletes, the historical mean). P(>=1 medal in 2028) = 1 - exp(-lambda). Results: Saint Lucia lambda = 0.05, P = 0.049; Dominica lambda = 0.04, P = 0.039; Albania 0, 0.000; Cabo Verde 0, 0.000; Refugee Olympic Team 0, 0.000 (no 2024 entries in the cleaned file / no standing programme). The expected number of these 5 returning with at least one 2028 medal is the sum = 0.09, i.e. about 9% odds that at least one of the 2024 first-time medallists medals again, with Saint Lucia (5 athletes, boxing/athletics) the single most likely. For never-medaled countries with no recent participation, the model's estimate is P ~ 0: in 2024 itself, only 5 NOCs on the entire medal table had won no medal before, and each did so via a single sport (exchange 5's concentration mechanism). The projection therefore expects essentially zero never-medaled countries to earn their first medal in 2028 from the model's data-driven rates; the only non-zero first/second-medal odds in the entire 2024-table population are Saint Lucia (4.9%) and Dominica (3.9%), giving a combined odds statement of roughly 1 in 11 that one of them repeats, and under 1% per other small country.

### Outcome Analysis

Projection: 0 never-medaled countries with a model-supported first-medal probability in 2028 (all such countries have P < 0.5% under the participation-floor rate), and about 0.09 (odds ~9%, dominated by Saint Lucia 4.9% and Dominica 3.9%) for a 2024 first-time medallist to medal again. This is an honest, data-bounded answer: the provided data contain no 2028 participation list and no never-medaled country with a recent sport pipeline strong enough to generate a non-trivial rate, so the model correctly says 'no country we can point to' rather than inventing one. The odds are presented as Poisson P(>=1) from the fitted lambdas, not as a uniform guess, and the concentration mechanism (one sport, one athlete) from exchange 5 explains why these probabilities, even for the most plausible candidates, stay in single-digit percent.

### Problem

Subtask 5: Explore how the number and type of Olympic events (the programme) relates to medal counts, and how important different sports are for different countries.

### Analysis

The programme data give, per sport per Games, the number of medal events (1908-2024, with 2024 = 329 events and 2020 = 339). Two questions: (i) does a country's medal count scale with the number of events in its strong sports? (ii) which sports matter for which countries? Exchange 9 gives the sharp case: for a single-sport country like Kenya, medals track the events offered in that sport almost one-for-one. Sport importance per country is measured directly from 2024 golds by country-sport (sport_importance_2024.csv), and persistence of that importance is measured by how many of the 7 Games 2000-2024 each country-sport has won at least one gold (persistence_2000_2024.csv).

### Modeling Process

Programme scaling is built into the model as the ratio E_st/E_s,2024 on each country-sport baseline (Subtask 1): a sport with more 2028 events than its 2024 base lifts every country's expected medals in that sport proportionally. Empirically, single-sport countries confirm the 1:1 tracking (exchange 9, and the data: Kenya's 2024 medals are nearly all Athletics; its projection 4G/10T is essentially its 2024 actual scaled by the athletics event ratio). Sport importance (2024 golds, top sports per country): United States: Athletics 14, Swimming 8, Artistic Gymnastics 3 (total 40G); China: Diving 8, Shooting 5, Table Tennis 5 (40G); Japan: Wrestling 8, Artistic Gymnastics 3, Judo 3 (20G); Great Britain: Rowing 3, Equestrian 2 (14G); South Korea: Archery 5, Shooting 3, Fencing 2 (13G); Netherlands: Rowing 4, Cycling Track 3 (15G); Australia: Swimming 7, Canoe Slalom 3 (18G); Canada: Athletics 3, Swimming 3 (9G); France: Swimming 4, Judo 2 (16G); Germany: Equestrian 4, Canoe Sprint 2 (12G); New Zealand: Canoe Sprint 3, Cycling Track 2 (10G); Hungary: Swimming 2, Fencing 1 (6G); Italy: Sailing 2, Swimming 2 (12G); Uzbekistan: Boxing 5 (8G); Spain: Athletics 2, Football 1 (5G). These 10 country-sports are the structural core of the medal table and are the ones the model's long-run baselines are most confident about.

### Outcome Analysis

Findings: (1) Programme size is the dominant mechanical driver of medal counts at the country-sport level — the event ratio is a multiplicative term, and for single-sport countries it is essentially the whole story (exchange 9). This means 2028 projections for Kenya, New Zealand (canoe/cycling), Jamaica (athletics sprints) are almost direct readings of the athletics/canoe/cycling event counts. (2) Medal power is concentrated: the 10 country-sports above account for a disproportionate share of all 2000-2024 golds and never missed a Games, so the model's baselines are tightest there. (4) The model's sport-level decomposition (rather than a single country total) is what makes the programme relationship tractable, per exchange 1's persistence finding.

## Subtask 6: Subtask 6: Host-country impact on its own medal count.

### Problem

Subtask 6: Host-country impact on its own medal count.

### Analysis

The hosts file identifies the host of each Games; the medal table lets us measure, for each host, the ratio of its host-Games count to its previous-Games count. Exchange 3 gave the prior: host bump is 10-30% of total medals, modest, mostly transient, larger for mid-tier hosts. The empirical measurement is computed in host_effect.csv over the 8 host Games 1996-2024.

### Modeling Process

For each host year, compute host_gold/prev_gold - 1 and host_total/prev_total - 1: 1996 US: gold +0.19, total -0.06; 2000 Australia: gold +0.78, total +0.41; 2004 Greece: gold +0.50, total +0.23; 2008 China: gold +0.50, total +0.59; 2012 GB: gold +0.53, total +0.27; 2016 Brazil: gold +1.33, total +0.12; 2020 Japan: gold +1.25, total +0.41; 2024 France: gold +0.60, total +0.94. Mean total bump = +0.37, mean gold bump = +0.68; the total bumps span -0.06 to +0.94. The model uses phi = 0.15 on both gold and total (exchange 3, midpoint of the 10-30% band, applied to the blended mean), which is below the historical mean total bump — a conservative choice: it reflects that several of the historical jumps (China 2008, France 2024) were record-setting Games whose reversion is already handled by the blend, so the pure 'host' component is the residual. Sensitivity: sweep phi = 0.05/0.15/0.25 gives all-country total MAE 4.81/4.77/4.73 — the backtest is nearly insensitive to phi, so the 0.15 choice is not a fragile point.

### Outcome Analysis

The host effect is real in the data (7 of 8 hosts beat their previous Games in total medals; the mean total bump is +37%) but highly variable (-6% to +94%) and concentrated in mid-tier hosts (Brazil +1.33 gold on a small base, Japan +1.25, France +0.94) and one dominant host (China 2008 +0.50). The US 1996 case is the natural analog for 2028 and shows the bump can be modest for an already-dominant host (+19% gold, -6% total). The model therefore applies a bounded +15% to the US for 2028 rather than extrapolating the historical mean, and the reversion blend ensures that if the US 2024 record reverts, the host bump does not mask it. Residual risk: a US 2028 performance like China 2008 (+59% total) would fall outside the model's band, but that is a once-in-decades event and is exactly what the wide prediction intervals are meant to contain.

## Subtask 7: Subtask 7: The 'great coach' effect — evidence from the data, its estimated contribution, and three countries plus sport

### Problem

Subtask 7: The 'great coach' effect — evidence from the data, its estimated contribution, and three countries plus sports to invest in, with an impact estimate.

### Analysis

A 'great coach' should show up as a country-sport whose gold output jumps well above what the programme change explains between two Games, holding the event counts roughly constant. Exchange 6 gave the prior: a great coach moves a sport by a few medals, about 10-30% of that sport's contribution to the country's total, concentrated in 1-2 disciplines, and smaller than the athlete-talent base (a coach cannot manufacture depth). The data test is: for each country-sport with golds in 2008 and 2024, compare gold_2024 to gold_2008 * (events_2024/events_2008) — the programme-scaled expectation — and flag jumps with ratio >= 2 and gold_2024 >= 3 (coach_candidates.csv).

### Modeling Process

coach_candidates.csv (7 candidates): Uzbekistan-Boxing: 0 gold in 2008 -> 5 in 2024 (11->13 events, expected 0, ratio 10x); Germany-Equestrian: 0 -> 4 (6->6 events, ratio 8x); Canada-Athletics: 0 -> 3 (47->48 events, ratio 6x); Japan-Wrestling: 2 -> 8 (18->18 events, ratio 4x); Netherlands-Rowing: 1 -> 4 (14->14 events, ratio 4x); South Korea-Shooting: 1 -> 3 (15->15 events, ratio 3x); South Korea-Archery: 2 -> 5 (4->5 events, ratio 2x). In every case the event counts are nearly unchanged, so the jump is a performance effect, not a programme effect — the data signature of coaching/talent-pipeline investment. Magnitude check against exchange 6: each jump is 3-8 golds, i.e. a few medals, and for each country it is 10-60% of that sport's contribution to the country's total (e.g. Uzbekistan's 5 boxing golds are 63% of its 8 total 2024 golds — at the upper edge because boxing was the only sport, consistent with 'concentrated in one discipline'). Three investment picks with estimated impact (a coach-quality uplift of 10-30% of the sport's contribution, exchange 6, applied to a country-sport that has a demonstrated recent base but is below its persistence peers): (1) Japan-Wrestling — already 8 golds in 2024 and the #1 sport for Japan; a coaching-quality uplift of 10-30% on a base that has been 2-8 golds per Games since 2008 estimates +1 to +2 golds per Games (Japan is the most coach-leveraged country in the table: Wrestling is 40% of its golds and the 2008->2024 jump of 2->8 shows the system already responds to investment). (2) Uzbekistan-Boxing — 5 golds in 2024 on a 0 base in 2008 is the strongest recent coach/talent-pipeline signal in the data; sustaining and extending it (adding weight-class depth) estimates +1 to +2 more golds, and because Uzbekistan is single-sport, those are nearly all new country golds. (3) South Korea-Archery/Shooting — both jumped 2008->2024 (Archery 2->5, Shooting 1->3) with flat programmes; combined coaching investment estimates +1 gold (the two sports are 54% of Korea's 2024 golds, so a 10-30% uplift on their combined 8-gold base is +1 to +2, floored at +1 given the small event counts).

### Outcome Analysis

Evidence is positive and specific: 7 country-sports show the programme-invariant jump signature, and the 3 picks are those with (i) a recent multi-gold base (not a one-off), (ii) near-constant event counts (so the jump is not a programme artifact), and (iii) a sport that is a large share of the country's total (so the uplift propagates). Estimated contribution per exchange 6: a few medals, 10-30% of the sport's contribution — the picks are sized to that band (+1 to +2 golds each, +3 to +5 country golds across the three if all succeed), which is realistic against the historical evidence (the observed jumps are 3-8 golds, i.e. one-off realizations of a sustained coaching investment). The effect is bounded: exchange 6's 'a coach cannot manufacture depth' is reflected in the model by never crediting a coach beyond the sport's event-count ceiling (E_st bounds the possible medals in s). The three picks are also consistent with the persistence finding (Subtask 5): they are country-sports that have already medaled in recent Games, i.e. the investment extends an existing pipeline rather than creating one from nothing, which is the higher-probability use of coaching money.

## Subtask 8: Subtask 8: An original insight from the analysis.

### Problem

Subtask 8: An original insight from the analysis.

### Analysis

Beyond the persistence and host-bump findings, the most novel structural fact in the data is the near-perfect persistence of a small set of country-sports: 10 country-sport combinations won at least one gold in all 7 of the 7 Games from 2000 to 2024, with zero missed Games (max gap 0). This is a stronger statement than 'countries are persistent in their sports' (exchange 1): it says that for a specific set of country-sport pairs, the probability of winning at least one gold in any given modern Games is empirically 1.0, and that these pairs, not countries, are the atomic units of Olympic medal production.

### Modeling Process

Measured in persistence_2000_2024.csv: for each (country, sport), count Games in 2000, 2004, 2008, 2012, 2016, 2020, 2024 with >=1 gold, and the maximum run of consecutive Games without gold. The 7/7 set: Australia-Swimming, China-Badminton, China-Diving, China-Shooting, China-Table Tennis, China-Weightlifting, Cuba-Wrestling, Ethiopia-Athletics, Great Britain-Sailing, Japan-Judo. Of the ~1,100 country-sport pairs with 2+ gold Games in the window, these 10 are the only ones with zero gaps. Two structural consequences: (1) The medal table's top tier is actually a country-sport table — China's 5, Japan's 1, Australia's 1, GB's 1, Ethiopia's 1, Cuba's 1 guaranteed golds explain a large share of the top-10 totals before any other sport is counted, which is why the model's sport-level baselines, not country-level averages, produce the top-10 ranking.
