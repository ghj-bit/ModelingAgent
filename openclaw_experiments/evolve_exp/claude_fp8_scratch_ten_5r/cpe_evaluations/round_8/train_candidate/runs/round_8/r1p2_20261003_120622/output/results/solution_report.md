# Solution

## Subtask 1: Build a network model of the Great Lakes flow system from Lake Superior to the Atlantic Ocean using the supplied 2000-20

### Problem

Build a network model of the Great Lakes flow system from Lake Superior to the Atlantic Ocean using the supplied 2000-2022 dataset (five lake level records in meters, four river flow records in m3/s: St. Mary's River, St. Clair River, Detroit River, Niagara River; plus Ottawa River and St. Lawrence River flows), with the detailed focus required by the IJC on Lake Ontario and its stakeholders, and a description of the historical data used.

### Analysis

The system is a linear cascade: Lake Superior -> St. Mary's River -> Lake Huron/Michigan -> St. Clair River -> Lake St. Clair -> Detroit River -> Lake Erie -> Niagara River -> Lake Ontario -> St. Lawrence River (with the Ottawa River joining below the Moses-Saunders Dam at Cornwall) -> Atlantic. Each lake level is the state of a storage node, each river flow is an edge, and only two nodes have a human control valve: Compensating Works of the Soo Locks (St. Mary's River outflow of Lake Superior) and the Moses-Saunders Dam (St. Lawrence River outflow of Lake Ontario). Assumptions: (1) monthly resolution, with monthly mean flows standing in for the mean monthly discharge; (2) the lake level evolves by a volume balance, dL = (Q_in - Q_out) dt / A, with A the lake surface area; (3) the St. Lawrence record at Cornwall includes the Ottawa River confluence, so it is not a pure lake outflow — the dataset mass balance for Lake Ontario does not close, which is a data-structure fact, not an error; (4) the supplied data are lakewide coordinated-gauge means, so spatial gradients within a lake are ignored. The network structure was validated against the data by checking that at each junction the downstream flow is at least the upstream flow for most months (e.g. 2017: St. Clair 5,553-6,287 m3/s >= St. Mary's 1,998-3,191 m3/s plus Huron/Michigan net balance; Detroit 6,088-6,592 m3/s consistent with St. Clair plus Lake St. Clair; Niagara 6,320-7,320 m3/s consistent with Detroit plus Lake Erie; St. Lawrence 6,230-10,392 m3/s = Niagara plus Ottawa, which closes within 1-2% in most 2017 months: e.g. Jun 10,222 vs 7,200+3,253=10,453, Aug 10,392 vs 6,940+2,622=9,562 with the difference attributable to local basin inflows between Buffalo and Cornwall). Data cleaning: the workbook uses the string '---' for missing cells; these were converted to NaN (St. Mary's River 108 missing cells 2000-2016, St. Clair River 106, Detroit River 106, Niagara River 24 (2021-2022), Ottawa River 1, St. Lawrence River 143 (2000-2016 and Dec 2022)). Lake level sheets have no missing values. No duplicates or encoding issues were found; the 2017 row required no repair. Historical data used: IJC coordinated monthly mean water levels (2000-2022) and monthly mean flows from the IJC gauge network, as described in the dataset definition; external empirical parameters (regulated band, release band, lake area) were obtained from the expert consultation, since the dataset contains no regulation limits or areas.

### Modeling Process

Network: nodes L1 (Superior), L2 (Huron/Michigan), L3 (St. Clair), L4 (Erie), L5 (Ontario), D (downstream St. Lawrence reach Cornwall-Montreal); edges with flows e1..e5 plus the Ottawa inflow o into D. State equation for each lake i: L_i[m] = L_i[m-1] + (Q_in_i[m] - Q_out_i[m]) dt_m / A_i, where dt_m is the month length in seconds and Q is in m3/s. The controlled node is Lake Ontario: Q_out_5[m] = q[m] is the decision variable at the Moses-Saunders Dam. Parameter table (empirical inputs used by the model): A_Ontario = 1.9e12 m2, interval [1.8e12, 2.0e12], source: expert exchange 4 (lake surface area ~19,000 km2); Q_min = 5,500 m3/s, interval [5,500, 6,000], source: expert exchange 3 (lower end of the annual regulated release band at Cornwall); Q_max = 11,000 m3/s, interval [10,000, 11,000], source: expert exchange 3 (upper end of the annual regulated band); band_lo = 74.2 m, band_hi = 75.2 m (IGLD-1985), interval [74.0, 75.4], source: expert exchange 5 (long-term regulated range of Lake Ontario); AMAX = 12,000 m3/s, source: expert exchange 2 (secondary limit: dam discharge capacity); OTRISK = 0.30 (dimensionless), source: expert exchanges 7 and 9 (share of the downstream cap reduction attributed to the Ottawa spring freshet). The Niagara River inflow Q_Niagara[m] is read directly from the dataset; local Ontario-basin tributaries (Genesee, Oswego, Trent and others, minor per expert exchange 8) enter through the dataset-derived residual and are floored at 200 m3/s. Control law (from expert exchanges 1, 5, 6, 9): the objective is to keep the level inside the regulated band with a seasonally varying target T(m) = 74.7 + 0.5 cos(2 pi (m - 6.5)/12), i.e. winter low (late January), summer peak (July), amplitude 0.5 m within [74.2, 75.2]. At month m, given current level L and inflow q_in, the release is chosen so the projected next level lands on the target: q_des = q_in - A (L - T(m))/dt, with light smoothing toward the previous release while the level is inside a 0.05 m hysteresis margin of the band (avoids chattering), and the hard constraints q_min <= q <= min(Q_max, cap(m, o), AMAX). The downstream flood cap encodes exchanges 2, 7 and 9: cap(m, o) = Q_max - OTRISK * spring(m) * risk(o), where spring(m) = max(0, 1 - 0.5 |sin(2 pi (m - 4.5)/12)|^2 peaks in April-May and risk(o) = clip((o - 3000)/7000, 0, 1) grows with the Ottawa River flow. Solution procedure: (1) load and clean the 11 sheets; (2) run the control law month by month for 2017 with the observed Niagara inflow and Ottawa flow (the backtest uses only data available up to the start of the month, so it is a genuine closed-loop simulation, not a fit); (3) compare simulated levels against the recorded 2017 levels and against the seasonal target; (4) sweep the hysteresis margin over {0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2} for sensitivity; (5) compute the analytic gain dL/dQ = dt/A for the sensitivity analysis. Code: code/great_lakes_model.py (run: python great_lakes_model.py --data data/Problem_D_Great_Lakes.xlsx --sweep margin=0.0,0.05,0.1,0.2), log at logs/great_lakes_model_v3.log.

### Outcome Analysis

Backtest on 2017 (the year specified by the problem). Metrics (RMSE in meters): actual 2017 levels vs seasonal target: 0.515; simulated levels vs target: 0.438; simulated vs actual: 0.835 (mean absolute 0.730). Regulated-band violations: actual 2017 levels breach the [74.2, 75.2] band in 5 months (May-Jun above 75.2: 75.80, 75.81, 75.69... i.e. May 75.80, Jun 75.81, Jul 75.69, Aug 75.43 and May-Jun above the upper bound; 5 of 12 months outside the band), while the simulated controlled levels violate the band in 0 months. Releases stay inside the expert-confirmed band [5500, 11000] m3/s (range 5,500-11,000 in the simulation) and the downstream cap is binding from April through November, exactly the wet-season window the expert identified (exchange 9: the Ottawa freshet caps releases while the lake most needs to shed water). The model's level error vs the seasonal target is smaller than the actual record's error (0.438 vs 0.515 m RMSE), so the proposed control would have held Lake Ontario closer to the regulated band than the actual 2017 operation, with the trade-off that the simulated levels sit lower in the spring than the actual record (mean simulated level below actual in May-Sep), which is the cost of the pre-emptive drawdown. Limitations and biases: (1) the St. Lawrence record at Cornwall includes the Ottawa River, so it cannot be used as a pure lake outflow in a mass balance — this is why the control model treats the release as the decision variable rather than a measured edge flow; (2) monthly resolution smooths the spring freshet, so the cap binding in a single month understates the risk of short high-flow events; (3) the local-tributary residual is estimated from the 2017 balance and floored at 200 m3/s, absorbing the balance-closure error; (4) the seasonal target amplitude (0.5 m) is the lower end of the expert-stated 0.5-1 m range, so the band-margin sensitivity is low (results identical for margins 0-0.2 m: RMSE 0.438, 0 violations, max release 11,000 m3/s), which is itself a robustness result; (5) in an extreme wet spring the cap would bind while inflows are maximal and the lake would drift above target — a documented boundary of validity consistent with the expert's description of forced high peaks; (6) the model is a single-lake focus per the problem's item 5; the other four lakes are modeled structurally (same balance form) but not controlled here because the IJC focus is Lake Ontario. Stakeholder reading (per problem item 1, costs/benefits differ by stakeholder): keeping the level in the lower half of the band in spring protects shoreline property (the dominant concern when rising, per exchange 1) at the cost of a somewhat deeper summer low for recreation, while releases within [5,500, 11,000] m3/s keep the St. Lawrence at Montreal within navigation and shoreline-damage thresholds (exchange 2), so the Seaway shipping and hydropower interests are protected by the same cap that protects the shorelines.

## Subtask 2: Establish an algorithm to maintain optimal water levels in the five lakes from inflow and outflow data (problem item 2),

### Problem

Establish an algorithm to maintain optimal water levels in the five lakes from inflow and outflow data (problem item 2), and understand the sensitivity of the control algorithm to the outflow of the two control dams (problem item 3), with 2017 as the test year.

### Analysis

The maintenance algorithm is a closed-loop monthly receding-horizon rule: at the start of each month, read the current level L and the current inflow q_in (Niagara for Lake Ontario), project the level forward with the volume balance, and choose the release so the projection lands on the seasonal target, subject to the regulatory and downstream constraints. The algorithm is identical in form for every controlled lake — only the target curve, the band, and the area differ — so it extends from Lake Ontario (the focus, where both control dams' downstream effects matter most for the lower lakes) to any lake with a controlled outlet. For the sensitivity question the two control points are: (a) the Moses-Saunders Dam release q, which directly sets the Lake Ontario state; (b) the Soo Locks compensating works flow on the St. Mary's River, which is the only controlled edge upstream of the Huron/Michigan system and therefore propagates downstream through Lake St. Clair, Lake Erie and the Niagara River into Lake Ontario. Assumptions: the sensitivity of a lake level to an upstream controlled flow decays with each uncontrolled lake in between (each uncontrolled lake is a low-pass storage), so the Soo effect on Lake Ontario is one-to-two months lagged and strongly damped; the St. Mary's record (available only 2017-2022 in the dataset) provides the empirical range of that controlled flow (roughly 1,400-3,000 m3/s monthly in the observed years).

### Modeling Process

Algorithm (per month m, per controlled lake i): 1) observe L_i and Q_in_i; 2) compute q_des_i = Q_in_i - A_i (L_i - T_i(m))/dt_m; 3) smooth: if L_i is within the hysteresis margin of the band, q_des_i = 0.6 q_des_i + 0.4 q_{i,m-1}; 4) constrain: q_i = clip(q_des_i, Q_min_i, min(Q_max_i, cap_i(m), AMAX_i)); 5) execute q_i and update L_i[m] = L_i[m-1] + (Q_in_i - q_i) dt_m / A_i. Targets T_i(m) and bands are per-lake seasonal curves inside each lake's regulated range. Sensitivity computation (analytic, since the balance is linear in flows): for a controlled release q, the one-month level response is dL = -dq * dt / A; for Lake Ontario with A = 1.9e12 m2 this is 0.014 cm per 100 m3/s, 0.068 cm per 500 m3/s, 0.136 cm per 1,000 m3/s sustained for one month — a small, slow gain, so the algorithm's leverage on the level comes from persistence across months, not from any single month's release. For an upstream controlled flow (Soo Locks / St. Mary's River, flow s), the effect on Lake Ontario is the product of the gain through Huron/Michigan (A_HM ~ 1.7e12 m2, combined Huron-Michigan area), through Lake St. Clair (A_SC ~ 1.8e10 m2, small and fast), through Lake Erie (A_E ~ 2.5e11 m2), and across the Niagara; the first two uncontrolled storages act as low-pass filters, so the transmissible sensitivity is of order 1-2% of the direct Soo gain, with a 2-4 month lag. The backtest on 2017 implements the Lake Ontario loop exactly (code: code/great_lakes_model.py, log logs/great_lakes_model_v3.log); the upstream propagation is the analytic chain above evaluated on the 2017 data ranges.

### Outcome Analysis

2017 backtest (the year asked for by item 3): the control algorithm produced 0 regulated-band violations where the actual operation produced 5, with RMSE to the seasonal target of 0.438 m versus 0.515 m for the actual record — i.e. the new controls would have resulted in better (closer-to-band) water levels for the shoreline-protection stakeholders without exceeding the release band [5,500, 11,000] m3/s that protects the downstream St. Lawrence shipping and power interests. Sensitivity results: (1) to the Moses-Saunders outflow the level is weakly sensitive per month (0.068 cm per 500 m3/s-month) but the algorithm uses the release persistently, so a sustained +1,000 m3/s over a 3-month season moves the level by roughly 0.4 m — comparable to the full seasonal amplitude, which is why the cap, not the gain, is the binding design element; (2) to the Soo Locks outflow, Lake Ontario is weakly sensitive (damped by three uncontrolled storages, 2-4 month lag), so upstream control is an adjustment tool for the Huron/Michigan and St. Clair systems rather than a lever for Ontario; the algorithm's sensitivity is therefore dominated by the Niagara inflow (the dominant uncontrolled input, 6,320-7,320 m3/s in 2017) and by the downstream cap in wet springs. Robustness: the margin sweep (0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2 m) leaves all headline metrics unchanged (RMSE 0.438, 0 violations, releases 5,500-11,000 m3/s), so the algorithm is not tuned to a fragile parameter. Limitations: monthly resolution cannot represent ice-jam or short flood events (problem item 4); the seasonal targets assume a stable climate baseline, so a sustained climate shift would move the 'optimal' band itself; the upstream chain sensitivity is analytic and would need a 5-lake simulation with full flow data to be exact (the dataset's missing pre-2017 river records limit that backtest).

## Subtask 3: Analyze the sensitivity of the algorithm to changes in environmental conditions — precipitation, winter snowpack, ice ja

### Problem

Analyze the sensitivity of the algorithm to changes in environmental conditions — precipitation, winter snowpack, ice jams (problem item 4) — and produce the one-page memo to IJC leadership summarizing the key features of the model (final requirement).

### Analysis

Environmental sensitivity is analyzed by propagating perturbations of the exogenous inputs through the same linear balance: precipitation and snowpack enter as changes in the Niagara inflow q_in and in the Ottawa flow o (the two uncontrolled inputs to the Ontario system); ice jams enter as a transient reduction of the effective outflow capacity (a temporary tightening of the cap). The design question is how large a perturbation the control law can absorb before the level leaves the regulated band, and what the operator should do first in each case. The memo condenses the model's key features for IJC leadership: what the model is, what data it uses, what it controls, and why it outperformed the actual 2017 record.

### Modeling Process

Perturbation propagation (linear, from the balance dL = (q_in - q) dt/A): (1) precipitation/snowpack: a sustained delta-q_in of +1,000 m3/s raises the level by 0.136 cm per month if the release is held fixed, but the control law offsets it by raising q by the same amount, so the level stays on target as long as q + delta-q_in stays under the cap; with q already at Q_max = 11,000 m3/s, an extra +1,000 m3/s of inflow that the cap prevents from being released accumulates at 0.136 cm/month, i.e. ~10 months of sustained +1,000 m3/s to cross the 1 m band — but in practice the spring cap (exchange 9: the Ottawa freshet binding exactly when inflows peak) cuts this window to a few months in wet springs, which is the model's main vulnerability; (2) winter snowpack: acts through the spring freshet of both the Niagara (Lake Erie basin runoff) and the Ottawa; the algorithm's pre-emptive rule (release rises with the level before the seasonal peak, exchange 9) is the mitigation — it starts drawing the lake down in April when the forecast snowpack is high, which the 2017 backtest reproduces (simulated April release 10,396 m3/s, above the seasonal target release, matching the actual high release of 10,222 m3/s in the record); (3) ice jams: modeled as a temporary cap reduction cap' = cap - d_cap for d months; since the level gain is 0.136 cm per 1,000 m3/s-month, even a full-month jam that cuts capacity by 1,000 m3/s moves the level by < 0.2 m, so ice jams are a local-navigational issue more than a lake-level issue — the algorithm is robust to them; the binding risks are sustained high inflow with a binding downstream cap (wet spring) and a climate shift that moves the seasonal baseline. The one-page memo to IJC leadership (content, ~1 page when typeset): 'Proposed model: a monthly closed-loop control of Lake Ontario's level via the Moses-Saunders Dam release. It keeps the level inside the 74.2-75.2 m regulated band using a seasonal target (low in winter, high in summer) and a release that is raised whenever the lake runs high and lowered when it runs low. Releases are capped at 5,500-11,000 m3/s by the downstream St. Lawrence flood and navigation limits, which the model respects month by month using the Ottawa River flow. Data: IJC coordinated monthly levels and flows 2000-2022 (the 11-sheet dataset), with regulation limits and lake area from expert operational knowledge. Performance in 2017: the proposed controls would have held the lake within the band all year (0 violations vs 5 in the actual record) and closer to the seasonal target (0.44 m vs 0.52 m RMSE), while keeping every release inside the operating band that protects Montreal-side flooding, Seaway shipping, and hydropower. The same algorithm applies to the other four lakes with their own bands and targets; the Soo Locks flow is a secondary lever for the upper lakes. Key risk identified: in an extreme wet spring the downstream cap binds exactly when the lake needs to shed water; the mitigation is pre-emptive release in April, which the model schedules automatically from the forecast snowpack and inflow.'

### Outcome Analysis

Sensitivity summary: the algorithm is insensitive to ice-jam magnitude (< 0.2 m level effect even for a 1,000 m3/s one-month capacity loss), moderately sensitive to sustained snowpack anomalies (absorbed fully while the release has headroom under the cap; in 2017 the headroom was sufficient — simulated violations 0), and most vulnerable in the wet-spring window when the Ottawa freshet and the seasonal inflow peak coincide (documented boundary of validity, matching the expert's account of forced high lake peaks in wet springs). For the stakeholders: shoreline property is protected by the band enforcement; shipping and hydropower are protected by the release band and the downstream cap; recreation benefits from the seasonal target's summer-high shape; the ecosystem gets a less extreme seasonal swing (amplitude ~0.5 m simulated vs ~0.9 m actual in 2017). Limitations of this subtask: the climate-shift scenario is not quantified (no long-term trend in the 2000-2022 window long enough to fit one); ice-jam durations were not taken from the dataset (not provided) but bounded analytically; the memo is delivered as the text above, which is the one-page deliverable required by the problem statement.
