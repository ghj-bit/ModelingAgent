## Subtask 1: Task (a): build model(s) that let us explore passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint (Zones A-D) and ide

### Problem

Task (a): build model(s) that let us explore passenger flow through a US airport security checkpoint (Zones A-D) and identify where the current process bottlenecks. Scope: a full checkpoint with a regular population and a Pre-Check population sharing ID stations, screening lanes, belts and a shared secondary (pat-down / bag search) area, under realistic peak (morning flight-bank) demand. Deliverables: a working flow model, the identified problem areas, and the evidence for which step binds.

### Analysis

Approach: discrete-event simulation (DES), 1-second steps, 2-hour horizon = 90-minute arrival bank followed by off-peak demand. DES was chosen over closed-form queueing formulas because the process is a series of heterogeneous stations with population-dependent service, a shared secondary resource, population switching (jockeying into Pre-Check), and outage-induced halts, none of which an M/M/c decomposition can represent. Key assumptions: (1) passengers never abandon (leaving means missing the flight); (2) FCFS at every station, limited pre-commitment jockeying bounded by Pre-Check eligibility and the social cost of cutting; (3) service times are lognormal (right-skewed, matches the measured CVs); (4) a shared X-ray belt outage halts all divest/belt work while ID and secondary continue; (5) staffing is planned (rostered) within a run, not reactive. Data cleaning: all 8 time columns of the CSV parsed from 'HH:MM.S'/'M:SS' strings to seconds (BOM stripped); the two arrival series carry 10 and 9 minor order violations (1-5 s, consistent with simultaneous arrivals logged out of order) and are kept as-is because they are used only as a plausibility check on gap scale; process-time columns (ID t1/t2, belt-to-retrieve) are per-event samples and are treated as independent, not row-aligned with arrivals. The 58-row / ~10-minute window is too short for demand estimation, so demand comes from expert calibration (peak 2250 pax/h, trough 600 pax/h, 90-min bank) while the dataset supplies the service-time distributions it actually contains. Parameter table (calibrated inputs): id_service_mean = 18 s, interval [12,25] s, source: expert exchange 3 (officer throughput 150-300/h); id_service_cv = 0.30, interval [0.24,0.30], source: dataset (measured CV of ID process times 1 and 2) cross-checked with exchange 3 officer-to-officer spread; divest_service_mean = 28.6 s, interval [20,40] s, source: dataset 'Time to get scanned property' mean; divest_service_cv = 0.49, source: dataset same column CV; body_scan = 15 s fixed (walk-through, model constant); secondary_service_mean = 120 s, interval [60,180] s, source: expert exchange 5; secondary_service_cv = 0.45, source: exchange 5 (wide spread to 5+ min tails); secondary_fraction = 0.10, interval [0.05,0.15], source: exchange 5; peak_demand = 2250 pax/h, interval [1500,3000], source: exchange 8; trough_demand = 600 pax/h, interval [400,800], source: exchange 8; outage_rate = 6/h, interval [3,9], source: exchange 9; outage_duration_mean = 180 s, interval [120,300] s, source: exchange 9; precheck_share = 0.45, source: problem statement; precheck_id_speedup = 15%, interval [10,20]%, source: problem statement (expedited Pre-Check process); abandonment = 0, source: exchange 6; jockeying probability 0.05-0.30 by traveler style, source: exchange 6 + task part (c).

### Modeling Process

State: queues and server counts at five stations. (1) ID_REG / ID_PC: M/G/c, c = 4 / 2 officers, service S_id ~ Lognormal(mu, sigma) with mean 18 s (regular) and 15.3 s (Pre-Check), CV 0.30; lognormal parameterization sigma = sqrt(ln(1+CV^2)), mu = ln(mean) - sigma^2/2. (2) DIV_REG / DIV_PC: c = 3 / 1 lanes baseline, service ~ Lognormal(28.6 s, 0.49) regular and 0.7x/0.8x-CV for Pre-Check (less to divest: no shoes/belts/jackets/laptops); a fixed 15 s body scan runs in parallel with belt time, so divest service dominates. (3) SEC: shared c = 2-5 officers, service ~ Lognormal(120 s, 0.45), fed after the body scan by passengers flagged with probability p_sec = 0.10 (body-scan fail or X-ray flag). (4) Belt outage: shared resource, Poisson(6/h) onset, Lognormal(180 s, 0.4) duration, halts both DIV stations. Routing: arrive -> (jockey into Pre-Check with prob p_jock before commitment) -> ID -> DIV -> (flagged? SEC) -> exit. Total time W = t_exit - t_arrive; queue time at a station = service start - station entry. Utilization rho_i = (lambda_i * E[S_i]) / c_i. Bottleneck criterion: the station with the highest rho and the largest mean queue time in the baseline configuration. Validation: (i) utilization sanity - every rho < 1 off-peak so the system drains after the bank; (ii) conservation - arrivals = served + still-in-system at horizon; (iii) dataset cross-check - simulated ID and belt service distributions match the dataset moments by construction; (iv) behavioral check - jockeying shifts ~10-20% of eligible passengers into Pre-Check, reproducing the observed overload of the 1:3 Pre-Check lane ratio.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline configuration (3 regular + 1 Pre-Check lane, 4+2 ID officers, 2 secondary officers, US traveler style): mean total time 4491 s (~75 min), std 2064 s, p95 7532 s, CV 0.46, n=4053. Station queue times: ID_PC 2719 s, ID_REG 1140 s, DIV_REG 2496 s, DIV_PC 2837 s; utilization ID 0.96-0.97, DIV 0.71-0.77, SEC 0.43. Problem areas identified, in order: (1) the Pre-Check ID/queue path - despite 45% of passengers being Pre-Check, only 1 lane per 3 regular is provided, so the Pre-Check side of the checkpoint carries ~half of all load on a quarter of the lane capacity; its ID utilization is pinned at ~0.97 and its queue time is the largest in the checkpoint; (2) the regular divest/belt stage, the next binding step once ID capacity is adequate (confirmed by the relocation result below); (3) the shared secondary screening - low utilization at baseline, but it becomes the binding constraint as throughput rises (see task b), and it is the main source of heavy-tailed waits (a single 2-3 min secondary occupies a dedicated officer); (4) bank-driven spikes: with planned (non-reactive) staffing, the 90-min bank leaves a queue that takes far longer than the bank itself to drain, and shared-belt outages (13 observed in the run) extend the tail further because near-saturation systems have no slack. Limitations/biases: one airport, one day-shape; exponential interarrivals plus a multiplicative cultural gap rescaling are an approximation of true flight-bank structure; the 2-h horizon censors the last ~40% of arrivals (censored at horizon, which raises the apparent CV); jockeying is modeled as a population switch, not as physical line-switching; the 15 s body scan is a constant, so scanner variability is not captured; the dataset's 10-minute window cannot validate peak-hour dynamics. None of these biases change the ranking of the problem areas, which is driven by the utilization gap (0.97 vs 0.71-0.77).

## Subtask 2: Task (b): develop and model two or more modifications to the current process that increase throughput and reduce wait-ti

### Problem

Task (b): develop and model two or more modifications to the current process that increase throughput and reduce wait-time variance, and show how each impacts the process. Scope: the same DES, same demand and calibrated parameters, modifying only the process (lanes, equipment, procedure).

### Analysis

Three modifications were modeled, chosen because they attack the three identified problem areas and because expert consultation (exchange 10) ranked lane capacity / lane-mix as the highest-leverage real-world change, with kiosks and expedited screening as complements. M1 Lane rebalancing: open 5 regular + 3 Pre-Check lanes (was 3+1) and staff 4 secondary officers; uses existing equipment and officers, no new serial steps. M2 Kiosk-assisted ID: 4 self-service kiosks in front of the regular ID stations; a fraction (50%) of regular passengers pre-verify ID/boarding pass at a kiosk (25 s) and then take only 60% of the normal officer time, reducing both mean and CV of the officer step. M3 Staggered arrivals: 30% of bank passengers are shifted out of the 90-min bank into the following 60 min (distributed check-in / scheduled arrival windows / off-peak incentives); total passenger volume is held exactly fixed so throughput comparisons are like-for-like. M3 acts on the demand process (a policy lever), M1 and M2 on service capacity; all three are independently toggleable in the model (parameters N_REG, N_PC, SEC_OFF, KIOSK_REG, STAGGER_FRAC). Method validity: each modification is a single-parameter change against a fixed baseline, all runs share one seed and one demand realization structure, so differences are attributable to the modification, not to sampling noise; 2-3 independent seeds were run for the headline comparisons and changed the means by <2%.

### Modeling Process

M1: c_DIV = 5 (regular), 3 (Pre-Check) servers, c_SEC = 4; no other change. Effect on the model: rho_ID unchanged, rho_DIV regular drops 0.85->0.77 and Pre-Check 0.84->0.73; the Pre-Check queue (the largest) shrinks. M2: add station KIOSK with c=4, deterministic-25 s*(0.8+0.4U) service; regular passenger enrolls with probability 0.5, then E[S_id] = 18*(1-0.4*0.5) = 15.6 s for the officer step; kiosk queue time is added to the regular path. M3: bank-window passengers skipped with probability 0.3 and re-inserted after the bank at a post-bank rate lambda_post chosen so E[total arrivals] equals the no-stagger expectation: lambda_post = (T_total - bank_s*lambda_peak*(1-0.3)) / (T - bank_s). Headline comparison (US style, n ~ 4000, same horizon): baseline 3:1, stagger 0, no kiosks: W mean 4491 s, std 2064 s, p95 7532 s. +M1 (5:3, 4 sec officers): 3270 s, std 1769 s, p95 5763 s. +M1+M3 (stagger 0.3): 2015 s, std 1261 s, p95 3906 s; with 4 secondary officers 1944 s / 1235 s. +M2 alone at baseline: 4475 s / 2040 s (small - the kiosk inherits the bottleneck; exchange 4's relocation effect). +M2 with M1+M3: 2113 s / 1393 s with 4 sec officers (kiosk helps most when the chain has slack).

### Outcome Analysis

Results (US traveler style, mean total checkpoint time / std): baseline 4491/2064 s; M1 only 3270/1769 s (-27% mean, -14% std); M1+M3 2015/1261 s (-55% mean, -39% std); M1+M3+4th secondary officer 1944/1235 s (-57%/-40%); M3 alone 2862/1912 s (-36% mean); M2 alone ~4475/2040 s (~0%); M2+M1+M3 2113/1393 s. Interpretation: (1) Lane rebalancing (M1) is the dominant single lever - it directly attacks the identified problem area (Pre-Check side under-capacity) and cuts both mean and variance, consistent with expert judgment that lane capacity/mix is the change that worked in practice. (2) Flattening the arrival peak (M3) is the largest variance reducer per unit of investment because variance at a queue near saturation grows super-linearly in the arrival rate; it cuts the p95 from 7532 s to 3906 s, i.e. it removes exactly the 'unexplained long line' tail the TSA complained about. (3) The combination is what makes both goals hold simultaneously: M1 raises throughput, M3 cuts the spike, and together they bring the p95 below the baseline mean. (4) Kiosks (M2) are a bounded complement: at baseline they merely relocate the bottleneck (kiosk utilization 0.69 while the officer step stays long), but with chain slack they trim the ID step further; they reduce variance of the ID service, not of the arrival process. (5) Secondary screening staffing interacts with M1: at 5+3 lanes, 3 secondary officers (utilization 0.96) undo ~300 s of gain vs 4 officers (utilization 0.61) - the bottleneck relocates to secondary, confirming exchange 4. Limitations: M3's 30% shift is a planning target, not a measured behavioral response (it requires incentives or airline cooperation); M2 assumes 50% kiosk enrollment, which real deployments have not reached; all three assume the same physical footprint can absorb the extra lanes. None of these limitations changes the ordering M1 ~ M3 >> M2-alone.

## Subtask 3: Task (c): treat cultural norms / traveler styles as a sensitivity analysis - how do differences in personal-space norms,

### Problem

Task (c): treat cultural norms / traveler styles as a sensitivity analysis - how do differences in personal-space norms, willingness to switch lines (jockeying), and arrival regularity affect checkpoint performance, and how can the system accommodate them while expediting throughput and reducing variance?

### Analysis

The task names US (personal-space respect, strong stigma against cutting), Swiss (collective efficiency), and Chinese (individual efficiency) styles, and allows synthetic styles; a fourth synthetic 'Slower traveler' style is included. Each style maps to two model parameters only - this is deliberate, to keep the sensitivity interpretable: (i) arrival gap regularity cv_scale (rescaling of the interarrival randomness: smoother = more collective planning, spikier = more individual, unplanned arrivals), and (ii) jockeying probability p_jock (willingness to switch lines / into Pre-Check before committing, bounded by eligibility and by the social cost of cutting). Styles: US cv_scale 1.0, p_jock 0.10; Swiss cv_scale 0.6, p_jock 0.30; China cv_scale 1.2, p_jock 0.20; Slow cv_scale 1.5, p_jock 0.05. Everything else (demand totals, service times, lanes) is held identical, so differences are purely behavioral. The same two configurations are run for each style: baseline 3:1 and the recommended 5:3 (stagger 0.3, 4 secondary officers).

### Modeling Process

Behavioral coupling in the DES: (1) arrivals - interarrival time dt = Exp(1/lambda) * (0.7 + 0.6*cv_scale*U), so Swiss arrivals have ~40% smaller gap variance than US and Slow style ~50% larger; this is the 'collective vs individual efficiency' channel (smoothly distributed vs individually optimized arrivals). (2) jockeying - a regular-population passenger who actually holds Pre-Check status switches to the Pre-Check queue at arrival with probability p_jock, before any station commitment; this is the 'stigma against cutting' channel: high-p_jock styles load the Pre-Check side more, which under the 1:3 lane ratio worsens both sides (the Pre-Check queue grows and regular passengers lose a fallback). Metrics: mean and std of total time, per-station queue time, Pre-Check share actually served in Pre-Check lanes, and station utilizations, per style per configuration.

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline 3:1, stagger 0.3 (n per style): US 4002 pax, W mean 3387 s, std 2025 s; Swiss 4592 pax, 3440 s, std 2140 s; China 3652 pax, 3083 s, std 1954 s; Slow 3472 pax, 3031 s, std 1851 s. Recommended 5:3, stagger 0.3, 4 sec officers: US 1944 s / 1235 s; Swiss 2411 s / 1709 s; China 1470 s / 1259 s; Slow 1414 s / 1068 s. Findings: (1) The recommended configuration is best for every style - no style benefits from the old 1:3 split, so lane rebalancing is a globally applicable policy, not a culture-specific one. (2) Swiss-style (collective, smooth arrivals, high line-switching) is the hardest case: smooth arrivals load the system evenly at high rate (no off-peak trough to absorb), and high jockeying overloads the Pre-Check side even at 5:3 (ID_PC utilization 0.97, Pre-Check ID wait 2674 s) - mean and std are both the worst of the four. Accommodation: a 4th Pre-Check ID officer or a 6th Pre-Check lane would be the Swiss-tailored fix; in general, style-adaptive lane-mix (more Pre-Check share where jockeying/Pre-Check enrollment is high) is the mechanism by which the system accommodates cultural differences. (3) Slow-style (synthetic) does best in absolute terms (fewer passengers) but has the highest CV (0.76) of the recommended config: individually paced, unplanned arrivals are the most variable, so variance-focused accommodations (arrival windows, real-time wait displays that let slow/individual travelers self-schedule) matter most for them. (4) Chinese-style (spiky arrivals, moderate jockeying) sits between: the spike raises peak queue, but moderate jockeying keeps the Pre-Check side from saturating. (5) US-style is the reference middle. Bias note: styles are caricatures of the task's framing, not measured distributions; the absolute numbers per style should be read as a direction-and-magnitude sensitivity, and the robust claim is the ordering (Swiss worst, Slow most variable, recommended config best for all), which is stable across the two configurations and across seeds.

## Subtask 4: Task (d): propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model - globally applicable o

### Problem

Task (d): propose policy and procedural recommendations for security managers based on the model - globally applicable or tailored by culture/traveler type - plus model validation, strengths, weaknesses, and future work.

### Analysis

Recommendations are derived directly from the measured effects in tasks (a)-(c): each recommendation names the mechanism it exploits and its measured effect. Tailoring follows from the sensitivity analysis (task c): global policies apply everywhere; culture-specific policies apply where a style parameter moves the binding constraint. Validation summary (restated from task a): utilization sanity (rho < 1 off-peak), flow conservation, dataset moment matching for the two measured service-time distributions, and the jockeying-induced Pre-Check overload as a behavioral check. Strengths: (1) the model reproduces the two qualitatively observed facts - the ID/Pre-Check side is the worst queue, and extra ID capacity alone relocates rather than removes the line - both independently confirmed by expert consultation; (2) all empirical inputs are either dataset-measured or expert-calibrated with explicit intervals; (3) modifications are single-parameter, so effects are cleanly attributable. Weaknesses: (1) single-day, single-airport calibration; no seasonality or multi-bank structure beyond the one morning bank; (2) 2-h horizon censors late arrivals, biasing variance estimates upward; (3) jockeying modeled as a population switch, not physical queue-switching with re-queueing cost; (4) constant body-scan time omits scanner variability; (5) the 58-row dataset cannot validate peak-hour demand, so demand rests on expert judgment with the stated intervals; (6) kiosk enrollment and stagger adoption are planning assumptions, not observed behaviors. Future work: (1) calibrate demand against a full-day multi-hour log (the dataset is a 10-minute slice); (2) add a second daily bank (evening) and a multi-bank superposition to study queue interactions; (3) model physical jockeying and reneging with a flight-deadline constraint (passengers abandon when expected wait + flight risk exceeds a threshold); (4) optimize lane-mix and stagger fractions jointly against a cost function (officer cost + passenger delay cost) instead of sweeping; (5) add a queue-length-triggered lane-opening policy (the real-time sensing that exchange 7 found missing) and compare it against rostered staffing; (6) extend to multiple checkpoints feeding a single secondary area, and to Pre-Check enrollment dynamics (the $85/5-yr price as a demand-side lever).

### Modeling Process

Recommendation set with model evidence (all US-style baseline = 4491/2064 s mean/std total time unless noted): R1 (global, highest leverage) Rebalance the lane mix toward Pre-Check: move from 1 Pre-Check lane per 3 regular toward at least 1:1 during peaks (modeled 3:3-5:3). Effect: mean -27% to -30% with 5:3 alone; removes the worst queue (Pre-Check ID wait 2719 s -> 186-538 s depending on stage). Justification: 45% of passengers are Pre-Check but get 25% of lanes; the 1:3 ratio is the single largest structural inefficiency in the current process. R2 (global) Staff the whole chain, not just ID check: whenever lanes are added, add secondary-screening officers in proportion (modeled: 3 -> 4 secondary officers at 5+3 lanes recovers ~300 s and cuts SEC utilization 0.96 -> 0.61). Justification: the bottleneck relocates downstream when upstream capacity rises. R3 (global) Flatten the arrival peak: distribute 25-35% of bank passengers out of the 90-min bank via airline-coordinated arrival windows, off-peak incentives, or staggered check-in deadlines (modeled stagger 0.3: mean -55% when combined with R1, p95 7532 s -> 3906 s). This is the single largest variance reducer and directly targets the 'unexplained long lines' complaint. R4 (global, cheap) Kiosk-assisted ID verification in front of regular ID stations: bounded gain at baseline (~0%) but a further ~10-15% mean reduction once the chain has slack; also reduces ID-service CV. Deploy only together with R1. R5 (culture-tailored) Style-adaptive lane mix: in markets with high line-switching / collective-arrival behavior (Swiss-type), raise the Pre-Check share beyond 1:1 (modeled Swiss case is worst at 5:3: 2411/1709 s; an extra Pre-Check ID station or lane is the fix); in markets with individually-optimized, spiky arrivals (Chinese-type), prioritize R3 (arrival windows) because the peak, not the mix, binds; for slower/individually-paced travelers (synthetic), prioritize real-time wait displays + self-scheduling because their variance (CV 0.76) is the highest. R6 (global, procedural) Add real-time queue sensing: trigger lane opening at a queue-length/wait threshold (modeled proxy: R3's stagger effect shows the value of any mechanism that shifts load out of the bank; the sensing policy is its reactive twin) and maintain a bounded float pool of certified officers for peak-top-up, since rostered-only staffing is what lets the bank queue build. R7 (global, safety-neutral) Keep secondary screening off the main belt: a dedicated secondary area with >=2 officers (modeled) and a fast re-scan sub-channel for simple flags cuts the heavy tail (secondary wait 468 s -> 7-31 s with 4 officers). Validation and improvement plan as stated in the analysis field.

### Outcome Analysis

What the recommendations mean for the TSA's two goals: throughput - R1+R2 raise sustainable peak throughput from ~2250 pax/h (saturated) to a level where the 90-min bank clears within ~30-40 min after it ends (modeled: post-bank queue drain completes by ~1.5 h vs never draining at baseline); variance - R3 is the primary variance lever (std 2064 -> 1235 s, -40%; p95 -48%), R1 the secondary one, and R5 the tailoring layer that keeps variance low in non-US markets. Safety is untouched by every recommendation: all changes alter capacity, timing and information, not the screening standard (same stations, same secondary trigger rate, same Pre-Check background-check requirement). Residual risk / bias: R3 depends on airline/passenger cooperation that the model assumes at 30%; if adoption is 10%, the modeled gain scales roughly linearly (each 10% of stagger shifts ~1700 s of bank load into the off-peak window, where utilization is 0.3-0.5); R5's culture-specific lane mixes should be re-optimized per market with local enrollment and jockeying data, since the style parameters are proxies. Overall limitation statement: the model is a single-airport, single-morning-bank approximation; its quantitative effects (percentages) should be treated as order-of-magnitude planning figures, while its qualitative structure - which step binds, which lever is strongest, and where the bottleneck relocates - is robust to the stated calibration intervals.
