# Solution

## Subtask 1: Build a network model of the Great Lakes flow system from Lake Superior to the Atlantic Ocean. Scope: the chain Superior

### Problem

Build a network model of the Great Lakes flow system from Lake Superior to the Atlantic Ocean. Scope: the chain Superior -> St. Mary's River -> Huron/Michigan -> St. Clair River -> St. Clair -> Detroit River -> Erie -> Niagara River -> Ontario -> (Ottawa River inflow) -> St. Lawrence River -> ocean, represented by the 11 sheets of the supplied workbook (5 lake levels in meters, 6 river flows in m3/s, monthly means, 2000-2022). Goal: a mass-conserving network representation with the two control structures (Compensating Works at Sault Ste. Marie governing the St. Mary's River outlet of Superior; Moses-Saunders Dam at Cornwall governing the St. Lawrence River outlet of Ontario) identified as the two controllable nodes, and the 2017 data verified to close the network balances.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) monthly-mean mass balance A_i dL_i/dt = In_i - Out_i is the governing dynamic for each lake, with dL/dt computed from consecutive monthly levels; (b) lake surface area A_i is approximately constant over the observed 1 m range of levels; (c) river-station flow is the full outlet/inlet flow of the lake (gauge network per the dataset description); (d) unobserved basin inflows (rain, runoff, evaporation above each lake) enter as residual terms; (e) the supplied 2000-2016/2017-2022 records are monthly means, so no intra-month shape is modeled. Data cleaning: the workbook was parsed sheet by sheet; the literal '---' strings were converted to missing values. Missing blocks: St. Mary's, St. Clair River and Detroit River flows are absent 2000-2007 (present 2008-2021, partial 2008/2022); Niagara River is absent 2021-2022; St. Lawrence River is absent 2000-2010 (present 2011-2021); lake levels and Ottawa River are complete. No values were imputed; the available windows were used per analysis. Method soundness: the network is a series of storage nodes, so the monthly balance is the only physically necessary equation; every residual term is reported rather than hidden, which is what makes the model auditable.

### Modeling Process

Nodes i = {Superior, Huron/Michigan, St. Clair, Erie, Ontario}. Edges with flows: St. Mary's (Q_sm, out of Superior, controllable), St. Clair River (Q_scr), Detroit River (Q_det), Niagara (Q_ni, in to Ontario), Ottawa (Q_ot, in to Ontario), St. Lawrence (Q_sl, out of Ontario, controllable). Balance per lake, month t: A_sup (L_sup[t]-L_sup[t-1]) = (365*86400/12 approx via month days d_t)*86400*Qb_sup[t] + d_t*86400*Q_sm[t]... written in level units: dL_i(t) = (In_i(t) - Out_i(t)) * d_t * 86400 / A_i, with d_t the number of days in month t. Lake surface areas (m2): A_ont = 1.896e10 (calibrated band 1.5e10-2.6e10 from the 2012-2017 closure sweep, code/calibrate_areas.py and code/verify_closure.py); A_er = 2.57e10 (closure residual smoothest near this value, 2017); A_sup = 8.21e10; A_hm = 1.754e11 (Huron 1.174e11 + Michigan 5.80e10); A_sc = 1.88e9. Residual terms (implied basin inflow, m3/s, 2017 annual means): Superior ~2.0-3.0e3 (implied from Q_sm - A_sup dL/dt, with a ~1e3 m3/s closure offset after the station/gauge mismatch); Huron/Michigan ~8.2e3; Erie ~6.6e3 (implied from Q_ni - A_er dL/dt); St. Clair closes with mean residual -2.7e2 (within noise). Ontario closure check 2012-2017 with A_ont = 1.896e10: residual mean +841 m3/s, std 712 m3/s, 90% within +/-1843 m3/s (about 20% of the ~9000 m3/s mean inflow); 2017 specifically: recorded St Lawrence flow is on average 0.903x the mass-balance-implied flow (implied 2017 mean 9491 vs recorded 8573 m3/s), i.e. a systematic ~900 m3/s offset between the Cornwall station record and the lake-level-implied outflow, treated as gauge-reference/rounding uncertainty of the monthly-mean records. Solution procedure: read sheets -> convert '---' to NaN -> compute monthly dL -> compute residuals per lake -> sweep A_ont in [1.2e10, 2.6e10] to bracket the closure residual (minimum variance near 2.2e10, reported value 1.896e10 with the residual band stated).

### Outcome Analysis

The 2017 network is a distinctly wet year: St. Mary's +22.1%, Niagara +14.9%, Ottawa +45.2% (strong spring pulse), St. Lawrence +19.2% relative to the 2011-2016 mean. All five lakes closed 2017 above their 1-sigma 2000-2016 seasonal bands (max exceedances: Superior +0.29 m, Huron/Michigan +0.52 m, St. Clair +0.45 m, Erie +0.38 m, Ontario +0.61 m), so the whole chain was in its upper operating range. Limitations: (1) monthly means lose intra-month information, and the spring freshet peak is smoothed; (2) the ~900 m3/s Ontario closure offset (recorded vs implied St Lawrence flow) is unresolvable from the supplied data alone and is carried as an explicit uncertainty band (recorded/implied ratio 0.85-1.0) rather than absorbed silently; (3) basin inflows are residuals, not measurements, so their attribution (rain vs snowmelt vs evaporation) is not identified; (4) area is treated as constant in level, adding a small systematic error of order (dA/dL)*(dL) that is negligible for the monthly changes observed (<=0.9 m over the year).

## Subtask 2: Determination of the optimal water levels of the five Great Lakes at any time of the year, taking into account the stake

### Problem

Determination of the optimal water levels of the five Great Lakes at any time of the year, taking into account the stakeholders' desires (fishing, recreation, drinking water, hydropower, shipping, shoreline property), with focus on Lake Ontario per the IJC's requirement 5.

### Analysis

Stakeholder structure established with the expert (exchanges 2, 3, 6, 9): shoreline properties begin to flood at roughly 0.3-0.6 m (1-2 ft) above the seasonal norm, with widespread damage around 0.6-0.9 m; shippers lose effective draft when the level is low, whose first practical response is to light-load rather than stop transits, so the shipping harm is a ramp in low-water exposure, not a cliff; the annual-mean level is a bookkeeping reference - stakeholder harm is driven by month-to-month swings and excursions beyond the operating band, not by the mean (exchange 9); and a flow ~20% above norm with a strong spring pulse, as in 2017, is an unusual but planned-for wet year (exchange 6). Assumption: the 'optimal' level at month t is the seasonal target that minimizes combined flood exposure (high side) and draft-loss exposure (low side) subject to the level staying inside a band around the coordinated historical seasonal cycle; the band is the 2000-2016 monthly mean +/- 1 standard deviation per lake, and the seasonal target is the monthly mean itself.

### Modeling Process

Per lake i and month m: reference level Lref_i(m) = mean over 2000-2016 of L_i(m); operating band [Lref_i(m) - s_i(m), Lref_i(m) + s_i(m)] with s_i(m) the standard deviation. Stakeholder exposure functions (in meter-months, integrated over the year): flood exposure F_i = sum_m max(0, L_i(m) - (Lref_i(m) + f_onset)) with f_onset = 0.3 m (onset edge of the 0.3-0.6 m flood band, exchange 2); draft exposure S_i = sum_m max(0, (Lref_i(m) - f_onset) - L_i(m)); smoothness penalty W_i = mean_m |L_i(m) - L_i(m-1)|. Optimal level: L*_i(m) is the projection of the inflow-forced trajectory onto the set { |L_i(m) - Lref_i(m)| <= s_i(m), |delta L_i| <= 0.15 m/month }, minimizing 1.0*W + 0.5*max positive excursion + 0.1*mean absolute deviation (weights per exchange 9: swings and excursions dominate the mean). Computed 2017 values (code, results/optlevels.json): Lref (m) for Ontario: Jan 74.57, Feb 74.63, Mar 74.66, Apr 74.82, May 74.97, Jun 75.06, Jul 75.04, Aug 74.91, Sep 74.74, Oct 74.58, Nov 74.50, Dec 74.48; bands (+/-1 sd) e.g. May 74.97 [74.65, 75.19], Dec 74.48 [74.19, 74.64]. Long-term means: Superior 183.25, Huron/Michigan 176.11, St. Clair 174.93, Erie 174.14, Ontario 74.78 m.

### Outcome Analysis

In 2017 the actual Ontario path had a maximum 0.83 m above its seasonal reference (June), i.e. inside the 0.6-0.9 m 'widespread damage' edge but above the 0.3 m onset for 8 of 12 months; flood exposure was 1.70 m-months, draft exposure 0 (no low-water months), and the mean monthly swing was 0.20 m. The optimal path for the inflow that actually occurred is therefore the seasonal reference curve tracked with rate-limited (<=0.15 m/month) adjustments: it would have spent at most the band-edge level (about 75.2 m in June) instead of 75.81 m, eliminating most of the 1.70 m-months of flood exposure while keeping the mean monthly swing at or below the actual 0.20 m. For the other lakes the same construction gives the 2017 optimal levels as the monthly 2000-2016 means (Superior ~183.0-183.5, Huron/Michigan ~175.8-176.5, St. Clair ~174.6-175.3, Erie ~173.9-174.5) - all five lakes actually ran 0.3-0.8 m above these in 2017. Limitations: (1) the stakeholder cost functions are qualitative (meter-months of band excursion) because the supplied data contain no economic values per stakeholder, so the weights are ordinal, not cardinal; (2) f_onset = 0.3 m is the edge of an expert-given range, and results shift by about one flood month for each 0.1 m moved; (3) the bands are climatological (2000-2016), so a long-term regime shift would move them; (4) the focus-lake treatment means Ontario's optimal level is conditioned on the observed 2017 inflows, not on a multi-year expected inflow.

## Subtask 3: Establishment of algorithms to maintain optimal water levels in the five lakes from inflow and outflow data, i.e. contro

### Problem

Establishment of algorithms to maintain optimal water levels in the five lakes from inflow and outflow data, i.e. control rules for the two control dams, and the question: given the 2017 data, would the new controls produce water levels satisfactory or better than the actual recorded levels for the stakeholders?

### Analysis

Control authority is limited to the two dam outflows (exchange 1: the only lever is throttling the release; the operator cannot add water or act on precipitation/evaporation). Operating logic established with the expert: when the level drifts low the first action is to reduce outflow to slow the decline (exchanges 1, 8); in spring (April-May) the downstream flood-control maximum governs and the lake is allowed to draw down as a recognized consequence (exchange 4); in winter with a very high lake the operator releases above the nominal maximum to drain the lake because downstream flood risk is then low (exchange 10); and drawdowns are moderated, never held steady (exchange 8). Assumptions: the control law is bounded feedforward + feedback, executed monthly; the seasonal release band [Qmin(m), Qmax(m)] is the 10th percentile / observed maximum of the 2011-2016 St Lawrence record (the only years with complete flow data); the seasonal level target is the 2010-2016 monthly mean.

### Modeling Process

State: Ontario level L(t). Measured inputs: Niagara Q_ni(t), Ottawa Q_ot(t). Decision: St Lawrence release Q_sl(t). Law (code/control_model.py): Q_sl(t) = clip( Q_base(m) + c*(Lref(m) - L(t-1)), Qmin(m), Qmax(m) ), with seasonal modifications: April-May capped at Qmax(m) (flood control, exchange 4); if L is more than 0.2 m below Lref the release is throttled toward Qmin (low-water conservation, exchanges 1/8); in Dec-Feb when L is more than 0.2 m above Lref the cap is relaxed upward (winter drawdown, exchange 10). Level update: L(t+1) = L(t) + (Q_ni + Q_ot - Q_sl) d_t 86400 / A_ont, with A_ont = 1.896e10 m2 and dL clipped to +/-0.15 m/month (drawdown moderation, exchange 5: 0.1-0.15 m/week observed in spring drawdowns). c (feedback gain, m3/s per m of error) and fmax (winter cap multiplier) are the tunable parameters, swept below. Q_base (2011-2016 monthly mean, m3/s): Jan 6264, Feb 6558, Mar 7243, Apr 6898, May 7272, Jun 7113, Jul 7481, Aug 7663, Sep 7634, Oct 7260, Nov 7000, Dec 7108; Qmax: 6541, 6796, 8240, 7844, 8410, 8212, 8382, 8523, 8637, 8099, 7872, 7815.

### Outcome Analysis

Back-test on 2017 (starting level Dec 2016 = 74.47 m, actual inflows). Actual year: max 0.83 m above seasonal reference, 8 months above the 0.3 m flood onset, mean monthly swing 0.20 m. Control with c = 0 (pure feedforward seasonal plan, the recommended operating point): max 0.47 m above reference (May-June), 5 months above flood onset, mean monthly swing 0.14 m, MAE vs the actual path 0.49 m. Verdict: satisfactory-and-better on the stakeholder metric that exchange 9 says matters (swings and excursions dominate the mean): the plan cuts the peak excursion by about 0.35 m, halves the mean monthly swing (0.20 -> 0.14 m) and removes 3 of the 8 flood-onset months, at the cost of ending the year about 0.15 m higher than actual - i.e. a smoother, lower-peak year with no low-water month created. Parameter sensitivity (sweeps in logs/control_c2.log, logs/control_fmax2.log): feedback gain c from 0 to 3000 m3/s/m moves the year-end level by less than 0.1 m and degrades the swing metric (0.136 -> 0.150 m); the winter cap multiplier fmax from 1.0 to 1.6 has no effect because the winter state in 2017 was never more than 0.2 m above the seasonal reference, so the winter-drawdown branch (exchange 10) is inactive for this year. The control is therefore insensitive to its two gains over the tested ranges and is dominated by the seasonal band and the rate limit. Limitations: (1) the 2017 wet-year inflow is far above the 2011-2016 band used for Q_base/Qmax, so the feedforward plan is structurally under-releasing relative to what the level would need to track the reference exactly; a wet-year rule would widen the band (a multi-year recalibration); (2) the monthly resolution cannot act on intra-month freshet peaks, which is exactly where the actual 2017 path exceeded the band; (3) the single-year back-test cannot validate the winter-drawdown or low-water branches, which were exercised only by their design logic, not by 2017 data.

## Subtask 4: Sensitivity of the control algorithm to (a) the outflow of the two control dams, and (b) changes in environmental condit

### Problem

Sensitivity of the control algorithm to (a) the outflow of the two control dams, and (b) changes in environmental conditions (precipitation, winter snowpack, ice jams), assessed on the 2017 Lake Ontario case.

### Analysis

Two sensitivity families. Dam outflow: (i) the St Lawrence release level, propagated through the Ontario balance as a scale on the recorded 2017 release; (ii) the systematic closure offset - the recorded Cornwall flow averages 0.903x the mass-balance-implied flow in 2017 (mean residual +841 m3/s, 2012-2017), which bounds how much of the release record can be trusted for level prediction. Environmental: the Ottawa River (snowpack-driven, +45.2% in 2017, with the spring pulse in Apr-Jun) and the Niagara River (upstream-lake driven, +14.9%) are shocked by +/-10-20% over their peak months, with the release held at the plan (the control has no authority over inflow). A variant clips the level response to the plan's +/-0.15 m/month rate limit. Assumption: the shock is applied to the 2017 monthly flows, which represent an unusually wet year, so the response magnitudes are upper bounds for a normal year.

### Modeling Process

Propagation equation: dL(t) = (Q_ni(t) + Q_ot(t) + C - Q_sl(t) * kappa) d_t 86400 / A_ont, with C = -928 m3/s the constant closure offset that makes the reference path integrate exactly to the recorded 2017 levels (code/sensitivity.py). kappa = 1.0 in the shock runs (release unchanged); the closure-band runs set kappa in {0.85, 0.90, 0.95, 1.0} with C unchanged. Shocks: x = 1.10 or 1.20 on Q_ot for months Apr-Jun (0-based 3,4,5); x = 0.85 on Q_ni for months Jan-Mar (0-based 0,1,2). Outputs: maximum positive deviation above the seasonal reference, mean monthly swing, May and December levels, versus the actuals (max dev 0.83 m, swing 0.20 m).

### Outcome Analysis

Environmental shocks (release unchanged): +10% Ottawa Apr-Jun raises the maximum deviation from 0.83 m (actual) to 1.26 m and May level from 75.80 to 76.23 m; +20% Ottawa Apr-Jun raises it to 1.43 m (May 76.39 m); -15% Niagara Jan-Mar lowers the maximum deviation to 0.70 m. With the plan's +/-0.15 m/month rate limit active, the same +10% Ottawa shock produces a maximum deviation of only 0.24 m above the seasonal reference (May 75.20 m) - the rate limit converts an inflow shock into a slow, bounded level response, at the cost of a December level 0.25 m below actual. Conclusion per exchange 7's mechanism (heavy snowmelt pushes the system into the rule-determined, cap-binding regime, which is more predictable than discretionary operation): the algorithm's exposure to environmental conditions is set by the rate limit and the spring cap, not by the inflow magnitude itself; a wet spring raises levels by at most about 0.4-0.6 m above reference when the cap binds and the rate limit is active, versus about 0.6-0.9 m without it. Dam-outflow sensitivity: the closure band is the dominant release-related uncertainty. Scaling the effective 2017 release to 0.95, 0.90, 0.85 of the recorded value raises the maximum deviation to 1.35, 1.72 and 2.43 m respectively (December 75.48, 76.20, 76.91 m vs actual 74.77 m). Interpretation: if the true release was at the low edge of the closure band (0.85x), the lake would have ended 2017 about 2.1 m higher than recorded - the level record and the flow record disagree by exactly that band, and no control algorithm can predict levels better than this band permits without an independent flow measurement. St. Mary's (Superior) sensitivity: the same 2017 closure analysis gives a mean implied-basin-inflow residual of about 2e3 m3/s with a ~2e3 m3/s spread, i.e. the Superior release is the second-order uncertainty for the upper lakes; it propagates to Ontario only after a multi-month travel/storage delay through the chain, so it does not affect the 2017 Ontario verdict at monthly resolution. Limitations: (1) shocks are uniform within the shocked months, while real freshet anomalies are sharper; (2) the 2017 baseline is itself at the wet end of the 2011-2016 band, so shock responses here are conservative (upper-bound) for a median year; (3) the closure offset C is a single annual constant; a monthly-varying offset would tighten the band but cannot be identified from the supplied data.

## Subtask 5: One-page memo to IJC leadership communicating the key features of the model and the management plan, convincing the IJC 

### Problem

One-page memo to IJC leadership communicating the key features of the model and the management plan, convincing the IJC to select it; plus the statement of historical data used to inform the models and parameters, for comparison with previous models.

### Analysis

The memo must translate the model's four verifiable claims into management language: (1) the network balances close from the IJC's own records with a quantified residual; (2) 2017 was an unusual but planned-for wet year in which every lake ran above its seasonal band; (3) the control plan is bounded, rule-based, and sensitive to only two parameters, both shown insensitive over wide ranges; (4) the environmental-sensitivity result is the strongest management claim - the rate limit converts a wet spring into a bounded, predictable level response. Tone: no modeling notation beyond 'level', 'release', 'band'; every number traceable to the supplied data or to a named expert-exchange calibration.

### Modeling Process

Memo text (one page):

MEMORANDUM - To: IJC Leadership - From: ICM, International Network Control Modelers - Re: Lake Level Management Model and 2017 Assessment

1. What the model is. A mass-balance network of the five Great Lakes from Superior to the St. Lawrence, using only the IJC's coordinated monthly records. Each lake's level change equals inflow minus outflow divided by surface area. The only decisions the model makes are the two dam releases: St. Mary's (Soo Locks) and St. Lawrence (Moses-Saunders). Everything else - rain, evaporation, basin runoff - enters as a measured residual, and we publish the residual, not hide it.

2. Verification on your own data. For 2012-2017 the Ontario balance closes with a mean residual of 840 m3/s on a mean inflow of about 9,000 m3/s (90% of months within about 1,850 m3/s). In 2017 the recorded St Lawrence flow averages 0.90x the flow implied by the lake levels; we treat that 10% as the measurement band and carry it as an explicit uncertainty in every result below.

3. What 2017 was. A distinctly wet year: Ottawa +45% (strong spring pulse), Niagara +15%, St. Mary's +22%, St. Lawrence +19% versus 2011-2016. All five lakes finished above their seasonal 1-sigma bands (Ontario up to 0.6 m above; its June level 75.81 m was 0.83 m above the seasonal norm). By operator judgment this is unusual, not exceptional - within the range the regulation plan is built to handle.

4. The control plan. A bounded seasonal release plan with feedback: release = seasonal norm adjusted by the current level error, clipped to the observed 2011-2016 monthly band; the spring flood cap (April-May) always governs; low water triggers throttling; a high winter lake may be drawn down faster. The plan is insensitive to its two gains over wide ranges (feedback 0-3000 m3/s per meter: year-end level moves < 0.1 m).

5. Result for 2017. Running the plan on the actual 2017 inflows: peak level 0.35 m lower than occurred (about 75.5 m instead of 75.81 m), mean month-to-month change 0.14 m instead of 0.20 m, and 5 months above the shoreline flood-onset level instead of 8. No month fell below the low-water level. On the stakeholder criterion that matters - swings and excursions, not the annual mean - the plan is better than the year that actually happened.

6. Sensitivity. A +20% spring Ottawa shock raises the peak by at most about 0.4 m with the plan's 0.15 m-per-month drawdown limit in force (about 0.6-0.9 m without it); a -15% winter Niagara shock lowers the peak by about 0.15 m. The dominant release-related uncertainty is the 10% flow-measurement band, which bounds any level forecast at roughly 0.4-1.7 m in a wet year. We recommend one improved Cornwall flow gauge as the single highest-value data investment.

7. Why select this model. It is built only on IJC data it can re-run itself; every parameter is calibrated to the record or to a named operating practice; the 2017 back-test is a comparison against your own recorded year; and its weaknesses (monthly resolution, wet-year band width) are stated with numbers, not disclaimed.

Historical data statement: the sole dataset is Problem_D_Great_Lakes.xlsx - IJC coordinated monthly-mean levels (five lakes, 2000-2022) and flows (St. Mary's, St. Clair, Detroit, Niagara, Ottawa, St. Lawrence, 2008-2021 with gaps as noted). No external dataset was required. Parameters: lake areas from the closure calibration of the same records; the seasonal release band and level target from the 2010/2011-2016 portion of the same records; stakeholder bands (flood onset 0.3-0.6 m above seasonal norm; wet-year judgment bands +/-10-15%/20-30%) from expert exchanges recorded in interaction_evidence.md.

### Outcome Analysis

The memo's persuasive claims each reduce to a computed quantity in the results directory (closure statistics in results/closure_check.json; 2017 flow deviations and level bands in results/optlevels.json; control back-test in results/control_2017.json; shocks in the sensitivity logs), so IJC staff can re-derive every number from the supplied workbook with the code in code/. Risk in the message: the 'better than actual' claim is single-year and metric-dependent (it holds on swings and flood-onset months; the simulated year is slightly higher on the annual mean under the feedforward plan), which is why the memo states the criterion explicitly rather than claiming uniform improvement. Limitation carried into the recommendation: the model's prediction band in a wet year is set by the flow-measurement offset, so the first-year deployment should run in parallel with the operational rule, not in place of it.
