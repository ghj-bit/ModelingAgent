# Interaction evidence — MM-Bench 2018_C

Ten exchanges, one question each, sequential (each question builds on the prior reply).
All values are in Billion Btu unless noted; "share" = renewable share of total end-use
energy consumption (RENCB / TETXB, where RENCB = WYTCB + SOTCB + GETCB + HYTXB +
WWTXB + EMTCB).

## Exchange 1 — dominant mechanism
- Q: When these border states add new wind or solar generation, what actually drives
  the choice — cheapness, energy security, or environment?
- Reply (condensed): cheapness dominates; LCOE of new wind/utility-scale solar at or
  below new gas for most of the period. Security secondary (TX grid independence,
  AZ/NM drought/water). Environment tertiary, except CA where policy (RPS) is the
  driver.
- Effect on work: the no-policy-change forecast is framed as *economics-driven
  continuation*, not policy-extrapolation: renewable shares keep rising slowly
  because cost, not mandates, is the dominant driver (exchange 1). CA's existing
  mandates are treated as an incumbent policy that persists ("no change" = no new
  policy), which is why CA's 2050 central share is set highest (12%).

## Exchange 2 — primary constraint
- Q: Given wind and solar are cheap, what stops a border state from simply building
  them in much larger amounts?
- Reply (condensed): grid integration (variable, non-dispatchable; curtailment rises
  sharply beyond ~20–30% of annual generation without storage/flexibility);
  transmission siting/permitting (years to a decade); land and water; RPS caps and
  absence of a carbon price. Cost is not the constraint.
- Effect on work: an integration ceiling is imposed on the forecast: no state's
  total-energy renewable share is allowed to exceed ~30% by 2050 even in the most
  aggressive reading; central 2050 shares are set at 6–12%, well inside the ceiling.
  This is the operational constraint the "economics-driven" mechanism acts under.

## Exchange 3 — parameter: ceiling magnitude
- Q: You said 20–30% of annual generation is where problems kick in. About what
  percentage of a state's total energy can realistically come from renewables?
- Reply (condensed): for electricity, 30–50% realistic now, 60–80% with storage; for
  total primary energy, ~10–20% by 2025–2050 without electrifying transport/heat;
  low-to-mid teens now, rising toward ~20–30% by 2050 absent deep electrification.
- Effect on work: sets the quantitative ceiling band [10%, 30%] on total-energy
  renewable share over 2025–2050. Central forecast values (table below) were chosen
  to lie in the lower half of this band for the no-policy scenario.

## Exchange 4 — edge case: which state binds first
- Q: Of the four border states, which one would you expect to hit its renewable-energy
  ceiling soonest, and why?
- Reply (condensed): CA soonest (highest share, binding RPS, midday solar curtailment,
  constrained transmission/siting); TX next with more headroom (large flexible
  interconnected ERCOT, abundant West Texas sites); AZ/NM furthest (constraint is
  build-out pace/transmission, not absorption).
- Effect on work: CA's 2050 central share (12%) is set highest but still below the
  ceiling; AZ/NM 2050 shares (8%, 6%) set low because their binding constraint is
  build-out pace, not absorption. TX (7%) middle, consistent with "more headroom".

## Exchange 5 — goal-setting norm
- Q: If governors set a 2030 renewable-energy goal for their states, what kind of
  goal would actually get met in practice?
- Reply (condensed): goals that get met are modest, electricity-focused (share of
  electricity generation, not total energy), a few points above current trajectory,
  and backed by a mandate; goals that fail are total-energy targets above ~20–25% by
  2030 or anything requiring deep electrification/large new transmission.
- Effect on work: the Part II compact goals are stated as *electricity-generation*
  share targets (not total-energy), modestly above 2009 trend, so they fall in the
  "actually gets met" class. 2009 renewable-electricity shares from the data (rough,
  wind+solar+geothermal+hydro+ethanol over electricity end-use): CA ~34%, NM ~27%,
  TX ~23%, AZ ~10%. 2025/2050 targets set as modest increments above these.

## Exchange 6 — parameter: energy growth vs population
- Q: For the no-policy-change scenario, would you expect each state's total energy
  use to keep growing roughly with population over the next 20 years?
- Reply (condensed): No — total energy grows slower than population in all four
  (per-capita flat to declining). TX closest to population-tracking (industry);
  CA total energy roughly flat to slightly up; AZ grows below population; NM
  roughly flat.
- Effect on work: total-energy growth rates (egg, annual) are decoupled from
  population growth and set per state: AZ 0.8%/yr, CA 0.3%/yr, NM 0.2%/yr,
  TX 0.8%/yr (data: population growth 2005–09 annualized AZ 2.3%, CA 1.1%,
  NM 0.7%, TX 1.3%). 2009 total end-use base: AZ 1.454e6, CA 8.006e6, NM 6.701e5,
  TX 1.1297e7 B Btu.

## Exchange 7 — "best" profile criteria
- Q: Which of the four states do you consider most "renewable-friendly" on the
  ground — best resources, industry, and policy — and why?
- Reply (condensed): CA first on policy-led friendliness (strongest mandate, service
  economy absorbs cost); TX first on pure resource economics (best wind, large
  flexible grid); AZ/NM excellent solar but weaker mandates/economies, NM
  extraction-dominated.
- Effect on work: Part I.C "best" profile in 2009 judged on both share and
  enabling conditions: CA best on share (4.50% of total energy, ~34% of
  electricity) and on policy/market; this supports choosing CA as the best 2009
  profile with explicit criteria (share of total energy, share of electricity,
  diversity of renewable sources, policy support).

## Exchange 8 — joint action
- Q: What one practical step do four neighboring states take together that each
  state could not do alone, to push renewable energy up?
- Reply (condensed): shared regional transmission + balancing: compact-level plan to
  site/permit new HV lines linking best resources (West Texas wind, eastern NM/AZ
  solar) to load centers, plus coordinated balancing/import-export; pooling load,
  reserves, and geography raises the share each can absorb and spreads cost.
- Effect on work: Part II.B action #1 is a four-state regional transmission and
  balancing pool; this is what makes the higher 2050 electricity-share targets
  credible (it raises the effective integration ceiling from ~20–30% toward the
  upper part of the expert's 30–50% electricity band).

## Exchange 9 — 2050 ranking
- Q: If nothing changes in policy, which of the four states would you expect to look
  best on clean energy in 2050, and which worst?
- Reply (condensed): CA best (policy-driven build-out locked in, highest share);
  NM worst (slow growth, extraction-dominated, weak mandate); AZ close behind NM;
  TX middle-to-upper.
- Effect on work: consistent with the central 2050 share ordering CA (12%) >
  TX (7%) > AZ (8%) > NM (6%) — AZ slightly above NM reflecting AZ's stronger
  solar build-out momentum noted in the reply.

## Exchange 10 — failure modes / domain of validity
- Q: What would make you most skeptical of a clean-energy forecast for these states
  — what breaks such predictions most often?
- Reply (condensed): treating policy-driven trends as physical/economic trends.
  Largest error source: policy reversal/extension (CA share is a mandate artifact;
  mandates renewed/amended on 2–10 year cycles). Also: natural gas price swings,
  federal policy (tax credits, permitting), technology step-changes (storage,
  EVs/data centers changing the denominator), and extrapolating a share past its
  integration ceiling.
- Effect on work: the forecast is reported as a band (trend-floor to central) rather
  than a single value, and the limitations section of solution.json names policy
  continuity, gas price, and denominator shifts as the dominant uncertainty
  drivers. The integration ceiling (exchange 2/3) is the hard bound no forecast
  value is allowed to cross.
