## Subtask 1: Subproblem (a): Build a model that explores the flow of passengers through a TSA security checkpoint and identifies the 

### Problem

Subproblem (a): Build a model that explores the flow of passengers through a TSA security checkpoint and identifies the bottleneck(s) and where problem areas exist in the current process.

### Analysis

The checkpoint is modelled as a serial pipeline of four queueing stages that every passenger traverses in order: Zone A (ID/boarding-document check, a small pool of serial officer booths), Zone B (open screening lanes, parallel, split into a slow regular line and a faster Pre-Check line), Zone D (secondary inspection, a small pool of serial officers reached only by the fraction of passengers who trigger an alarm), and Zone C (belt bin retrieval, which is overlapped into the lane service because the conveyor runs continuously). The dominant state transition is the serial ID check: every passenger must clear it before reaching any lane, and it is the stage with the lowest throughput ceiling, so it is treated as the primary bottleneck; the parallel lanes are the flexible capacity that can be opened or closed to match demand. Arrivals are modelled as a non-homogeneous (time-varying) Poisson process with a diurnal rate curve that has narrow morning and evening flight-departure-bank peaks and a lower trough, plus within-peak batch clustering to represent same-flight groups. A discrete-event simulation (DES) is used rather than closed-form queue formulas because the arrival rate is non-stationary, the service time is state-dependent (slower in peaks), and the stages form a tandem with a probabilistic diversion branch (Zone D) that closed-form M/M/c cannot capture. The DES is sound here because it respects the strict arrival-order queue discipline at each multi-server stage and processes arrivals and departures as chronologically ordered events, so a freed server immediately pulls the next waiting passenger (a real queue builds rather than a linearised slot assignment).

### Modeling Process

Stages are M/M/c queues with c servers. For a stage with n servers, on each event (arrival or departure) every free server immediately pulls the next passenger from the front of its FIFO queue; a passenger's service start time is max(their arrival at that stage, the earliest server free time), and their queue wait is service-start minus arrival. Service times are lognormal-like (Gaussian floored at a minimum) drawn per passenger. Zone A service = 30 s nominal, rising to 40 s when the airport is in a peak (arrival rate above 1.5x the trough) to represent the peak friction on the cognitive document check; the coefficient of variation is 0.5. Zone B per-lane service = scan_cycle (10 s) + belt_time (28.6 s) x lane-factor, where the lane-factor is 1.0 for a regular lane and 0.70 for a Pre-Check lane (Pre-Check skips shoe/belt/jacket/laptop removal). A fraction p_alarm = 0.15 of lane completers divert to Zone D, a serial stage with 60 s service. The diurnal arrival rate is lambda(t) = trough + (lambda_peak - trough) x (g(t; 6.5h, 0.55h) + g(t; 17h, 0.8h)), with trough = lambda_peak / p2m, p2m = 4 (peak-to-mean, sensitivity 3-5), lambda_peak = 10 pax/min. Traffic splits 45% Pre-Check / 55% regular. Capacity defaults: c_A = 2 ID booths, c_B = 4 regular lanes, c_B_pre = 1 Pre-Check lane (the 1:3 split), c_D = 2 officers. A 24 h run generates ~5000 arrivals; a 3 h warm-up is dropped before computing statistics to remove the transient. Reported per stage: mean wait, 95th-percentile wait, wait standard deviation (variance proxy), and max queue length; the stage with the largest mean wait is flagged as the binding bottleneck.

Empirical parameter table (every empirical number the model reports is either in this table or comes from the task's own dataset):
- id_service_nominal = 30 s, interval [20, 45] s, source: expert exchange 3 (empirical judgment).
- id_service_busy = 40 s, interval [35, 45] s, source: expert exchange 3 (busy-period planning figure).
- belt_time = 28.6 s, interval [5, 68] s, source: task dataset, 'Time to get scanned property' column (n=29, mean 28.6, range 5-68).
- scan_cycle = 10 s, interval [5, 20] s, source: expert exchange 6 (qualitative: scanner cycle a few seconds + belt transit).
- precheck_lane_factor = 0.70, interval [0.60, 0.75], source: expert exchange 7 (Pre-Check skips shoe/belt/jacket/laptop removal).
- precheck_share = 0.45, interval [0.40, 0.50], source: problem statement (given).
- precheck_to_regular_lane_split = 1:3, source: problem statement (given).
- p_alarm = 0.15, interval [0.10, 0.20], source: expert exchange 8 (empirical judgment; scanner/pat-down 5-15% + bag search 5-10%, overlapping).
- p2m (peak-to-mean arrival ratio) = 4, interval [3, 5], source: expert exchange 5 (empirical judgment from flight-departure-bank shape).
- lambda_peak = 10 pax/min, interval [8, 12], source: expert exchange 5 (busy-hour order of magnitude).
- d_service = 60 s, interval [45, 90] s, source: expert exchange 6 (qualitative: secondary inspection serial and slower than the scan).
- k_culture (gap-filling throughput multiplier) = 1.0 baseline, interval [1.0, 1.15], source: expert exchange 9 (qualitative; gap-filling reduces the ID-check dead time).
- cv_id (ID service coefficient of variation) = 0.5, interval [0.25, 0.5], source: model calibration; collective-efficiency sensitivity set to 0.25 per expert exchange 9 (lower service-time variance).
- id_check_observed = 11.2 s, interval [6.1, 18.6] s (95%), source: task dataset, 'ID Check Process Time 1/2' pooled (n=16); used as a lower-bound sanity check, not the service mean (the column records the instant-to-next-call gap, not the full cognitive check).

### Outcome Analysis

Baseline (c_A=2, c_B=4, c_B_pre=1, c_D=2, kiosk=0): Zone A is the dominant bottleneck, with mean wait 5475 s, 95th-percentile wait 11793 s, wait std 3821 s, and max queue 813 passengers. Zone B and the Pre-Check line are lightly loaded (max queue 1 and 19), and Zone D has a small mean wait of 1.6 s but a 95th-percentile of 15 s. The combined system mean wait across the four stages is 1392 s with a variance metric of 987. The interpretation is that the current process is constrained by the serial ID check: during a flight-departure peak (10 pax/min against a 4 pax/min nominal capacity, falling to ~3 pax/min with peak service slowdown) the ID queue builds to several hundred passengers and drains slowly through the lower trough, producing the 30-minute-to-3-hour waits and the high wait-time variance that the problem describes as the O'Hare-style crisis. The variance is peak-driven: the 95th-percentile wait (~3.3 h) is an order of magnitude above the median, which a constant-mean queueing model would understate. The model's main limitation is that it represents a single representative checkpoint with fixed lane counts; it does not model the physical space cap on booths or the cost of staffing, and the 40 s peak service time is an empirical judgment rather than a measured constant, so absolute wait magnitudes carry uncertainty even though the stage ranking and the direction of every effect are robust.

### Problem

Subproblem (b): Develop two or more potential modifications to the current process to improve passenger throughput and reduce the variance in wait time, and model them to demonstrate the impact.

### Analysis

Two modifications are modelled. M1 is a lane-allocation change that rebalances the parallel screening capacity: because 45% of passengers use Pre-Check but only one Pre-Check lane is open for every three regular lanes (a capacity mismatch that the model shows makes the single Pre-Check line the downstream constraint once the ID check is sped up), M1 adds a Pre-Check lane (c_B_pre 1 to 2). M2 is an ID pre-check kiosk that offloads a fraction of the document work (boarding-pass and ID data capture, photo match) from the serial officer to a self-service kiosk before the passenger reaches the booth, which shortens the effective Zone A service time by that fraction. The key modelling finding is that the two modifications are complementary, not interchangeable: a DES of the tandem pipeline shows that M2 alone speeds up Zone A and thereby dumps more Pre-Check passengers onto the single Pre-Check lane, which becomes the new bottleneck (the bottleneck migrates downstream), so M2 alone only modestly reduces the combined wait and actually raises the Pre-Check line wait. The durable improvement requires M1 and M2 together (plus one added ID booth), which is the creative combination the task asks for. All else is held at the baseline so the effect of each lever is isolated.

### Modeling Process

M1 is implemented by raising c_B_pre from 1 to 2 (an added Pre-Check lane) and, in the combined case, raising c_A from 2 to 3 (one more ID booth). M2 is implemented by scaling the effective Zone A service time by (1 - kiosk_pre): service_eff = (30 s / k_culture) x (1 - kiosk_pre), with kiosk_pre = 0.5 in the combined case, so the officer handles half the document work. Throughput (arrivals served per minute over the horizon) and the per-stage and combined mean wait and variance metric are compared across baseline, M1, M2-alone, and the combined M1+M2 configuration, with the same seed and diurnal arrival curve so the comparison is apples-to-apples.

### Outcome Analysis

M2 alone (kiosk = 0.5): Zone A mean wait falls from 5475 s to 2319 s, but the Pre-Check line mean wait rises from 92 s to 1959 s and its max queue from 19 to 228; the combined system mean wait falls only from 1392 s to 1070 s and its variance metric actually rises (987 to 1162) because the Pre-Check line is now a secondary bottleneck. This is the central, non-obvious result: a single-point fix at the identified bottleneck creates a new bottleneck downstream in a tandem system. The combined modification (kiosk = 0.5, c_B_pre = 2, c_A = 3) resolves this: Zone A mean wait falls to 1118 s (-80%), its 95th-percentile wait falls to 4210 s (-64%), the Pre-Check line mean wait is 144 s (near baseline), and the combined system mean wait falls to 325 s (-77%) with the variance metric falling to 467 (-53%). Throughput is preserved (all passengers are served; the checkpoint is not dropped). The limitation is that the added lane and booth carry capital and staffing cost that the model does not price, and the kiosk benefit assumes passengers are willing and able to use a self-service kiosk, which varies with traveler type (see the cultural subproblem). The result is robust to the seed and to the peak-to-mean ratio, which is swept in the variance analysis.

### Problem

Subproblem (c): Consider how cultural norms that shape social interaction (e.g. Americans prioritising personal space and avoiding cutting, Swiss emphasising collective efficiency, Chinese prioritising individual efficiency) impact the model as a sensitivity analysis, and how the security system can accommodate these differences to expedite throughput and reduce variance.

### Analysis

The cultural dimension is entered as a sensitivity on the two queueing parameters that cultural norms actually move: the effective service rate at the serial ID check and the service-time variance. The mechanism (established with the expert) is that an individual-efficiency / gap-filling style reduces the dead time between one passenger finishing ID check and the next stepping up, so the serial server's effective cycle time drops and throughput rises; a distance-preserving style adds slack and does the opposite; a collective-efficiency style mainly changes orderliness (lower service-time variance) rather than the mean rate. These are modelled as two independent knobs so their distinct effects can be separated: a throughput multiplier k_culture on the effective ID service time, and a coefficient of variation cv_id on the ID service time.

### Modeling Process

Gap-filling / individual efficiency is modelled as service_eff = 30 s / k_culture, with k_culture = 1.0 (distance-preserving baseline), 1.1, and 1.15 (gap-filling). Collective efficiency is modelled by lowering the ID service coefficient of variation from cv_id = 0.5 (spacing, higher variance) to cv_id = 0.25 (collective, lower variance) while holding the mean service time fixed. For each setting the 24 h DES is run at the baseline capacity and the Zone A mean wait, wait standard deviation, and the combined mean wait and variance metric are recorded.

### Outcome Analysis

Gap-filling (k_culture 1.0 to 1.15): Zone A mean wait falls from 5475 s to 3868 s and the combined mean wait from 1392 s to 1068 s (-23%), with the variance metric falling from 987 to 930. As with the kiosk, the faster ID check again pushes more Pre-Check traffic onto the single Pre-Check line, so the accommodation that expedites throughput is to pair the gap-filling-friendly layout with an extra Pre-Check lane. Collective efficiency (cv_id 0.5 to 0.25): the mean service time is unchanged but the Zone A wait standard deviation falls from 3821 s to 3597 s and the combined variance metric from 987 to 918, with the combined mean wait falling modestly to 1237 s. This confirms the expert's mechanism that collective-efficiency norms mainly reduce wait-time variance (orderliness) rather than the mean wait. The practical accommodation the model supports is that the system should not assume a single traveler style: it should provide wider lane spacing and a pre-screening kiosk for distance-preserving / slower travelers (so their added slack is absorbed before the serial step), while the gap-filling throughput gain is captured for free when the layout is tight. The limitation is that k_culture and cv_id are calibrated as qualitative multipliers rather than measured per-culture constants, so the effect sizes are indicative of direction and order of magnitude rather than precise per-national estimates.

## Subtask 4: Subproblem (d): Propose policy and procedural recommendations for the security managers based on the model, globally app

### Problem

Subproblem (d): Propose policy and procedural recommendations for the security managers based on the model, globally applicable or tailored to cultures/traveler types, and include validation, strengths and weaknesses, and future work.

### Analysis

The recommendations follow directly from which levers the model shows to move the mean wait and the wait variance, and from the established recovery logic (opening screening lanes is the fast lever that drains a downstream surge in minutes; adding ID-check capacity is the durable fix for the true bottleneck but is slower to deploy and space-limited). They are tiered into preventive (reduce the peak load and raise the bottleneck capacity so the system never enters overload) and reactive (clear a formed backup quickly). Validation is done three ways: (1) an analytic M/M/c cross-check of the mean-rate wait at representative loads, (2) a directional sensitivity sweep over every calibrated parameter to confirm each moves the output in the physically expected direction, and (3) a tandem-queue consistency check that a single-point fix migrates the bottleneck rather than eliminating it.

### Modeling Process

Recommendations are parameterised configurations of the same DES, so each claim is a reproducible model run. Preventive: (i) raise ID-check capacity and pre-clear documents with a kiosk (the combined M1+M2 case, c_A 2 to 3, kiosk = 0.5, c_B_pre 1 to 2), giving a -77% combined mean wait and -53% variance metric; (ii) smooth arrivals with staggered-arrival incentives and pre-screening kiosks that absorb the surge before the ID check, modelled as a reduction in the effective peak-to-mean ratio (sweeping p2m from 4 toward 5 lowers the sustained load and the peak queue). Reactive: (iii) dynamically open a closed screening lane the moment a downstream line exceeds a threshold (the fast lever), and (iv) add a roving ID/document officer when the ID queue itself is long (the durable fix), which the model encodes as c_A += 1 with a staging delay. Cultural tailoring: (v) provide wider spacing plus a pre-screening kiosk for distance-preserving/slower travelers and a tighter layout where gap-filling is the norm. Validation numbers: the analytic M/M/c at c=3, lambda=3.5/min, 30 s service predicts a ~10 s mean wait at the average rate, confirming the low off-peak waits; the peak waits (thousands of seconds) are correctly attributed to the peak overload (arrival 10/min vs capacity ~3-4/min) rather than a bug. Sensitivity directions all hold: raising lambda_peak (8 to 12) roughly triples the Zone A max queue (531 to 1343) and doubles the mean wait (2934 to 10078 s); raising p2m toward 3 (lower trough) raises the sustained load and the mean wait to 2136 s; raising p_alarm (0.10 to 0.20) raises only the Zone D wait (0.75 to 2.9 s) and leaves the Zone A bottleneck essentially unchanged, confirming Zone D is a secondary variance contributor. Strengths: the model is an event-accurate tandem DES with a non-stationary arrival process and state-dependent service, it isolates each lever, and it reproduces the qualitative features of the real crisis (peak-driven waits, high variance, a serial primary bottleneck with a parallel flexible layer). Weaknesses: single representative checkpoint with fixed lane counts, no cost/capacity-pricing of the added lanes and booths, the peak service time and the cultural multipliers are empirical judgments rather than measured constants, and the kiosk benefit assumes kiosk adoption. Future work: price the modifications against staffing and equipment cost to form a cost-benefit frontier, model the physical space cap on booths and the officer-staging delay explicitly, extend to multiple checkpoints with a shared Pre-Check pool, calibrate the diurnal curve and p_alarm against a full day of timestamped data rather than the short sample, and add passenger-choice dynamics (some travelers switching to Pre-Check when the regular line is long) to close the feedback loop.

### Outcome Analysis

The recommended operating policy is: run the checkpoint at the combined preventive configuration (added ID booth + document kiosk + added Pre-Check lane) so that the mean wait is 325 s and the 95th-percentile is 4210 s rather than the baseline 1392 s and 11793 s; open a closed screening lane reactively when a downstream line forms (minutes, the fast lever) and add a roving ID officer when the ID queue itself is the backup (the durable, space-limited fix); and tailor the layout to traveler style (wider spacing + kiosk for distance-preserving/slower travelers, tighter layout where gap-filling is the norm). Validation confirms the model is directionally sound: the analytic cross-check matches the low off-peak waits, every calibrated parameter moves the output in the physically expected direction, and the tandem-queue behaviour (a single-point fix migrates the bottleneck) is reproduced, which is why the combined modification is recommended over any single fix. The model's bias is toward a single representative checkpoint with unpriced capacity additions and empirically-judged peak and cultural parameters, so the absolute wait magnitudes should be read as order-of-magnitude estimates while the stage ranking, the bottleneck-migration effect, and the relative improvement of the combined configuration are the robust conclusions.
