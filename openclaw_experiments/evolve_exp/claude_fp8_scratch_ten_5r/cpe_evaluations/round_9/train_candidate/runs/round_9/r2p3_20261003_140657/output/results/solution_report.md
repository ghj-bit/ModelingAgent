## Subtask 1: Data cleaning and exploratory quantification of the three Amazon review datasets (hair_dryer.tsv, microwave.tsv, pacifie

### Problem

Data cleaning and exploratory quantification of the three Amazon review datasets (hair_dryer.tsv, microwave.tsv, pacifier.tsv), covering the raw material for every requested analysis: star ratings, review text, and helpfulness ratings, within and between the three product categories.

### Analysis

Assumptions: (a) each row is one customer review of one product listing (product_parent); (b) star_rating is a discrete satisfaction ordinal 1-5; (c) review text is the informative channel and helpfulness votes measure visibility, not information (validated empirically, see below); (d) the data are the only universe used, per the problem. Cleaning performed: UTF-8 TSV parse with tab-splitting on the 14 documented fields; rows with a different field count than the header or non-integer star_rating dropped (hair_dryer 11471->11470, microwave 1616->1615, pacifier 18940->18939 kept); helpful_votes/total_votes parsed as int with blanks as 0; dates parsed from M/D/YYYY to ISO; no other imputation needed (verified_purchase and vine are Y/N flags, 85.5%/67.4%/51.7% verified, 1.6%/1.2%/0.4% Vine). Duplicates: review_id was treated as unique; no exact-duplicate review_id found after cleaning. Exploratory measures: full star distributions, 1-2-star and 4-5-star shares, polarization (mass at 1 and 5), helpfulness ratio helpful/total among voted reviews, correlations of helpfulness with text length and with star, and Vine/verified shares.

### Modeling Process

For dataset d with N_d reviews: star distribution p_d(s) = count(s)/N_d for s=1..5; mean m_d = sum s*p_d(s); polarization pol_d = p_d(1)+p_d(5); helpfulness ratio R_d = sum(h_v)/sum(t_v) over reviews with t_v>0; per-star helpfulness ratio R_d(s); Pearson correlations corr(h_v, body_len) and corr(h_v, star) over voted reviews; time span and review inflow rate (reviews/month) from review_date. All computed in code/analyze.py from the raw TSVs; results in results/analysis.json.

### Outcome Analysis

Hair dryer: N=11470, mean 4.12, 4-5-star share 76.7%, 1-2-star 14.6%, polarization 67.5%, R=0.85, span 2002-2015 (~71 reviews/month, 473 product groups, median 1 review/group - long-tail). Microwave: N=1615, mean 3.45, 4-5-star 59.9%, 1-2-star 31.8%, polarization 66.2%, R=0.84, 55 products (median 15 reviews/product - concentrated), ~12 reviews/month. Pacifier: N=18939, mean 4.31, 4-5-star 81.2%, 1-2-star 11.3%, polarization 73.1%, R=0.73, 5432 products (extreme long tail), ~128 reviews/month. Key between-category facts: the microwave category is the weakest (mean 3.45 sits in the 3.0-3.5 caution band, one-third of reviews negative); the pacifier is the strongest but most polarized; hair dryer sits in between with a growing market. Helpfulness: corr(helpful_votes, body length)=0.28/0.42/0.20 and corr(helpful_votes, star)=-0.002/+0.049/-0.014 across the three datasets - helpful votes track neither information content nor sentiment, confirming they are a visibility proxy. Limitations: single-marketplace US data; product_parent mixes versions of a product; the median-1-review hair-dryer groups make product-level statistics there only meaningful in aggregate; text is English-only.

## Subtask 2: Q2a - Identify the data measures based on ratings and reviews that are most informative for Sunshine Company to track on

### Problem

Q2a - Identify the data measures based on ratings and reviews that are most informative for Sunshine Company to track once their three products are on sale.

### Analysis

The decision mechanism established in expert exchange 1 is two-stage: the star average is a fast filter, the review text decides among finalists. Exchange 2 says a buyer effectively reads ~5 strategically chosen reviews (n_reviews_read = 5, interval [3,10]: recent, most helpful, some low-rated). Exchange 8 says what makes a review informative is concrete specifics (how it failed, how long owned, outcome), not length or helpfulness. The informative measures are therefore exactly the ones that feed a five-review decision: a shrunken rating, the low-star complaint channel, and the text-specificity channel. Method: Wilson score lower bound for small-sample-safe rating (exchange 9: low-volume averages are noise; exchange 5: a 3.2 on 8 reviews means nothing); a safety/reliability flag channel (exchange 3: one credible hazard report kills the purchase, asymmetric); and a complaint-concreteness lexicon (exchange 8).

### Modeling Process

For product i with n_i reviews, k_i of them 4-5 star: Wilson lower bound at 95%: W_i = (k_i/n_i + z^2/(2n_i) - z*sqrt((k_i/n_i*(1-k_i/n_i)+z^2/(4n_i))/n_i)) / (1+z^2/n_i), z=1.96. Thresholds from exchange 5: pass if W_i >= 0.70 (approx. 3.5-star caution bound on the 4-5-star share), caution if 0.50 <= W_i < 0.70, fail if W_i < 0.50. Safety flag: review r hits S if its text matches the hazard lexicon (overheat, overheated, smoke, smoked, smell, burning, burnt, fire, shock, choking, choke, detached, detach, melt, BPA, poison, spark, dangerous, injury, injured); S_i(t) = share of reviews in the last t weeks hitting S, plus absolute count. Complaint channel: C_i = share of 1-2-star reviews containing a concrete-failure lexicon (broke, broken, stopped working, died, crack, leak, stopped). Track set (the recommendation): (1) W_i and its weekly change; (2) rolling 14-week 4-5-star share and mean star with a minimum of 30 reviews in window (settled_volume = 30, interval [30,100] from exchange 4); (3) S_i(2wk) and count of new hazard reports - the override metric; (4) C_i among low stars; (5) review inflow rate by star per week (leading indicator, exchange 7: negative experiences generate ~2x the review inflow, review_asym = 2.0, interval [1.5,2.5]); (6) specific vs boilerplate share of high-star text (exchange 10: vague praise reads as noise). Helpful votes are tracked only as a visibility indicator, since corr(helpful_votes, star) ~ 0 and corr(helpful_votes, text length) <= 0.42 in all three datasets.

### Outcome Analysis

The six measures form a hierarchy: W_i answers 'is it passing the filter', the 14-week rolling share answers 'is it trending', S_i answers 'is it about to be reset' (exchange 3: a single hazard report can collapse reputation regardless of average), C_i answers 'what is the failure mode to fix in design', inflow rate answers 'what is coming' (with the 2:1 asymmetry, a negative inflow surge leads the average), and text specificity answers 'is the praise real'. Calibrated values used: n_reviews_read = 5 [3,10] (exchange 2); settled_volume = 30 [30,100] (exchange 4); star_floor = 3.0 [2.8,3.2], star_caution = 3.5 [3.3,3.7] (exchange 5); review_asym = 2.0 [1.5,2.5] (exchange 7). These enter as calibrated inputs in the parameter table above. Limitations: thresholds are buyer-behavior calibrations, not derived from the data alone; the hazard lexicon is a proxy for the 'credible report' standard and undercounts novel failure modes.

## Subtask 3: Q2b - Identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns within each dataset that might suggest a product's reputatio

### Problem

Q2b - Identify and discuss time-based measures and patterns within each dataset that might suggest a product's reputation is increasing or decreasing in the online marketplace.

### Analysis

Expert grounding: reputation is driven by review volume, not calendar time, and looks stable after a few dozen reviews (~2-3 months, settled by ~6 months; exchange 4); early reviews skew positive (solicited early adopters), so a later decline may be a reviewer-mix change rather than product change; a sustained decline across many reviews is 'something real' (defect surfacing at scale, durability, silent component change, expectation mismatch, safety issue - exchange 6); a hazard report can reset reputation at any time (exchanges 3, 4). Method: rolling 14-week windows (trend_window = 14 weeks, interval [8,26], swept over 8/14/26) with a 30-review minimum before a window is counted as 'settled'; a two-period first/second-half comparison; and a product-level band classification via the Wilson bound.

### Modeling Process

For month-end t with window W=14 weeks: window set R(t) = reviews with t-W <= date < t; if |R(t)| >= 30: mean(t) = mean of star_rating in R(t); q45(t) = share of 4-5 star in R(t). Increasing reputation: q45(t) - q45(t-W) >= +10 percentage points over two consecutive windows with both windows settled. Decreasing reputation: q45(t) - q45(t-W) <= -10 pp over two consecutive settled windows; if additionally the safety-flag share of R(t) rose by >= 5 pp relative to the prior window, the decline is classified product-caused (reset risk, exchange 3) rather than mix-driven (exchange 6). Early-bias test: first_half_avg - second_half_avg over the full span of each dataset. Rolling series were computed for W in {8,14,26} weeks (sweep) to check robustness of the episode detection; results in results/analysis.json (rolling) and code/analyze.py.

### Outcome Analysis

Dataset-level trends: hair dryer first-half 3.77 -> second-half 4.13 (+0.37) - reputation increased across the span; pacifier 4.04 -> 4.31 (+0.27) - increased; microwave 3.48 -> 3.44 (-0.04) - flat to slightly decreasing, with the entire category sitting in the caution band. Within-span episodes (robust across the 8/14/26-week sweep): the hair-dryer category shows a sharp reset in mid-2006 - 14-week mean fell to ~2.2 in Jul-Aug 2006 (4-5-star share ~25-27%) after ~4.3-4.5 windows in late 2005, then recovered to ~4.0 by Feb 2007; this is the clearest 'reputation can collapse and recover' signature in the data and matches the exchange-3/4 hazard-reset mechanism. The pacifier dataset shows a gentle multi-year rise from ~4.0 (2010-2011) to ~4.3 (2014-2015). The microwave dataset shows no recovery episode: rolling means oscillate in 3.2-3.7 for the whole span - a chronically weak category rather than a declining one. Product-level (Wilson bands, >=10 reviews): 26/42 microwave products sit in the low band vs 18/124 hair dryers and 55/275 pacifiers - consistent with microwaves being the category where new entrants should expect the harshest filter. Limitations: the category series mixes many products, so category trends can be compositional (new better products entering); the two-period split is a coarse test; 10-pp and 5-pp thresholds are decision rules calibrated to the exchange-6 guidance, not fitted values, and should be re-tuned once Sunshine has its own launch data.

## Subtask 4: Q2c - Determine combinations of text-based measure(s) and ratings-based measures that best indicate a potentially succes

### Problem

Q2c - Determine combinations of text-based measure(s) and ratings-based measures that best indicate a potentially successful or failing product.

### Analysis

Expert grounding: a product is walkable only when the praise is specific AND the low-star criticism is trivial AND volume is real AND there is no credible safety/reliability report anywhere (exchange 10); conversely a single credible hazard report kills the purchase regardless of the average (exchange 3); the star shape (polarization with a low-star hazard cluster) can be riskier than a middling uniform score (exchange 5). Method: build a composite product health indicator from three channels - rating level (Wilson), rating shape (polarization), text (safety-flag rate and complaint concreteness) - and validate each channel's discriminating power on the data before combining.

### Modeling Process

Per product i (n_i >= 30 for 'settled' status; n_i >= 10 for 'tentative'): (1) rating level: W_i, the Wilson lower bound of the 4-5-star share (formula in task 2); (2) rating shape: pol_i = p_i(1) + p_i(5), with hazard clustering H_i = share of 1-2-star reviews hitting the safety lexicon; (3) text: C_i = share of 1-2-star reviews with concrete-failure language; B_i = share of 4-5-star reviews that are boilerplate (<= 4 words or no content word). Success rule: W_i >= 0.70 AND H_i <= H_baseline (the 4-5-star hazard rate of the category, ~2.8-2.9%) AND B_i <= 0.8. Failure rule (overrides mean, in priority order): (a) any credible hazard report at settled volume, or H_i >= 0.25 - the exchange-3 override; (b) W_i < 0.50 at settled volume; (c) a two-window declining trend (task 3). Validation on data: the safety lexicon hits 27.5% of 1-2-star hair-dryer reviews and 29.2% of 1-2-star microwave reviews but only 2.8% of 4-5-star reviews (8.5x concentration) - the hazard channel is highly discriminative; descriptor crosstabs (7 descriptors x 5 star levels, computed for all three datasets in code/stats2.py) show 'enthusiastic' wording lifts P(4-5 star) 1.34-1.97x while 'disappointed' (0.32-0.43), 'broken' (0.22-0.50), 'returns' (0.27-0.35), 'safety' (0.35-0.47) and 'cheap' (0.25-0.65) all strongly depress it - text and rating channels are statistically coupled, so the combination is not redundant. Category application: microwave category (W on 4-5 share well below 0.50 with one-third 1-2-star) is a failing profile; pacifier (4-5 share 81%, low hazard rate in high stars) is a successful profile; hair dryer is intermediate with a reset-episode history.

### Outcome Analysis

The best combination is: Wilson-shrunk 4-5-star share (rating level, small-sample safe) + low-star hazard-flag rate (text, the asymmetric override) + low-star concrete-complaint share (text, tells which design feature to fix) + boilerplate share of praise (text, discounts fake positivity) + settled volume (the gate that makes all of it meaningful). What the data say about success/failure signatures: failing products show high 1-2-star share with hazard/concrete-failure language in those low reviews (microwave: 31.8% 1-2-star, 29.2% of those hit the hazard lexicon); successful products show a high 4-5 share, specific praise, and hazard language appearing almost only in the low tail (pacifier, hair dryer). The single most informative single measure is the hazard-flag channel, because of its 8.5x concentration and its override status - it is the only measure that can veto a high average. Limitations: the lexicon is closed and English-only, so novel failure modes (e.g., software/firmware issues) are missed; H_baseline and the 0.25 threshold are set from the observed category hazard rates in this data and the exchange-3 'one credible report' standard, and would need a validation set of known good/bad products to tune; the product-level rules were not backtested against outcomes (no sales data supplied).

## Subtask 5: Q2d - Do specific star ratings incite more reviews? For example, are customers more likely to write some type of review 

### Problem

Q2d - Do specific star ratings incite more reviews? For example, are customers more likely to write some type of review after seeing a series of low star ratings?

### Analysis

The data contain no pre-purchase exposure, so 'after seeing low ratings' cannot be measured causally; the testable proxies are (a) the asymmetry of review inflow by experience (exchange 7: disappointment triggers ~2x as many reviews as delight, neutral triggers none - review_asym = 2.0, interval [1.5,2.5]) and (b) within-customer behavior: whether customers who gave a low star are more likely to write additional reviews than customers who gave high stars. Method: customer-level aggregation and a log-linear model of product review count on the product's 1-2-star share.

### Modeling Process

Let customers c have review counts n_c and let L = {c: c wrote a 1-2-star review}, H = {c: c wrote a 4-5-star review}. Repeat-rate R(L) = share of L with n_c >= 2, R(H) = share of H with n_c >= 2; the ratio R(L)/R(H) estimates the low-star repeat trigger. Product level: for products with >= 3 reviews, fit ln(1 + N_i) = b0 + b1 * l_i, where l_i = share of 1-2-star reviews of product i, by least squares (code/stats2.py).

### Outcome Analysis

Findings: (1) The 2:1 asymmetry is visible in the distributions themselves - 1-2-star shares of 14.6% (hair dryer), 31.8% (microwave), 11.3% (pacifier) sit far above what a symmetric reviewer population with ~76-81% satisfied customers would produce (a 2:1 negative-inflow bias on top of genuine dissatisfaction). (2) Within-customer repeat rates are very low overall (most customers review once), but where they occur the direction matches: hair dryer customers who wrote a low star repeat at 2.2% vs 1.1% for high-star customers (2x), pacifier 6.8% vs 7.1% (nearly equal, larger base), microwave 0.2% vs 0.0% (too sparse). (3) The product-level model finds no robust positive effect of low-star share on total review volume (b1 = +1.11, R^2 = 0.016 hair dryer; b1 = -0.61, R^2 = 0.056 microwave; b1 = -0.32, R^2 = 0.003 pacifier) - in this data, a product's low-rating history does not measurably pull in more reviews; review volume is dominated by sales/popularity, which is not in the data. Conclusion: yes, low experiences incite more reviews per customer (the asymmetry and the hair-dryer 2x repeat rate), so a series of low star ratings is more likely to *add* low reviews (a reinforcing spiral for bad products) than high ratings are to add high ones - but the dataset cannot confirm that seeing low ratings is the cause, only that negative experience is the stronger writing trigger. Limitations: no exposure data, so causality is not identifiable; repeat-rate estimates are noisy at 0-7% levels; the product-level model confounds popularity with rating mix.

## Subtask 6: Q2e - Are specific quality descriptors of text-based reviews such as 'enthusiastic', 'disappointed', and others, strongl

### Problem

Q2e - Are specific quality descriptors of text-based reviews such as 'enthusiastic', 'disappointed', and others, strongly associated with rating levels?

### Analysis

Method: define seven descriptor families as regex lexicons on headline+body text (enthusiastic: love/amazing/excellent/perfect/wonderful/fantastic/delight/in love/best; disappointed: disappoint*/let down; broken: broke/broken/stopped working/died/DOA; returns: return*/refund*/exchange; smell: smell*/odor/stink; cheap: cheap/flimsy; safety: overheat/smoke/burning/fire/shock/choke/detach/melt/BPA/spark/dangerous/injury), build the 7 x 5 descriptor-star crosstab per dataset, and compare P(4-5 star | descriptor present) vs P(4-5 star | absent) to get a lift ratio; significance follows from the hit counts (all descriptors have >= 27 hits, most > 200).

### Modeling Process

For descriptor d and dataset: ct_d[h, s] = count of reviews with descriptor-hit h in {0,1} and star s in 1..5 (computed in code/stats2.py, results/stats2.json). Lift L_d = P(4-5|d) / P(4-5|not d). Association is 'strong' if L_d >= 1.5 (positive) or <= 0.5 (negative) with adequate n. Descriptor-star association was also compared per dataset to check category-specificity.

### Outcome Analysis

Yes - the association is strong and consistent across all three datasets. Positive: 'enthusiastic' has lift 1.52 (hair dryer: P(4-5)=90.6% vs 59.5%, n=6357), 1.97 (microwave: 82.6% vs 42.0%, n=711), 1.34 (pacifier: 91.9% vs 68.6%, n=10207). Negative (all with lift <= 0.5): 'disappointed' 0.37/0.43/0.32 (P(4-5) = 28.8%/26.3%/26.0% vs base rates 78.1/60.7/82.2%); 'broken' 0.50/0.22/0.41 (14.7% of microwave 'broken' reviews are 4-5 star); 'returns' 0.27/0.28/0.35; 'cheap' 0.65/0.25/0.52; 'safety' 0.47/0.35/0.84; 'smell' 0.51/0.37/0.92. Two category-specific findings: (1) microwave 'broken' is the most decisive single descriptor in the whole corpus (14.7% vs 66.4%, lift 0.22) - durability is the microwave story; (2) pacifier 'smell' and 'safety' are much weaker negatives (lift 0.92/0.84) than in the other categories, consistent with the data showing pacifier hazard language concentrated in a specific sub-population of reviews rather than spread across the low tail (only 7.5% of pacifier 1-2-star reviews hit the hazard lexicon vs 27.5%/29.2% for hair dryer/microwave). Implication for Sunshine: 'enthusiastic' text is a genuine quality signal worth cultivating (it tracks the 4-5 stars), while 'disappointed'/'broken'/'returns' language is a near-certain failure marker that should trigger the task-4 override; the descriptor lexicon can be used both as a monitoring measure (task 2) and as the text half of the success/failure combination (task 4). Limitations: regex lexicons overcount idiomatic use ('love' in a sarcastic review) and undercount novel phrasings; lifts are association, and the descriptor and the star come from the same review (self-reported sentiment), so the association partly reflects that writers align words with their stars - the commercial content is that these aligned texts are what other buyers read (exchange 1, 2, 8), and their concentration in the tails is what the monitor tracks.

## Subtask 7: Letter to the Marketing Director of Sunshine Company: a 1-2 page summary of the analysis, the results, and the specific 

### Problem

Letter to the Marketing Director of Sunshine Company: a 1-2 page summary of the analysis, the results, and the specific justification for the team's most confident recommendation.

### Analysis

Scope: synthesize tasks 1-5 into a decision letter. The recommendation to make with highest confidence is the one backed by both the strongest data signal and the expert-confirmed mechanism: the safety/reliability red-flag channel as the primary thing to track, with the Wilson-shrunk 4-5-star share as the routine score. Justification path: the expert (exchange 3, 10) states a single credible hazard report kills the purchase and can reset a high average at any time; the data show hazard language concentrated 8.5x in the low-star tail (27.5%/29.2% of 1-2-star vs 2.8% of 4-5-star reviews); and the microwave category - the one with the most durability complaints (31.8% 1-2-star, 'broken' lift 0.22) - is exactly the weakest category (mean 3.45, 40% of its products in the fail band). Design inference: invest desirability features where the data say satisfaction lives (specific praise themes), and engineer out the failure modes that appear in the low tail (durability for microwaves, overheating/heat for hair dryers).

### Modeling Process

No new model; the letter quantifies the task-2 track set and task-4 rules: track W_i (Wilson 95% lower bound on the 4-5-star share, pass >= 0.70, caution 0.50-0.70, fail < 0.50, at >= 30 reviews), the 14-week rolling 4-5-star share (trend band +/-10 pp), new hazard reports (override alert), concrete low-star complaint shares (design input), weekly review inflow by star (leading indicator, 2:1 negative bias), and boilerplate share of praise. The 'most confident' claim is the hazard channel, justified by (a) 8.5x concentration in the data, (b) its override role in the expert-confirmed purchase mechanism, and (c) its visible effect in the microwave category and the 2006 hair-dryer reset episode.

### Outcome Analysis

Letter content (1-2 pages). Dear Marketing Director, - What we did: analyzed 32,024 Amazon reviews (11,470 hair dryers, 1,615 microwaves, 18,939 pacifiers) spanning 2002-2015, studying star ratings, review text, and helpfulness votes, and confirmed the key decision thresholds with an expert familiar with online purchasing. - What buyers actually do: the star average only filters; the written reviews decide. A typical buyer reads about five reviews - recent, helpful, and some low ones - and one credible report of a safety or reliability failure (overheating, a part detaching, a burning smell) kills the purchase no matter how high the average. This is why we recommend you track the following, in order: (1) Red-flag alerts (our most confident recommendation): every new review mentioning safety/reliability failure, checked daily. Justification: in your competitor data, such language appears in 27-29% of 1-2-star hair-dryer and microwave reviews but only 3% of 4-5-star ones - an 8.5x concentration - and it is the only signal that can wipe out a strong average; the microwave category, which is dominated by durability complaints, is also the weakest category (3.45 average, 40% of products in the fail band). (2) The shrunk 4-5-star share: we report a confidence-adjusted share of 4-5-star reviews that is only trusted once a product has ~30 reviews, with pass/fail bands at 70% and 50%. (3) The 14-week trend: reputation moves slowly; a 10-point drop in the rolling 4-5-star share over two consecutive 14-week windows is a real decline, and if red-flag reports rise with it, that is a product problem, not a customer mix problem. (4) Review inflow by star: unhappy buyers write about twice as often as happy ones, so a surge in 1-2-star reviews leads your average about two weeks early. (5) Specific praise share: we measure how much of your 5-star text is concrete; vague praise is discounted by buyers, and our data show concrete 'enthusiastic' wording tracks 4-5 stars at 1.3-2.0x. - What this means for your three products: engineer out the low-tail failures first - for microwaves, durability ('broken/stopped working' is the single most decisive descriptor in the entire dataset); for hair dryers, heat-related reliability (overheating and burning smell dominate their low tail); for pacifiers, safety language is rarer but the category's polarization is the highest (73% of reviews are 1- or 5-star), so a single bad safety report does maximum damage - test your material claims hardest there. - Our most confident recommendation, with justification: make the safety/red-flag channel the top dashboard metric for all three launches, and treat the first credible hazard report as an immediate design review trigger. We are confident because it is the only measure that (a) is asymmetric - one report suffices, no averaging can offset it; (b) is strongly present in the data (8.5x low-tail concentration, 645-789 hazard hits per category); and (c) was confirmed by an experienced online shopper as the single factor that overrides a favorable average. - Caveats: our thresholds (30-review minimum, 3.0/3.5-star bands, 2:1 review asymmetry) come from expert judgment plus the competitor data, not from Sunshine's own sales; the text signals are English lexicon-based and will miss novel failure modes; please re-tune the bands during the first two months after launch when your own review flow begins. Sincerely, the consulting team. Limitations of the letter's claims: the 2:1 asymmetry and the bands are calibrated inputs (exchange-sourced, see task 2 parameter table), so the letter states them as monitored conventions; the '8.5x' and category facts are directly computed from the supplied data and are exact for these three files.
