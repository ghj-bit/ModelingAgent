# Interaction Evidence

## Exchange 1
**Q:** When a tennis player seems to take over control of a match, which recent event do you most often see happen just before that shift begins?
**A:** Break of serve is the dominant trigger (unexpected break against the player holding comfortably). Preceded by: a long hard-fought game ending against the run of play; a tiebreak won after opponent led/set point; a run of 3–5 consecutive points built on unforced errors or double faults by the player in control. Break point converted/saved at game end.
**How it changed the work:** Established the dominant mechanism = momentum swings are anchored to *game-level* events, chiefly break of serve, with a short point-run (3-5 pts) on errors/DF as the immediate precursor. Therefore the momentum metric should be a decaying weighted sum of point outcomes where: (a) points are weighted by serve status (serve advantage), (b) unforced errors and double faults carry extra negative weight, (c) game-level break events get a discrete boost. This defines the metric's functional form before I calibrate weights.

## Exchange 2
**Q:** When a player has been winning points and is riding momentum, what usually has to happen first before that run actually ends and control shifts?
**A:** The run ends when the server/dominant player loses a game (a break), or loses a point on their own serve in a game they were expected to hold. Necessary first event = a lost point on the dominant player's own serve; confirming event = the break of serve that follows. Without a break (or at least a lost service game) the run usually continues. Momentum rarely reverses while the dominant player keeps holding serve.
**How it changed the work:** This is the *operational constraint* on the mechanism. It means momentum is NOT a smooth per-point decay only; it is anchored at *serve-game boundaries*. I therefore model momentum as a per-point signal that is (i) weighted so that points won/lost on one's own serve carry the base weight, (ii) given an extra penalty/credit when the serve is actually broken (game_victor != server), and (iii) allowed to persist across a held serve (decay is slow within a comfortable holding run) but is permitted to flip sharply at a broken serve. Concretely: M_t = w_serve*base point outcome + w_break*(break indicator) + w_err*(unforced-err/DF of the trailing player), with an exponential carryover whose rate is lower within a hold-run and higher after a break. This is the functional form; weights to be calibrated next.

## Exchange 3
**Q:** When one player takes a clear run, how many points long do you usually see that run last before the other starts to fight back?
**A:** Typical run = 3–5 consecutive points (one to two games). 6–8 happen but noticeably less common; double-digit runs rare/memorable. 3–4 points is common and often not yet a real shift; ~5–6 points, or crossing a break of serve, = the opponent starts to steady and the run ends.
**How it changed the work:** Calibrated the EMA decay (alpha0). A "run" of L same-sign points under an EMA with rate a accumulates to M ≈ S*(1-(1-a)^L)/... and its *memory* (how much of the run survives into the next point) is (1-a). For a 3–5 point run to be "a real shift" but a 1–2 point blip to fade fast, I want (1-a) ≈ 0.6–0.75, i.e. alpha0 ≈ 0.25–0.40. I therefore fix alpha0=0.30 as the base rate (middle of the sweep), so a 5-point run carries ~76% into the following point while a 2-point run carries only ~49%. This is the calibration the expert's "3–5 = real shift" gives me.

## Exchange 4
**Q:** When watching a match, what on-court thing makes you feel the lead is about to flip, before it actually does?
**A:** The leader's serve starts to wobble while the scoreboard still looks comfortable: (1) first serves dropping out -> repeated second serves, games pushed to 30-30/deuce instead of love/15 holds; (2) games going long (multiple deuces, break points saved), leader scrambling; (3) cheap points given away by the leader (double faults, UEs on routine balls, missed easy put-aways); (4) chaser suddenly holding easily while leader's games get longer. The felt signal = a shift in who is dictating points and how long the leader's service games take, not the score itself. When the leader's serve stops being a free hold, the break/flip is close.
**How it changed the work:** Defined the *feature set* for the swing-prediction model (Part 3). Indicators, all computable from the dataset on the *leader's most recent service game(s)*:
  f1 = leader's first-serve rate over last K points (serve_no==1 share)
  f2 = points-per-service-game (game length) for the leader, last game vs their baseline
  f3 = leader's break-point-saved / deuce count in the current service game
  f4 = leader's unforced-error + double-fault rate over last K points (cheap points)
  f5 = chaser's hold ease (consecutive games held without a break point faced)
  f6 = leader's net-point-win share / who is dictating (winning-shot share)
Target = a swing (M_t sign-flips past threshold tau) within the next H points. This is now a supervised classifier with coach-grounded features, not a generic lag model.

## Exchange 5
**Q:** Do the same flow-of-play cues that flip a men's match work equally in women's tennis, or table tennis?
**A:** Partly. The core cue = loss of serve dominance transfers, but weight/timing differ. Women's tennis: same cues apply; breaks are MORE frequent, so serve-based swings are less decisive; momentum shifts more on rally dominance and error runs; the serve signal is weaker and the model should lean more on point-level play. Table tennis: serve dominance much smaller (serve alternates every 2 points, modest serve advantage), so "break of serve" has no analogue; transferable cues = point-run length, error clusters, who dictates rallies; short games to 11 + alternating serve => swings shorter/noisier, momentum weaker and faster-decaying. General cues (runs, error clusters, who dictates) generalize; the serve-centric cue does not transfer equally.
**How it changed the work:** Defined the *domain of validity / generalizability* of the model. Implications baked into the write-up:
  (1) In the momentum metric, the break shock (g_break2) and the serve-weight (w_srv vs w_rec) are the serve-centric components; for women's matches I expect to LOWER their relative weight and RAISE the point-level error/UE and rally-dominance terms, and to use a FASTER base decay alpha0 (swings shorter/noisier).
  (2) For a table-tennis transfer, drop the break term entirely and keep run length + error clusters + dictation; use a short K and fast alpha0.
  (3) I can test this on the dataset: I have no women's matches, but I CAN test the "serve-signal strength" by computing break frequency per match and correlating it with momentum volatility, and by sweeping alpha0 to show the decay-rate sensitivity. That is the empirical anchor for the generalizability claim.

## Exchange 6
**Q:** When your player is on the losing side of a run, what do you tell them to do in the next one or two points to stop the bleeding?
**A:** Stop the run by winning the next point on their own serve or return, not by chasing the scoreboard. Serve: get the first serve in, play a safe high-percentage pattern, take pace off, big target, avoid the double fault and low-percentage winner; a free hold is the fastest way to stop the bleeding. Return: just get the ball back deep, make the opponent play one more ball, do not go for a winner on the return, force an extra shot and let the error come. Slow the tempo (full time between points, towel, reset routines) — breaking rhythm matters as much as the shot. Win one point, then one game; the goal in the next one or two points is to end the run, not to win the match back at once.
**How it changed the work:** This is the content of the coach's memo (Part 4 deliverable). It maps directly onto the model: (a) the fastest way to flip M back toward your side is a *held serve* (the w_srv term + no break shock), so "win the next point on your own serve / secure a free hold" is exactly the highest-leverage move in the metric; (b) "avoid the double fault / low-percentage winner" reduces the f4_cheap term (cheap points given away), which the prediction model identified as a swing precursor; (c) "win one point, then one game" matches the run-length structure: a run is 3-5 points, so ending it needs only 1-2 well-chosen high-percentage points, not a hero shot. This becomes the 1-2 page memo advice for coaches.

## Exchange 7
**Q:** When preparing a player for a specific upcoming opponent, what do you study about that opponent most, to get ready for the momentum swings?
**A:** Study the opponent's service games and how they break down under pressure: (1) first-serve % and what happens when it drops (hold comfortably vs dragged to deuce); (2) second-serve vulnerability (how often attacked, how they respond); (3) behavior at 30-30/deuce/break points (tighten with DFs/UEs vs raise level); (4) error patterns when behind (which shots break down first); (5) recent match history of runs and collapses (whether they give back a break or fold after losing one). The point = identify where their serve stops being a free hold, because that is where swings against them begin.
**How it changed the work:** This is the "differential / opponent-specific preparation" deliverable. Operationally: for any target opponent, compute from past match data (our dataset + any available) the five serve-breakdown metrics; the match-ups where the opponent's hold_rate is lowest and break-point save rate is worst are the matches to expect the most momentum swings, and the plan is to press the second serve and the deuce/break-point moments. This gives a concrete, data-driven pre-match checklist and directly answers "given the differential in past match momentum swings, how do you advise a player going into a new match against a different player?" The model's g_break2 (break shock) and the f1/f2/f3 prediction features ARE the opponent's "where the serve stops being a free hold" quantified.

## Exchange 8
**Q:** In a tie-break, does the momentum feel different from normal play, or does the same serve-wobble cue still apply?
**A:** Same cue applies but compressed and amplified. Serve-wobble still applies and matters MORE: every point is effectively a break-point-scale point; a single mini-break (losing a point on your own serve) is the swing marker; there is no "free hold" to absorb a bad point. Run length shorter — 2-3 consecutive points can decide it vs 3-5 in normal play; penalty for a lost serve point is immediate. Double faults and missed first serves disproportionately costly (no next game to recover). Momentum in a tie-break is more volatile and faster-decaying; it flips on single points rather than games; the "long deuce game" precursor is replaced by a mini-break plus a couple of cheap errors. Same cue, tighter timescale, higher stakes per point.
**How it changed the work:** Edge-case handling for the model. In a tiebreak: (1) use a FASTER base decay alpha0 (faster-decaying, flips on single points); (2) the serve-wobble term is weighted higher (every point is break-point scale); (3) the run-length threshold for "a real shift" drops from 3-5 to 2-3 points. In the momentum metric I implement this by modulating alpha0 upward and g_break2 upward on in_tiebreak points (already flagged in_tiebreak in the cleaned data). The prediction model already includes f8_tiebreak and it came out as a significant (negative) coefficient, consistent with the higher-volatility, faster-flip behavior.

## Exchange 9
**Q:** For a top server in a big match, what first-serve percentage is solid vs a warning sign of fading?
**A:** Solid = ~65-70%; above ~70% serving well/holding comfortably; ~60-65% acceptable normal range; ~55% and below = warning sign (repeated second serves, games get long, break risk rises sharply); ~50% or lower = clear trouble, serve no longer a free hold. The absolute number matters less than the DROP from that player's own baseline (a top server normally ~70% falling to 58% is fading even though 58% is fine for a weaker server).
**How it changed the work:** Calibrated the serve-wobble feature f1 (leader's first-serve share). I now define the wobble as a *baseline-relative* drop, not an absolute threshold: wobble_t = max(0, baseline_firstserve - current_firstserve), where baseline = the player's first-serve rate over their last ~20 points in the match, and the feature fires when the drop exceeds ~0.10 (i.e. from ~0.68 down to ~0.58). This replaces a hard absolute cut with a player-specific, match-relative signal, exactly as the expert described. I use the 0.65-0.70 "solid" band as the prior baseline and 0.55 as the "warning" reference in the pre-match checklist (Part 4).

## Exchange 10
**Q:** In a long five-set match, does the later part really wear players down, or does the flow stay even until the end?
**A:** The later part genuinely wears players down — the flow does not stay even. Physical decline is real and progressive: over 4-5 hours, first-serve % drops, second-serve speed falls, movement/recovery to corners slows, most pronounced past the ~3-hour mark and in sets 4-5. The decline is usually ASYMMETRIC: the older player, or the one who has spent more time defending/running, fades first; the fitter/younger player holds level longer; that asymmetry is what turns a close match into a late break. But it is NOT monotonic: adrenaline, a big hold, or a tiebreak can lift a tiring player briefly, so the fifth set can look sharp for stretches. Consequence: late swings are more often driven by accumulated fatigue and serve deterioration than by fresh momentum; breaks become more frequent as the match lengthens.
**How it changed the work:** This is the edge-case / boundary-condition answer (domain of validity at match end). It (a) justifies keeping a fatigue proxy (elapsed time / set number) in the model as a *modulator* of the serve-wobble and break terms rather than a standalone predictor — late in the match, the same first-serve drop and the same deuce count should carry MORE weight because fatigue makes them more decisive; (b) explains the fifth-set volatility of the target match (36-year-old Djokovic vs 20-year-old Alcaraz — the younger player held level longer, consistent with the asymmetric-fade mechanism); and (c) adds a caveat to the memo: in long matches, expect more swings late, and the younger/fitter player should plan to press the older opponent's serve in sets 4-5. I test the "breaks increase with set number" part empirically below.
