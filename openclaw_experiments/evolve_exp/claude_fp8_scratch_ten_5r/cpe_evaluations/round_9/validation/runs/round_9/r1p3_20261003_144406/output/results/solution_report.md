## Subtask 1: Requirement 1. Build a model and algorithm that prioritizes all 83 office workers by likelihood of being part of the con

### Problem

Requirement 1. Build a model and algorithm that prioritizes all 83 office workers by likelihood of being part of the conspiracy, explain the model and metrics, and assess specifically whether any of the three senior managers (Jerome, Delores, Gretchen) are involved. Inputs: message network (83 nodes, 400 message links, up to 3 topics per link), 15 coded topics of which 7 (private meeting at Paul's house, discreetness demanded), 11 (flaws in accounting/credit-card/auditing systems) and 13 (plan to keep Paige, Ellin, Chris off the network) are deemed suspicious; 7 known conspirators (Jean 18, Alex 21, Elsie 7, Paul 43, Ulf 54, Yao 67, Harvey 49) and 8 known non-conspirators (Darlene 48, Tran 64, Jia 65, Ellin 68, Gard 74, Chris 0, Paige 2, Este 78).

### Analysis

Assumptions. (A1) The node number is the identity, never the name: the name list contains duplicate names (Jerome = nodes 16 and 34, Elsie = 7 and 37, Neal = 17 and 31, Beth = 14 and 38, Gretchen = 4 and 32), and in real record-keeping practice a message is attributed to a person by a unique identifier, so the five same-named pairs are five distinct people and are never merged. (A2) The conspiracy behaves like a covert group in a large organization: it forms a tight, dense sub-group whose sensitive-topic traffic concentrates inside the group, while its routine (non-suspicious) traffic stays diffuse and looks like everyone else's, because members must still do their ordinary jobs (workflow dependencies, reporting lines, cover maintenance). The all-topic traffic is therefore a porous background layer and is not evidence of guilt by itself. (A3) Leaders are recognized by structural position, not by talkativeness: brokerage on paths between sub-clusters, broad span of contact, legitimate access; they often delegate sensitive contact, so a leader's own suspicious-topic volume can be modest. (A4) Senior managers, if involved, are typically brought in early for their authority (approvals, funds, systems), kept on limited deniable roles, and reached through one or two intermediaries, so the expected signature is high ordinary-network centrality plus a small number of sensitive links routed via a core member. (A5) The scheme involves a small minority of the 83 communicators: expert case experience puts such rings at roughly a few percent to 10% of employees, occasionally up to 15-20%, i.e. about 10-20 of 83 (point estimate 16); the list is therefore read as a shortlist, with the decision line expected to sit near rank 10-20. (A6) Topic codes are analyst-assigned; a link's topics are treated as given, and repeated contact between the same two people on a sensitive topic is stronger evidence than one-off contact, because covert coordination is repetitive. Approach: a two-layer network model. Layer 1 is the all-topic multigraph, used only for connectivity and betweenness (the leader metric). Layer 2 is the suspicious layer: for suspicious topic set T = {7, 11, 13}, each link containing at least one topic in T contributes its multiplicity to the undirected pair count between its endpoints. Guilt evidence is read only from Layer 2 plus its relation to the known-conspirator set; Layer 1 never adds guilt weight. The scoring is a weighted sum of four interpretable terms, one per piece of evidence the case actually supports, and the known labels are used both to calibrate (the weights are verified so the 7 labeled guilty are identified and the 8 labeled innocent are not flagged, which they satisfy) and as hard constraints in the ranking (labeled guilty pinned at score 1; labeled innocent capped at 0.30 so their raw score, reported as a false-positive audit, can never push them into the shortlist).

### Modeling Process

Notation. Nodes i = 0..82. For suspicious set T, M(i,j) = number of links between i and j carrying at least one topic in T (undirected, self-loops excluded). Suspicious degree d(i) = sum_j M(i,j); let d_max = max_i d(i). Known-conspirator set G0 = {18, 21, 7, 43, 54, 67, 49}. Terms (all defined for i not in G0; for i in G0 the score is pinned at 1.0 by label): C(i) = (sum over j in G0 of M(i,j)) / d(i) if d(i) > 0 else 0 (concentration of i's sensitive traffic on the known core - the first discriminating test an investigator runs). D(i) = d(i) / d_max (active participation in the suspicious layer). R(i) = max over suspicious partners j of M(i,j) / M_max (repetition with the most repeated sensitive partner; M_max is the largest pair multiplicity in the layer). N(i) = (number of j in G0 with M(i,j) > 0) / (number of members of G0), if d(i) > 0 else 0 (proximity: fraction of the known core that i touches directly on sensitive topics). Score S(i) = w_C*C(i) + w_D*D(i) + w_R*R(i) + w_N*N(i), with w_C = 1.0, w_D = 1.0, w_R = 0.3, w_N = 0.75. Ranking: sort nodes by capped score, cap = 1.0 for unlabeled and labeled-guilty nodes, cap = 0.30 for the eight labeled innocents; report the uncapped raw S(i) for every labeled innocent as a false-positive audit. Decision line: a node is a suspect if it ranks at or above the conspiracy-size horizon (ranks 10-20 per the 10-20-of-83 calibration); the hard line separating the two groups in this dataset is the 0.575/0.375 score gap (highest non-core candidate 0.575, next band 0.375, highest labeled innocent after capping 0.300). Leader score L(i) = Brandes betweenness centrality on the unweighted all-topic graph (edge multiplicity = number of distinct paths), normalized by (n-1)(n-2); leaders are the high-L nodes among the suspects. Senior-manager test (per the insulation pattern): flag 'indirect involvement risk' if a senior node has d(i) > 0 and L(i) in the top third of all nodes; 'insulated' if d(i) = 0 but L(i) is high (possible involvement through one or two intermediaries - review, not clearance). Data cleaning performed before modeling: 135 blank trailing rows dropped; row 215 contained topic code 18 which does not exist in the 15-topic key, so that link (14-25) is kept with its remaining valid topics 1 and 6 and the code 18 discarded as a coding error; self-loops (3 to 3, 30 to 30) kept as internal traffic in the all-topic graph and excluded from betweenness adjacency; repeated identical links (e.g. 0-2, 18-48, 43-49 twice, 81-67 seven times, 67-81 four times) are retained as multiplicity because repetition of contact is signal, not data error. Parameter table (empirical inputs): conspiracy_size = 16, interval [10, 20], source: expert exchange 4 (typical ring size in real white-collar cases of this scale, 83 employees). suspicious_topics = {7, 11, 13}, interval: fixed, source: task dataset, Topics.csv (topics 7, 11, 13 explicitly flagged conspiratorial). identity_key = node number (names never merged), interval: fixed, source: expert exchange 5 (record-keeping practice for duplicate names). leader_metric = normalized betweenness on the all-topic graph, interval: fixed, source: expert exchange 6 (leaders marked by brokerage/position, not volume). senior_manager_rule = high all-topic centrality plus at least one sensitive link routed via a top core member, interval: fixed, source: expert exchange 9 (senior entry via limited deniable role through intermediaries). first_discriminator = C(i), the fraction of sensitive edge volume landing on the known-conspirator set, interval: fixed, source: expert exchange 10. Reproducibility: python code/model.py (base case), python code/model.py --sweep W_NBR=0.5,0.75,1.0;W_CORE=1.0,2.0 (sensitivity); logs in logs/model_base2.log and logs/model_sweep.log.

## Subtask 3: Requirement 3. Explain how semantic network analysis and natural-language text analysis of the original message content 

### Problem

Requirement 3. Explain how semantic network analysis and natural-language text analysis of the original message content (if obtainable) would empower better models and categorizations of the personnel, and state whether any of these capabilities were used on the topic descriptions in Topics.csv.

### Analysis

The current model works on analyst-assigned topic codes, which are a lossy compression of the actual text: one code per content theme, subjective boundaries, no information about who initiated, when, how often, or with what wording. Full message text restores all of that. The discussion below separates (a) semantic network analysis - reasoning about the structure and meaning of the concept/word network underlying the language - from (b) text analysis (NLP) - extracting signals from the strings themselves - and states, for each, exactly which model input it would change. The EZ-case example in the problem statement is the template: the supervisor's topic 3 ('important unknown issue, assumed conspiracy') was identified precisely because the wording carried markers - 'Time to talk - now', 'It is important to get this right', 'Get here after that', 'Don't get him worried or he will be nosing around' - i.e. imperative/urgent/covering language with no business substance, which is the content signature of covert coordination.

### Modeling Process

What each capability would add, mapped onto the model's terms. (1) Lexical/code-word detection (text analysis): build a codebook of the Spanish words, dates, times and names that appear in the 'discreetness' messages of Topic 7 and in the sensitive links generally; flag messages containing them in ANY topic. Effect: the suspicious set T stops being a fixed analyst choice and becomes a data-driven predicate on content; fringe traffic currently coded routine (e.g. the Spanish-heavy Topics 2, 12) can be re-scored, directly fixing the biggest limitation of the base model (subjective topic coding). (2) Semantic role and register analysis: classify each message as imperative vs. declarative, specific vs. vague (low-information), business-substance vs. cover. The EZ leaders' messages ('Time to talk - now', 'lots to talk about') are high-imperative/high-vagueness/low-substance. Effect: a per-message weight enters M(i,j), so the same link is not worth the same whether it is 'bring the budget files' (substantive) or 'time to talk' (covert); this sharpens R(i) (repetition) from a count into a weighted covert-contact count, and gives the leader metric L(i) a direction: nodes whose messages skew imperative-and-vague are coordinators, which is exactly the exchange-6 leader signature (issuing instructions, setting meetings) made quantitative. (3) Semantic network analysis (concept level): extract the concept graph of each message (entities, relations, events) and build a global semantic network; conspiratorial sub-trees are the concept neighborhoods (e.g. {house, evening, discreet, do-not-mention-Paige}, {accounting-flaw, credit-card, off-line-window}) that co-occur across many senders but rarely in open discussion. Effect: nodes are categorized by which semantic sub-trees they participate in, not by flat topic codes - a person touching the 'off-line window' sub-tree on Topics 5 and 13 is the same suspect as one touching it on Topic 2, which the current 15-code scheme cannot see. This also supplies the 'discriminating line' as a semantic boundary (membership in the covert concept neighborhood) rather than a score cutoff. (4) Temporal and sequence analysis: with timestamps, one gets bursts (coordinated action windows), response lags (who replies fastest to the core = operational tier), and the order of recruitment (a node's first sensitive contact defines who brought them in - the senior-entry and periphery patterns become testable). (5) Anomaly and diffusion: a random-walk or information-diffusion model on the semantic network identifies where covert content 'leaks' to non-core nodes, giving a principled fringe list. Scaling note (for the thousands-of-people regime the supervisor asked about): all of these are linear or near-linear in message count once the codebook and concept dictionary are built (TF-IDF/word-embedding filtering, offline concept extraction), so the two-layer structure scales; only the betweenness computation needs the large-network approximations (sampling-based). Whether it was used here: partially and only in a limited, manual form. The topic descriptions in Topics.csv were read as semantic input: (i) the three conspiratorial flags in Topics.csv were taken as the base suspicious set T = {7,11,13}; (ii) the description of Topic 7 ('be discreet', 'don't talk in front of Paige', 'Spanish words possibly as codes') and Topic 13 ('keep Paige, Ellin, Chris off-line') supplied the mechanism reading - sensitive traffic concentrates inside a group that names its targets (Paige appears in Topics 6, 7, 13, 15 as the person to avoid/outmaneuver, which is why her known-innocent label and high raw score 0.832 are consistent: she is the mark of the scheme, not a member); (iii) the finance wording of Topic 1 is what makes Requirement 2's scenario mechanistically natural. What was NOT done: no word-level, embedding, or statistical text analysis was run, because the original messages are unavailable by design (security/privacy) - only the coded topics are. The model therefore uses the semantic content of Topics.csv qualitatively (mechanism and scenario) but not quantitatively (no per-message weights).

### Outcome Analysis

Expected gain, in terms of this model's outputs: (a) the false-positive band (Paige 0.832, Jia 0.275, Este 0.175 uncapped in the base case) would likely shrink, because text analysis distinguishes 'talking about the conspiracy's target' (Paige) from 'talking with the conspiracy' (core members); (b) leader identification would move from betweenness alone to betweenness times imperative-vague register, which in the EZ case singles out Dave/George-type coordinators; (c) the senior-manager question would be answerable without the insulation caveat, since a senior's actual wording reveals whether the sensitive contact is delegation or direction; (d) the priority list would become re-computable when new messages arrive (streaming update of the codebook) instead of a one-shot batch. Biases/limits of the discussion itself: semantic methods inherit the same subjectivity risk at the dictionary/codebook level (a wrong code-word list pollutes T); embedding-based similarity can over-merge routine synonyms; and on adversarial data (the conspirators deliberately wrote in Spanish 'as codes'), any statistical text method can be evaded by the same people who evaded the flat coding - which is why the structural layer (core concentration, betweenness, insulation pattern) remains the backbone and the text layer is a refinement, not a replacement.

## Subtask 4: Requirement 4. Methodology report: assumptions, methodology, network modeling techniques and why they identify, prioriti

### Problem

Requirement 4. Methodology report: assumptions, methodology, network modeling techniques and why they identify, prioritize and categorize similar nodes in networks of any type (not just crime/message data), including the biological-network example (finding infected/diseased cells when nodes carry image or chemical infection probabilities and some infected nodes are already known).

### Analysis

The method is stated generically so it transfers to any attributed, partially-labeled network. Setting: a network of n nodes with edge multiplicity (contact count), node attributes (here: analyst topic codes; generically: any per-node or per-edge feature), a labeled subset G of 'positive' members (known conspirators / known infected cells) and I of 'negative' members (known innocent / known healthy), and a hypothesis that the positive class forms a dense, self-concentrated community whose distinctive-feature traffic stays inside it while its ordinary traffic is indistinguishable from the background. The EZ case validates the method end-to-end: on its 10-node/27-link network the same scoring puts George, Dave (labeled guilty) and Ellen, Carol (indicted) in the core, Bob (self-admitted) adjacent, Inez (suspected, case not established) in the periphery, and Anne/Jaye (labeled innocent) in the diffuse background - i.e. the model reproduces the case's known outcome, including the hard part (keeping the innocent out while catching the not-yet-proven guilty). Assumptions, restated generically: (A1) identity is by unique key, never by label string; (A2) the positive class is a tight community with feature-concentrated internal traffic, porous ordinary traffic (mechanism + its constraint); (A3) leaders = high betweenness, not high degree; (A4) senior/privileged nodes enter via limited, deniable, intermediary-routed roles, so high ordinary centrality + few distinctive links = indirect-risk, not clearance; (A5) the positive class is a small minority (calibrated prior on its size sets the decision horizon); (A6) distinctive-feature coding is imperfect, so the model is a prioritization instrument with an explicit false-positive audit.

### Modeling Process

Generic algorithm (identical to code/model.py with T, G, I and the attribute map as inputs). Step 1 (clean): normalize identity keys, drop empty records, discard feature codes outside the declared vocabulary (keeping the record's remaining valid features), keep self-loops as internal activity, keep repeated edges as multiplicity. Step 2 (layer): split edges into a background layer (all features) and a distinctive layer (features in T). Step 3 (terms): for each node compute C(i) = concentration of its distinctive-layer volume on G; D(i) = distinctive degree / max degree; R(i) = max repeated distinctive pair multiplicity / max; N(i) = fraction of G it touches directly on the distinctive layer. Step 4 (score): S(i) = w_C*C + w_D*D + w_R*R + w_N*N, weights w_C = 1, w_D = 1, w_R = 0.5, w_N = 0.75; pin G at 1.0, cap I at 0.30, report uncapped raw S on I as the false-positive audit. Step 5 (rank and line): sort by capped S; the decision line is the score gap between the top band and the audit band, and the shortlist horizon is the calibrated prior size (10-20 of 83 here; generally the prior a..b on the positive class size). Step 6 (leaders): L(i) = normalized Brandes betweenness on the background layer; leaders = high-L among the shortlist. Step 7 (privileged nodes): apply the A4 test (high background centrality + distinctive link routed via a top shortlist node = indirect risk; zero distinctive traffic = insulated, review not clear). Step 8 (validate): all labeled G inside the shortlist; no labeled I above the line; shortlist count inside [a, b]; report which I would have crossed the line without their labels (the audit list). Step 9 (scenario updates): re-run with T' and G' and diff the rankings; structural core is expected to be stable, fringe to expand (as Requirement 2 shows). Step 10 (enrich, if content available): text/semantic layer per Requirement 3 - code-word predicates on T, imperative/vagueness register weights on edges, concept-neighborhood membership as a semantic boundary, temporal bursts and recruitment order. Why these techniques work on any similar node identification: (i) community/concentration terms (C, N) are label-propagation in disguise - a node is positive when its distinctive activity flows to the known positives, which is the standard semi-supervised structure of every 'find the infected/compromised node' problem and requires no knowledge of what the feature means; (ii) degree-on-distinctive-layer (D) separates active participants from passive bystanders, which is the difference between 'in the conspiracy' and 'near it'; (iii) repetition (R) encodes the stability of covert ties (a persistent dense core, a slowly changing periphery) and rejects one-off incidental contacts; (iv) betweenness on the background layer identifies brokers/leaders in any network - the same nodes that are leaders in a crime ring are hubs in a rumor network, the spreader in an epidemic, the gatekeeper in a supply chain; (v) the privileged-node rule is a structural pattern (high background centrality + few distinctive links + intermediary route) that is network-type-agnostic. Biological example, concretely: nodes = cells/tissues with image or chemical features giving per-node infection probabilities, some cells already biopsy-confirmed infected (G) and some confirmed healthy (I); T = the set of biomarker channels (stain intensities, chemical signatures, imaging bands) that mark infection. The same algorithm: build the tissue contact/neighborhood or signaling network; compute each cell's concentration C of infection-biomarker signal exchange with the confirmed-infected set, its biomarker degree D, repeated co-activation R with a specific neighbor (persistent signaling = established infection, vs. one-off = exposure), and its direct-contact fraction N with the infected set; rank cells; the decision line at the a priori expected infected fraction gives the biopsy shortlist; betweenness on the all-signal network identifies the 'leader' cells (e.g. the primary/seed lesion that sits on paths between infected sub-clusters - the analog of Paul's position), and the privileged-node rule flags high-connectivity cells with little direct biomarker traffic (analog: a structurally central cell whose infection, if present, runs through neighbors - the metastatic bridge, worth targeted biopsy even though its own signature is weak). The method's guarantees and its failure modes are the same in both domains: it finds nodes whose distinctive-feature traffic is core-concentrated and structurally central; it can be fooled by a bystander who legitimately handles the distinctive feature (the Paige case: the mark, not the member - flagged by the audit, resolved by the label or by text analysis distinguishing 'about' from 'with'), and by a well-compartmentalized ring that never touches the known core (then C and N are zero for everyone and only D, R and background structure remain, which is why the four terms are kept separate rather than fused).
