# Expert Interaction Evidence

## Exchange 1
- Question (expert_question_1.md): "When a foundation gives a college one grant for one goal, what does the college actually spend that money on first?"
- Reply (summary of values, source: exchange 1): grant dollars go first to direct incremental costs of the goal, in order: (1) personnel, 50-70% of a program grant; (2) student financial support if the goal is access/retention; (3) program operations; (4) indirect/admin overhead, 10-20%, sometimes funder-capped. Caveats: fungibility (restricted grants often free existing funds rather than fund new activity); spending follows the program start-up timeline, costs drawn as incurred, not all at once.
- How it changed the work: ROI numerator is student outcomes, not grant dollars; a fixed per-school amount can only shift outcomes if the school can deploy it into direct program costs (tutoring, advising, emergency aid) rather than overhead or replacing existing staff. Two model consequences adopted: (a) an effectiveness gate per school — schools that cannot add net-new direct program cost from a lump sum (e.g., tiny colleges whose program cost exceeds the grant, or schools locked into overhead) get capped effectiveness; (b) the 5-year grant is modeled as an annuity over the start-up/drawdown window, not a day-0 shock, with the effect ramping over the drawdown period (parameter, later exchanges) and a post-grant persistence factor (parameter, later exchanges).

## Exchange 2
- Question (expert_question_2.md): "What stops a small college from spending a big grant on extra tutors and advisors?"
- Reply (values, source: exchange 2): binding limits are (1) restricted-purpose terms with audits/clawback; (2) absorptive capacity — hiring/training/supervising staff faster than it can manage produces poor results; (3) recurring-cost (salary cliff) risk — a 5-year grant creates a cliff at year 5, thin-margin small colleges won't commit recurring salaries; (4) scale mismatch — a grant far larger than the institution's operating budget can't be spent productively; not enough qualified people, students, or program slots; (5) board/accreditation/donor scrutiny on sudden unsustainable staffing expansions.
- How it changed the work: added the scale-mismatch constraint as a hard cap: grant to a school cannot exceed a fraction k of its annual operating scale (operating budget is not in the dataset; proxied by UGDS x per-student cost parameter, calibrated in a later exchange). Schools whose grant would exceed k x UGDS x C_per_student get their effective grant capped at that amount, with the excess reallocated (this is the constraint that stops the model from piling $10M onto a 400-student college). The salary-cliff point is encoded as the post-grant persistence factor: a school that must absorb or drop the program at year 5 keeps only a fraction of the outcome gain — that fraction is a parameter to be calibrated (exchange 5/6). Absorptive capacity enters as the ramp: the outcome effect is 0 until staffing scales up, so the drawdown/ramp window is a parameter (exchange 4).

## Exchange 3
- Question (expert_question_3.md): "Roughly how long before new tutors and extra advising measurably change college graduation rates?"
- Reply (values, source: exchange 3): short-term retention/credit-accumulation effects visible in 1-2 semesters (~1 year); graduation-rate effects typically 3-5 years after program start (4-year cohorts: first credible signal ~year 4; 2-year/150%-time community colleges: ~years 2-3). Effects usually modest, a few percentage points; cohort/150% reporting basis lags; several cohorts needed before the change is distinguishable from noise.
- How it changed the work: fixed the timing structure of the ROI model. Effect ramp R(t): 0 in year 1 (start-up/drawdown from exchange 1), full effect from year T_ramp, where T_ramp = 4 for 4-year institutions and 3 for 2-year (PCT_2YR or DISTANCEONLY/program-mix indicator from dataset; default 4). Graduation gain is therefore only realizable within the 5-year window for 2-year schools (full window) and partially for 4-year schools (signal ~year 4, one year of full effect in-window, plus a tail of in-progress cohorts that convert post-window — valued with a persistence/discount factor). Modest-effects caveat => ROI metric uses percentage-point gain in graduation/retention rate as numerator (not raw dollars), and I set a detectability floor: schools whose modeled gain is below ~1 percentage point get a "low-confidence" flag and lower priority, since the gain would be indistinguishable from year-to-year noise.

## Exchange 4
- Question (expert_question_4.md): "If the grant stops at year five, what fraction of that graduation boost usually remains?"
- Reply (values, source: exchange 4): if funded staff/activities stop at year 5, the graduation gain decays back to pre-grant baseline within ~2-4 years; residual only a few tenths of a pp to ~1 pp if practices were institutionalized. If the college absorbs the recurring cost or embeds the practices, a substantial share, often half or more, persists. Determinant is continuation, not the end date.
- How it changed the work: the persistence factor is now a two-branch parameter, not a single number: P_absorb = 0.5 (fraction of the gain persisting if the college absorbs the cost) and P_fade = 0.1 (residual if the program is discontinued), with a decay time of 3 years post-grant for the fading branch (midpoint of 2-4). The model computes the probability that a school absorbs the program, q_school, from its fiscal headroom (exchange 5/6 to calibrate the headroom threshold), so expected persistence is P_eff = q*P_absorb + (1-q)*P_fade. Schools that cannot absorb (thin margins) lose most of the long-run value, which tilts the allocation toward schools with enough fiscal slack to keep the program running — this is the mechanism that makes the 5-year finite grant an investable asset rather than a cliff.

## Exchange 5
- Question (expert_question_5.md): "What kind of college realistically keeps the tutors after the grant ends?"
- Reply (values, source: exchange 5): keepers = (1) operating surpluses/healthy endowments; (2) public institutions with a funding path (state appropriations, levy, system support); (3) programs that proved out on the school's own metrics and became core strategy; (4) activities embedded into existing structures (reassigned staff, peer tutoring, advising inside faculty roles) rather than a separate grant unit; (5) larger institutions where marginal cost per student is small. Non-keepers: small, tuition-dependent colleges with thin margins and enrollment pressure, "exactly the profile most likely to receive a grant in the first place".
- How it changed the work: q_school (probability of absorbing the program) is computed from dataset-available proxies: control flag (public => funding path, +), scale (UGDS above a threshold, +), and fiscal-stress proxy. The dataset has no balance sheet, so fiscal headroom is proxied by per-student cost level (NPT41/NPT42 net price per credit) and PCTPELL (tuition-dependent, Pell-heavy students signal thin margins): a school with high PCTPELL and low scale gets low q. This creates a real tension the model must respect: the same features (small, tuition-dependent, Pell-heavy) make a school an attractive target on need, but they lower q, which cuts expected long-run value. The allocation model handles this by scoring both impact and sustainability and making the trade-off explicit in the final ranking. Note the data caveat: the 2015 Scorecard has no endowment or surplus field, so "surplus/healthy endowment" is only partially observable; this is a documented limitation.

## Exchange 6
- Question (expert_question_6.md): "How much tutoring and advising cost per student a year at a typical American college?"
- Reply (values, source: exchange 6): incremental cost per student per year: light-touch/group tutoring or drop-in advising $100-$300; structured staff-intensive (dedicated advisors, scheduled tutoring, case management) $500-$1,500; high-intensity one-on-one/wraparound $2,000-$5,000+. Dominant driver is student-to-staff ratio: a full-time advisor/coordinator costs ~$50,000-$80,000 loaded, so 200 students per advisor => ~$300-500/student, 50 per advisor => ~$1,200-1,600/student. Community colleges and large publics at the lower end; small private and intensive programs at the upper end.
- How it changed the work: calibrated C_prog (per-student annual program cost). The model uses a mid-intensity design: C_prog = $1,000/student/year, interval [500, 1500] (structured staff-intensive band, matching a foundation that expects a real program, not drop-in tutoring). Per-school sustainable program size is S_max = G / (C_prog x 5) students over the 5-year grant; per exchange 2's scale-mismatch constraint, a school can productively serve at most ~UGDS students, so the grant is productive only if G <= 5 x C_prog x UGDS x k, and effective funded students are min(S_max, UGDS). At $1,000/student/year a $1M grant funds ~200 students for 5 years; a 2,000-student school is not scale-capped, a 300-student school is (200 < 300 OK; a $5M grant would cap at 1,500-student capacity). The advisor-count arithmetic (50-80k loaded salary; 200 vs 50 per advisor) is used in the CFO letter to show what the grant buys in staff terms.

## Exchange 7
- Question (expert_question_7.md): "What graduation boost have targeted tutoring and advising programs actually achieved at American colleges?"
- Reply (values, source: exchange 7): graduation-rate gains modest, commonly ~2-5 pp, occasionally ~10 for well-implemented intensive programs for disadvantaged students. Structured advising/coaching (intensive case management, community colleges): 3-8 pp completion/persistence, largest for first-gen/low-income. Tutoring alone: 1-3 pp, more visible in pass rates/retention than graduation. High-intensity small-caseload programs reach the upper end; effects shrink with scale or less intensity. Effects vary by institution, population, design; single-cohort gains can be within year-to-year noise.
- How it changed the work: calibrated the effect size Delta(g) as a function of served share. Base gain Delta0 = 3 pp (median of the 2-5 pp band; interval [2,5]), scaled up toward 8 pp at the ceiling for intensive, high-PCTPELL/first-gen-serve populations (the 3-8 pp advising band), and down to ~1.5 pp if the program covers only a small share of students (tutoring-alone band 1-3). Formula: Delta = Delta0 x (0.5 + 0.5 x served_share) x (1 + 0.5 x PCTPELL), clipped to [0.5, 8] pp. The "shrink with scale" caveat is implemented through served_share = funded_students/UGDS: a $1M grant at a 5,000-student school funds 200 students = 4% of students, so its per-capita intensity is low and the gain is near the bottom of the band; at a 400-student school the same grant funds 200 = 50% and the gain is mid-band. This makes small, capable schools win on ROI per dollar even though the absolute number of graduates is smaller, which matches the expert's warning that effects shrink when scaled.

## Exchange 8
- Question (expert_question_8.md): "Do grant foundations prefer one big gift to a few schools, or smaller gifts to many?"
- Reply (values, source: exchange 8): neither as a rule; both patterns common. Concentration (few large gifts) when the goal needs scale, measurable attributable results, close engagement, lower admin cost per dollar. Breadth (many small gifts) to seed/test across sites, build a field, spread risk; dilutes per-site impact and raises admin cost. For a funder like Goodgrant ($100M/year, seeking demonstrated potential and measurable return), the usual practice is a middle path: a prioritized list with a modest number of larger concentrated grants to the strongest candidates plus smaller awards to a wider set. Pure breadth => effects too small to detect; pure concentration => bets everything on a few institutions.
- How it changed the work: the deliverable is a 1-to-N prioritized candidate list with per-school amounts, so the allocation is a two-tier portfolio: Tier 1 = concentrated grants (the "modest number" of strongest candidates, sized $3M-$10M each) and Tier 2 = smaller awards ($1M-$2.5M) to a wider set. The split and tier sizes are set by the scale-mismatch constraint from exchange 2 (grant <= 5 x C_prog x UGDS x k) and the detectability floor from exchange 3 (a grant must fund enough students to push the modeled gain above ~1 pp). I will calibrate the tier counts in the remaining exchanges. Admin cost per dollar (concentration is cheaper to administer) enters as a small efficiency adjustment in the ROI: larger grants have a lower admin overhead rate, using the 10-20% overhead band from exchange 1 (admin = 15% of grant for Tier 2, 12% for Tier 1).

## Exchange 9
- Question (expert_question_9.md): "How many schools can a foundation realistically keep close tabs on at once?"
- Reply (values, source: exchange 9): professional program staff can keep close relationships with ~20-50 grantees at once; beyond that, engagement thins to routine reporting. Deep hands-on engagement: ~10-25 grantees per program officer. Active lighter management (annual reports, milestone checks): 50-150 grantees manageable. Beyond a few hundred: oversight is administrative, paper monitoring only. For a $100M/year funder, concentrated grants to a prioritized list of a few dozen schools is the practical norm, matching money and staff capacity.
- How it changed the work: fixed the portfolio size. N = 40 funded schools per year: 20 Tier 1 (concentrated, deep engagement, ~$5M each => $100M... no, $100M total budget) — with $100M/yr and 40 schools: Tier 1 = 15 schools at ~$5.33M avg, Tier 2 = 25 schools at ~$1.8M avg (15x5.33 + 25x1.8 = 80+45=125, rebalanced in code: Tier 1 12 schools at $5.5M = $66M, Tier 2 28 schools at ~$1.21M = $34M, total $100M). Deep engagement cap of 25 per officer is respected with 2 program officers (15+15 deep, rest lighter). The candidate list itself is 1-to-N: I will produce a ranked list of the top 40 funded plus the next 60 ranked-but-unfunded as the prioritized candidate list (N=100), which the CFO letter can draw from.

## Exchange 10
- Question (expert_question_10.md): "Which school outcome would a foundation trust most to judge whether a grant worked?"
- Reply (values, source: exchange 10): the outcome a foundation trusts is the one it can attribute to the grant and verify independently: direct, near-term, program-linked measures. Ranked trust: (1) retention/persistence and credit accumulation (fall-to-spring, year-to-year) — closest to the funded activity, visible in 1-2 semesters, least confounded; (2) course pass rates and remediation completion — directly tied to tutoring/advising; (3) graduation/completion — the outcome everyone cares about but the least trustworthy for judging a single grant: lags 3-5 years, cohort-based, moves a few points, confounded by admissions/economy/student mix. Foundations want graduation but trust intermediate retention/credit measures most and treat graduation as long-run confirmation, not proof.
- How it changed the work: the ROI metric is built on the trusted intermediate outcome, not graduation alone. The dataset provides RET_FT4 (4-year full-time retention) and the 6-year graduation proxy via C150_4_POOLED_SUPP (6-year completion/graduation, pooled) — wait, I need to verify which column is which in the data dictionary before finalizing. Per exchange 3, retention effects are visible in 1-2 semesters, so RET_FT4 is the primary near-term outcome (weight 0.6 in the composite) and the 6-year completion is the long-run confirmation (weight 0.4). The composite outcome O = 0.6 x retention_gain + 0.4 x completion_gain, both in percentage points, is the numerator of ROI. The graduation-only view is kept as a sensitivity (reported separately), which matches the expert's "long-run confirmation rather than the proof" — and it is the reason the model can show value within the 5-year window: retention gains land in years 1-2, completion confirmation in years 3-5, so the composite is realizable inside the grant period even though pure graduation is only partly realizable.
