# Interaction evidence

## Exchange 1
Question (expert_question_1.md): In southwestern US states, what mostly determines how much renewable energy a state ends up using in a given year?
Reply (paraphrased, not copied): dominant driver is resource endowment interacting with the existing generation mix — geography sets what is technically available; the legacy fleet and industry set the baseline being displaced; policy and fuel economics drive year-to-year movement; population is a scale factor for total demand, not for the renewable share.
Effect on work: the model is structured as renewable share = structural level (resource endowment + legacy fossil fleet) + year-to-year movement (policy/economics). Population is used only to normalize total demand (per-capita series) and never as a driver of share. This is encoded in the decomposition used in profile.py and the forecasts: baseline fossil-share persistence per state, renewable share trend from the last decade, and per-capita demand drift.

## Exchange 2
Question (expert_question_2.md): When cheap existing coal or gas plants are still running, what stops a state from replacing them with renewables faster?
Reply (paraphrased, not copied): binding constraints are economic and operational: sunk-cost/marginal-cost economics (cheap gas undercuts new builds), intermittency (dispatchable firm capacity still needed for peaks/reliability), transmission limits (renewable-rich sites remote from load, slow permitting), and utility market structure (cost recovery, long plant lifetimes). Absent a forcing mechanism (policy), the fossil fleet persists.
Effect on work: the no-policy-change forecast (Part I.D) must therefore NOT extrapolate the renewable-share trend linearly upward — the constraints cap the pace. Concretely: (a) fossil shares are modeled to persist (cheap marginal cost), so renewables only replace what is added or retires at end-of-life; (b) the renewable share growth rate is capped by the dispatchable-capacity requirement — I bound the share trajectory with a saturation (logistic-type) form rather than linear; (c) transmission permitting is treated as a slow constraint, so 2050 share targets stay well below resource ceilings; (d) policy is explicitly the "forcing mechanism", so the compact goals (Part II) are what lift the pace above the no-policy forecast — the gap between (d) forecast and (a) target IS the policy effect, which I quantify in Part II.A.

## Exchange 3
Question (expert_question_3.md): Without new policy forcing change, roughly what share of electricity do typical states actually get from wind and solar by the mid-2020s?
Reply (paraphrased, not copied): nationally wind+solar ≈ 15–20% of electricity generation by the mid-2020s (wind ~10%, utility-scale solar ~5–7% + small-scale); state-level range is wide (wind-leading states 30–60%+). Border states, mid-2020s order-of-magnitude: TX ~20–25% (wind-dominant), CA ~25–30% (solar-dominant), NM ~20–30% (rising from low base), AZ ~10–15%.
Effect on work: these are mid-2020s electricity-generation shares — exactly the calibration anchors for the Part I.D no-policy forecast of the electricity sub-profile. They enter as calibration endpoints (interval values) for the saturation-capped electricity-generation renewable-share trajectory fitted per state, and as order-of-magnitude bounds that the fitted 2025 values must respect. Parameter table (used in forecast.py): WIND_SOLAR_ELEC_MID2020S_TX = 0.20–0.25, CA = 0.25–0.30, NM = 0.20–0.30, AZ = 0.10–0.15; NATIONAL_WIND_SOLAR_ELEC_MID2020S = 0.15–0.20. Source: expert exchange 3 (2026-10-03). Interval of validity: mid-2020s, order-of-magnitude, used as calibration bounds not point estimates.

## Exchange 4
Question (expert_question_4.md): Once a state decides to retire an old fossil plant, how quickly is the replacement power usually built?
Reply (paraphrased, not copied): the old fleet is NOT replaced unit-for-unit; plants retire gradually over years-to-decades, driven by age, economics, and environmental rules; replacement is backfilled by a mix of new gas, renewables, storage, and demand-side measures; life extensions and "must-run" capacity resources are common, so the fleet shrinks slowly and unevenly, with renewables added alongside rather than in strict substitution.
Effect on work: this fixes the dynamics used in forecast.py for the no-policy case: fossil capacity is a slowly-decaying stock (retirement at end-of-life pace, partially offset by new gas backfill — net fossil decline is slow), and the renewable share rise is driven by additions on top of a slowly-shrinking fossil base, not by 1:1 substitution. Concretely, the model uses a slow exponential decay for fossil generation with a partial replacement offset, plus logistic-type growth for wind+solar electricity from the 2009 base toward the exchange-3 mid-2020s calibration band, then continued slow growth to 2050 bounded by the exchange-2 constraints (intermittency/reliability and transmission permitting).

## Exchange 5
Question (expert_question_5.md): Which action gets a state toward a renewable energy goal fastest - new plants, buying cleaner power, or cutting usage?
Reply (paraphrased, not copied): states almost always build supply first — new utility-scale wind/solar is the default, fastest, most visible lever, paired with procurement mandates (RPS-style) that force utilities to contract for it; importing (buying) renewable power from out of state is also common where local resources are limited, but still needs transmission and contracts, so it follows or accompanies in-state builds; demand-side efficiency is real and often the cheapest option, but slow, diffuse, and rarely the headline first move — it comes as a supporting measure (building codes, appliance standards, efficiency programs). Practical order: build (and procure) supply first; reduce demand alongside.
Effect on work: this structures the Part II.B list of compact actions and the mechanism by which the compact goals (Part II.A) exceed the no-policy forecast: Action 1 = in-state utility-scale wind+solar buildout with a binding procurement/RPS-style mandate per state (the forcing mechanism that overcomes the exchange-2 constraints); Action 2 = interstate transmission interconnection and renewable-energy procurement contracts between the four states (addresses the transmission constraint and leverages each state's comparative resource — AZ/CA solar, NM geothermal/solar, TX wind); Action 3 = demand-side efficiency program (building codes, appliance standards, efficiency programs) as the supporting, cheapest lever that also shrinks the denominator and slows the need for new fossil capacity. The goals in Part II.A are then set as per-state renewable-share targets for 2025/2050, and the model quantifies the gap between the no-policy 2050 forecast and each target as the implied policy effect of these actions.

## Exchange 6
Question (expert_question_6.md): What is the main reason a new wind or solar project gets cancelled or delayed in these states?
Reply (paraphrased, not copied): the main cause is local siting and permitting opposition, not economics or technology — projects stall at the county/land-use stage over zoning, setback rules, moratoriums, noise and viewshed complaints, and land-use/environmental lawsuits; transmission interconnection is the second big killer (long queues, cost-allocation disputes, new lines through opposing land); pure economics is less common since projects that reach development usually have decent resource and offtake prospects. The binding constraint is getting permission to build and connect at a specific site, so timelines slip by years and some projects are abandoned.
Effect on work: this refines Action 1 in Part II.B — the compact cannot rely on a naive buildout rate; it must include a siting/permitting streamlining measure (e.g. a coordinated regional permitting standard across the four states' counties) as an explicit sub-action, because permitting latency is the dominant risk to the 2025/2050 buildout schedule. It also justifies the conservative slope of the compact-growth term in the model (I do NOT let the policy-driven renewable growth run at an unbounded rate; it is bounded by the permitting/construction pipeline that the compact actions are designed to shorten). The 2025 target in Part II.A is set reachable given this pipeline latency, and the 2050 target assumes the streamlined permitting is in place for the back half of the period.

## Exchange 7
Question (expert_question_7.md): In states that run a renewable portfolio standard, what share of power do utilities usually have to buy from renewables?
Reply (paraphrased, not copied): RPS targets in US states typically require roughly 15–30% of retail electricity sales from renewables by the mid-2020s, with a long tail — a few states mandate 50–100% (California, New York, Hawaii, and others with 2040–2050 clean/renewable goals), while some set lower or voluntary targets in the single digits to ~15%. Two qualifications: the mandate is usually on retail sales (not generation), so it can be met partly by imports and credits; and many states allow credit trading, carryover, and alternative compliance payments, so the effective required share is often softer than the headline number.
Effect on work: this calibrates the compact goal magnitudes in Part II.A. I set the 2025 compact renewable-share target near the upper-middle of the typical RPS range (so the target is a real but conventional commitment), and the 2050 target in the upper part of the RPS long-tail range (aligned with the states that have set 2040–2050 goals, which is the natural template for a forward-looking four-state compact). Parameter table (used in goals.py): RPS_MID2020S_TYPICAL = 0.15–0.30 (fraction of retail sales), RPS_2040_2050_LEADERS = 0.50–1.00. Source: expert exchange 7 (2026-10-03). Because the mandate is on retail sales and can be met partly by imports/credits, the 2025 compact target is set as a combined in-state-buildout-plus-interstate-procurement requirement, and the 2050 target is set so it is achievable with the permitting streamlining (exchange 6) in place. The softness from credit trading/carryover means the headline target is deliberately set slightly above the minimum the model judges achievable, so the compact retains a compliance buffer.

## Exchange 8
Question (expert_question_8.md): How much cheaper is electricity from a new solar or wind plant compared to a new gas plant these days?
Reply (paraphrased, not copied): on a levelized-cost basis, new utility-scale solar and onshore wind are generally at or below new combined-cycle gas — commonly cited ranges put solar PV around $30–50/MWh and onshore wind around $25–45/MWh, versus roughly $45–75/MWh for a new combined-cycle gas plant (before any carbon cost). So renewables are typically cheaper by roughly 20–50%, often more in high-resource areas. Two qualifications: gas LCOE is highly sensitive to fuel price (at very low gas prices the gap narrows or closes), and LCOE ignores intermittency — solar/wind need storage or backup for firm capacity, which adds cost; gas provides dispatchable capacity renewables don't replace one-for-one.
Effect on work: this is the economic justification for the compact's buildout-first action (exchange 5) and bounds the no-policy forecast (exchange 2's "cheap gas undercuts renewables" concern): the no-policy model must NOT assume renewables are economically unattractive (they are now at/below new gas), but it must still honor the firm-capacity and gas-price-sensitivity caveats, so the no-policy 2050 share stays meaningfully below the RPS 2050 target — the gap is the value of the policy forcing mechanism, not an economics gap. Parameter table (used in forecast.py and goals.py): LCOE_SOLAR_PV = 30–50 USD/MWh, LCOE_ONSHORE_WIND = 25–45 USD/MWh, LCOE_CCGT_GAS = 45–75 USD/MWh. Source: expert exchange 8 (2026-10-03), order-of-magnitude, US utility-scale, before any carbon cost. Interval of validity: mid-2020s cost levels; used as the relative-cost basis for judging whether the compact's buildout targets are economically supportable (they are, since renewables are at/below new gas) and for bounding the no-policy growth rate (the economics are favorable but the firm-capacity constraint and gas-price sensitivity cap the pace absent a forcing mechanism).

## Exchange 9
Question (expert_question_9.md): Do states that buy renewable energy from other states usually get that power by building new transmission lines?
Reply (paraphrased, not copied): not usually as the first step — most interstate renewable purchases move over the existing transmission network (utilities and load-serving entities buy power or renewable energy credits from generators already interconnected, using established ties and contracts); new transmission is built only when the existing grid can't carry the volume or reach the resource. Typical sequence: contract for power/credits first (enabled by existing lines and regional markets), and build new transmission only later, when congestion or a large new resource requires it. New lines are slow, permitting-heavy, and expensive, so they are the exception rather than the default mechanism for buying renewable power across state lines.
Effect on work: this refines the compact's Action 2 (interstate procurement). The 2025 compact target is set so each state can meet its procurement share over the EXISTING four-state interconnection (fast, low-friction — the default mechanism), with new transmission explicitly a later-phase (2030s+) measure needed only if the existing grid can't carry the 2050 volume. This means the 2025 target is achievable on the existing grid (no new-line permitting dependency), while the 2050 target's achievability depends on the transmission build-out that the compact should initiate in the mid-period — so the 2050 target is set as the one that requires the new transmission, and the model flags that dependency explicitly. This also confirms the exchange-6 constraint (permitting latency) applies to the 2050 transmission build-out, not to the 2025 procurement.

## Exchange 10
Question (expert_question_10.md): After a few years of running an energy compact, what usually decides whether the member states keep following through?
Reply (paraphrased, not copied): follow-through is decided by whether the compact keeps delivering tangible, distributed benefits that individual states could not get alone, and whether the political coalition that created it stays in office. Compacts persist when they deliver money or capability to each member (shared procurement, pooled transmission planning, joint federal grants, coordinated siting), have a light flexible structure (voluntary targets, opt-outs, credit trading) rather than binding mandates with visible local costs, and align with each state's own politics and economy. They fade or stall when costs/burdens are concentrated (one state hosts transmission or generation for others' benefit), benefits seem uneven, a change in governorship or legislature shifts priorities, or the original mission becomes obsolete. Absent a forcing mechanism or continued funding, regional energy compacts tend to drift into information-sharing forums rather than binding action.
Effect on work: this shapes the GOVERNANCE design of the compact goals (Part II) and is the reason the model sets the targets as the specific structure below: (a) benefits must be distributed — each state's 2025/2050 target is set so every member gets a concrete, state-specific win (e.g. CA's solar buildout, TX's wind buildout, NM's geothermal/solar, AZ's solar) rather than a single shared number, avoiding the "concentrated burden" failure mode; (b) the structure is light and flexible — the model recommends voluntary targets with credit trading and an opt-out mechanism rather than hard binding mandates, because binding mandates with visible local costs are the failure mode; (c) the compact needs a continued funding/forcing mechanism (joint federal grants, pooled procurement) to avoid drifting into an information-sharing forum — this is an explicit Part II.B action, not an afterthought; (d) political durability — the model notes the targets should be codified at a level that survives a single change in governorship (e.g. a compact statute rather than an executive-level pledge), because a change in governorship is the single biggest follow-through risk. These governance features are what make the Part II.A targets credible rather than aspirational.
