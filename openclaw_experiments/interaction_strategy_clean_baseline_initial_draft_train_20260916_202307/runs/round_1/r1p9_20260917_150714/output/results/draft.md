# Flood Planning — Initial Modeling Plan (Draft)

**Problem ID:** `2005_Flood_Planning`
**Title:** Flood Planning
**Source:** MCM 2005
**Document type:** Modeling blueprint / planning draft (no solution performed)

> Scope note: This document is a *plan*. It describes the intended workflow, assumptions,
> candidate methods, data strategy, implementation roadmap, and validation approach that a
> future modeling team will follow. No data analysis, computation, simulation, fitting, or
> conclusions are included here. All statements are stated in future-oriented language.

---

## 1. Problem Background and Restatement

### 1.1 Background (as given)
Lake Murray lies in central South Carolina and was impounded by a large earthen dam completed
around 1930 for hydroelectric power production. The dam impounds the Saluda River. Beneath and
downstream of the dam, the Saluda River continues to a confluence area where it joins the Broad
River to form the Congaree River near Columbia, South Carolina. The S.C. State Capitol Building
sits on elevated ground overlooking the Congaree River. Rawls Creek is a minor, year-round stream
that enters the Saluda River a short distance downstream of the dam.

### 1.2 Restatement of the planning problem
The future modeling effort will simulate the downstream flood that would be generated if a
catastrophic earthquake were to breach (partially or fully) the earthen dam at Lake Murray. The
analysis is expected to answer two focal questions:

1. **Rawls Creek question.** If the dam fails, how much flooding will occur within Rawls Creek,
   and how far upstream (back) along the creek valley will floodwater extend?
2. **Capitol question.** Could the resulting downstream flood be large enough to reach the
   S.C. State Capitol Building, which sits on a hill above the Congaree River?

### 1.3 What the deliverable will NOT be
This draft will not contain simulated water depths, arrival times, flood extents, inundation
maps, or any yes/no answer to the Capitol question. Those outputs belong to the later
execution phase and will be produced only after the plan below is approved.

## 2. Objectives and Subproblems

### 2.1 Primary objective
To design a defensible, physics-consistent modeling chain that will predict the downstream
flood wave generated by a postulated catastrophic dam breach, and to translate that wave into
(a) inundation along Rawls Creek and (b) a comparison of predicted flood elevations with the
ground elevation of the State Capitol Building.

### 2.2 Subproblems to be decomposed
- **SP1 — Breach scenario definition.** Parameterize plausible catastrophic breach
  geometries (breach width, depth, side slopes, rate of formation, whether full or partial)
  that a major earthquake could plausibly induce in an earthen embankment.
- **SP2 — Reservoir release / outflow hydrograph.** Model how the impounded volume will
  empty through the breach over time to produce a discharge hydrograph at the dam.
- **SP3 — Downstream routing.** Route the flood wave along the Saluda River, through the
  Saluda–Broad confluence, and along the Congaree River toward and through the Columbia area.
- **SP4 — Rawls Creek response.** Model backwater and tributary interaction to determine how
  far floodwater will propagate up Rawls Creek and how deep it will be there.
- **SP5 — Capitol reach evaluation.** Compare predicted water-surface elevations in the
  Congaree reach against the ground elevation at the Capitol to assess reachability.
- **SP6 — Uncertainty and sensitivity.** Quantify how sensitive the answers are to breach
  scenario, roughness, terrain resolution, and initial pool level.

### 2.3 Deliverables (planned)
- A documented breach-to-inundation modeling pipeline (equations, parameters, code modules).
- Scenario definitions and a parameter table with sources/justification.
- Predicted hydrographs at selected control points (dam, Rawls Creek mouth, confluence,
  downtown Columbia reach).
- Inundation extent and depth fields for the Rawls Creek valley and the Congaree reach.
- A reachability assessment (with confidence framing) for the State Capitol.
- A sensitivity/uncertainty report and a limitations statement.

## 3. Assumptions

Each assumption below is listed with its justification and the future validation approach.

### 3.1 Physical / hydrological assumptions
- **A1. Single dominant failure mode.** The breach will be modeled as a dam-break outflow
  through a growing opening; alternative cascading failure mechanisms (e.g., separate
  downstream dam failures) will be treated as scenario variants, not the base case.
  *Justification:* keeps the base model tractable while allowing extension.
  *Validation:* compare base-case assumptions against literature dam-break case studies.
- **A2. Reservoir initial condition.** The lake will be assumed near a specified operating
  pool level (e.g., normal pool) at failure, with lower/higher levels used as sensitivity
  variants. *Justification:* initial level strongly controls released volume.
  *Validation:* test sensitivity to initial level; cross-check against typical operating range.
- **A3. Breach hydrograph governed by breach growth.** Outflow will be governed by an
  orifice/weir-type relation whose area grows over a finite formation time.
  *Validation:* bracket with instantaneous full-breach and slow-growth extremes.
- **A4. Terrain-controlled routing.** Flow will follow the existing river/creek channels and
  valley floors as represented by a digital elevation model (DEM); channel conveyance will be
  inferred from terrain and roughness rather than detailed surveyed cross-sections unless such
  data become available. *Validation:* check DEM channel continuity and against any available
  gauge/photo evidence.
- **A5. Roughness parameterization.** Manning-type roughness will represent valley and channel
  resistance, with values grouped by land-cover class. *Validation:* sensitivity sweep across a
  plausible roughness range.
- **A6. 1-D/2-D coupling.** The main stem (Saluda→Congaree) may be treated in 1-D for speed and
  the Rawls Creek / floodplain areas in 2-D where lateral spreading matters; the choice will be
  confirmed after terrain review. *Validation:* compare 1-D-only vs coupled results at control points.
- **A7. Negligible additional inflow.** Tributary inflows and rainfall during the event will be
  treated as secondary and tested as sensitivity perturbations. *Justification:* dam-break
  discharge is expected to dominate the hydrograph shape.
- **A8. Quasi-static morphological bed.** Bed and bank erosion during the event will be
  neglected in the base case, with an optional erosion/sediment extension noted.
- **A9. Elevation reference consistency.** All elevations will be placed in one consistent
  vertical datum so the Capitol ground elevation is comparable to predicted water surfaces.

### 3.2 Modeling-scope assumptions
- **A10.** The earthquake is treated only as the trigger that defines the breach; seismic
  wave propagation itself is out of scope.
- **A11.** Publicly available terrain/elevation and map data will be the primary geometric
  source; where unavailable, engineering estimates will be documented as such.

## 4. Data Processing Plan

> No data analysis will be performed in this planning phase. Below is the intended processing plan.

### 4.1 Data inventory to be assembled
- **Elevation/terrain:** a DEM covering the Lake Murray dam, the Saluda valley, Rawls Creek
  watershed, the Saluda–Broad confluence, and the Congaree reach through Columbia.
- **Hydrology/geometry:** lake surface area and storage–elevation relationship, dam crest and
  normal pool elevations, approximate channel widths/depths, Rawls Creek alignment.
- **Land cover / roughness proxies:** classes used to assign roughness coefficients.
- **Reference features:** geolocated coordinates of Rawls Creek, the confluence, and the
  State Capitol Building, plus its approximate ground elevation.
- **Meteorological/hydrologic context (optional):** typical seasonal flows to contextualize
  baseline conditions.

### 4.2 Preprocessing steps (planned)
1. **Ingestion & harmonization.** Reproject all geospatial layers to a common coordinate
   reference system and a single vertical datum.
2. **DEM conditioning.** Fill/repair sinks where appropriate, enforce downstream drainage,
   and confirm channel connectivity along the path of interest.
3. **Domain delineation.** Clip a computational domain covering the dam-to-Columbia corridor
   plus the Rawls Creek watershed; define boundary conditions at the domain edges.
4. **Mesh / cross-section construction.** Build either a 1-D cross-section network along the
   main stem and/or a 2-D computational mesh, sized to resolve channel and near-channel flow.
5. **Roughness assignment.** Map land-cover classes to roughness values and attach them to the
   mesh/cross-sections.
6. **Control-point definition.** Predefine extraction points (dam, Rawls Creek mouth/upstream,
   confluence, downtown Columbia, Capitol vicinity) for later hydrograph and stage reporting.
7. **Quality control.** Document resolution, datums, gaps, and any manual corrections so that
   results can be traced to inputs.

### 4.3 Feature construction (planned)
- Storage–elevation and elevation–area curves derived from the lake geometry for the release model.
- Breach-geometry parameter sets (width, depth, slope, formation time) as scenario features.
- Terrain-derived channel conveyance properties (width, slope, hydraulic radius proxies).
- Capitol comparison feature: ground elevation value versus predicted water-surface elevation.

### 4.4 Data usage strategy
- **Base case:** best-available terrain + central roughness + mid-range breach scenario.
- **Sensitivity set:** perturbations of breach size/timing, pool level, roughness, and mesh
  resolution.
- **Extreme set:** bounding scenarios (instantaneous full breach and gradual/worst-credible
  breach) intended to bracket the plausible answer range.
- Strict licensing/attribution will be recorded for every external dataset used.

## 5. Candidate Model Framework

### 5.1 Overall modeling chain (planned)
Breach trigger → reservoir depletion model → dam-break outflow hydrograph → downstream
routing (1-D and/or 2-D) → Rawls Creek floodplain response → stage comparison at the Capitol
reach → uncertainty/sensitivity layer.

### 5.2 Candidate models per subproblem

**SP1 — Breach scenario generation**
- Candidate: parameterized breach geometry with a growth function (e.g., linear or S-shaped
  widening). Reported as a scenario matrix rather than a single value.
- Alternatives: empirical breach-parameter regressions from dam-engineering literature.

**SP2 — Reservoir release**
- Candidate A: **level-pool (lumped) reservoir routing** using the storage–elevation curve and
  a weir/orifice breach relation; gives a discharge hydrograph at the dam cheaply.
- Candidate B: **2-D dam-break over the reservoir–breach area** for localized detail.
- Trade-off: A is fast and standard; B is more detailed but costlier. Likely start with A,
  escalate to B if the near-dam flow field matters.

**SP3 — Downstream routing**
- Candidate A: **1-D unsteady open-channel flow (Saint-Venant)** along the Saluda–Congaree
  main stem, suited to long, channelized reaches.
- Candidate B: **2-D shallow-water (depth-averaged) flow** for floodplain spreading, confluence
  mixing, and urban reaches.
- Candidate C: **coupled 1-D/2-D** hybrid — 1-D for the long river, 2-D where lateral inundation
  and urban complexity dominate.
- Mathematical ideas: conservation of mass and momentum, shallow-water approximations,
  finite-volume Godunov-type schemes with wetting/drying, Manning friction closure.

**SP4 — Rawls Creek response**
- Candidate: treat Rawls Creek as a tributary connected to the main stem via a **backwater**
  boundary; if the main-stem stage exceeds the creek mouth elevation, apply upstream-propagating
  backwater to determine how far flooding extends up the creek.
- Alternative: full 2-D simulation of the creek valley to capture lateral storage.
- Key concept: flooding in Rawls Creek may be driven primarily by *backwater from the Saluda*
  rather than by creek discharge itself — this will be explicitly tested.

**SP5 — Capitol reach comparison**
- Candidate: extract maximum predicted water-surface elevation near the Capitol and compare
  against its terrain elevation; express the outcome as a margin/overtopping-style comparison
  with uncertainty bounds rather than a bare yes/no.

**SP6 — Uncertainty layer**
- Candidate: scenario ensemble + local/global sensitivity (one-at-a-time sweeps and
  variance-based screening) to rank which parameters drive the two focal answers.

### 5.3 Key variables (planned)
- **State/output variables:** discharge hydrograph Q(t), water depth h(x,t), water-surface
  elevation z(x,t), inundation extent, arrival time, maximum stage envelope.
- **Input/parameter variables:** breach width/depth/slope/formation time, initial pool level,
  storage–elevation coefficients, Manning roughness, mesh resolution, boundary conditions.

### 5.4 Advantages and limitations of the framework
- **Advantages:** physically interpretable; standard, well-documented hydraulics; supports
  bracketing of extremes; modular so that Rawls Creek and the Capitol reach can be analyzed
  independently while sharing one main-stem solution.
- **Limitations:** sensitive to DEM resolution and roughness; 1-D cannot capture lateral
  spreading; sediment/erosion and debris ignored in base case; empirical breach parameters
  carry wide uncertainty; data availability may force engineering estimates.

## 6. Implementation Roadmap

### 6.1 Required modules (planned)
1. **Data module** — load, reproject, condition terrain; produce mesh/cross-sections and
   roughness maps.
2. **Scenario module** — generate breach parameter sets and initial pool levels.
3. **Hydraulics module** — reservoir release (level-pool and/or 2-D) and downstream routing
   (1-D and/or 2-D), with wetting/drying and stable time-stepping.
4. **Analysis module** — extract hydrographs, maximum stage envelopes, and inundation depth/
   extent at control points; compute Rawls Creek backwater limits.
5. **Comparison module** — evaluate the Capitol reach against its ground elevation with
   uncertainty margins.
6. **Reporting module** — assemble maps, tables, and a traceable narrative (execution phase).

### 6.2 Workflow (planned order of operations)
1. Confirm problem framing and approve assumptions (this draft).
2. Assemble and QC the terrain/hydrology/land-cover data.
3. Define base-case and bounding breach scenarios.
4. Build and validate the reservoir release model.
5. Build the main-stem routing model; calibration/consistency checks.
6. Couple or interface the Rawls Creek tributary; analyze backwater extent.
7. Compute stages through the Columbia reach; compare with Capitol elevation.
8. Run the sensitivity/uncertainty ensemble.
9. Interpret, document, and report (execution phase).

### 6.3 Algorithm/solver considerations
- Prefer finite-volume shallow-water solvers with conservative mass update and robust
  wetting/drying; use adaptive or stable time-stepping (e.g., CFL-controlled).
- Use level-pool routing as a fast first pass, then verify against a 2-D near-dam run.
- Guard against numerical pitfalls: dry-cell instability, high-slope cells, and
  boundary-reflection artifacts.
- Design the pipeline so scenario runs can be parallelized and reproduced from fixed seeds
  and versioned inputs.

### 6.4 Software / engineering plan
- Open-source hydraulic solvers and GIS/terrain tooling will be evaluated and selected against
  the reproducibility and licensing requirements of the task.
- All inputs, parameters, and code versions will be logged to make the eventual results auditable.

## 7. Validation Strategy

### 7.1 Evaluation metrics (planned)
- **Hydraulic consistency:** mass-balance residuals for the reservoir release and routing.
- **Hydrograph realism:** peak magnitude, time-to-peak, and attenuation behavior compared with
  expectations for dam-break waves.
- **Inundation metrics:** extent (area) and depth against any available reference maps/flood
  records or expert-expected envelopes.
- **Convergence metrics:** sensitivity of results to mesh refinement and time-step size.
- **Comparison metric:** elevation margin between predicted water surface and Capitol ground
  elevation, reported with uncertainty bands.

### 7.2 Validation methods (planned)
- **Benchmark tests:** standard dam-break verification cases (e.g., idealized channel and
  floodplain benchmarks) to confirm the solver behaves correctly.
- **Cross-model checks:** compare level-pool vs 2-D release, and 1-D vs coupled 1-D/2-D routing.
- **Historical plausibility:** where records exist, compare qualitative behavior with documented
  regional floods; treat as plausibility evidence, not proof.
- **Peer/engineering review:** sanity-check breach parameters against dam-engineering practice.

### 7.3 Sensitivity and uncertainty analysis (planned)
- One-at-a-time sweeps over breach width/depth/formation time, initial pool level, and roughness.
- Scenario ensemble combining bounding breach cases to bracket the answer range.
- Variance-based screening to identify which parameters dominate each focal answer.
- Explicit propagation of parameter uncertainty into the Capitol reachability statement and the
  Rawls Creek upstream-extent estimate.

### 7.4 Reproducibility
- Version-controlled inputs, documented parameter sources, and scripted runs so that any
  reported number can be regenerated.

## 8. Expected Result Interpretation

This section describes how future results will be *interpreted*, not what they will be.

- **Rawls Creek flooding:** the results will be read as a maximum inundation depth profile and
  an upstream limit of flooding along the creek valley. Interpretation will distinguish
  **backwater-driven** flooding (Saluda stage pushing water up the creek) from **creek-driven**
  flooding, and will report the upstream extent with a confidence interval tied to roughness
  and breach-scenario uncertainty.
- **Capitol reachability:** the outcome will not be a bare "yes/no." Instead, the predicted
  maximum water-surface elevation near the Capitol will be compared with the building's ground
  elevation, and the difference will be reported as a margin, together with uncertainty bands
  from the sensitivity ensemble. Interpretation will be phrased in terms of likelihood and
  bracketing (e.g., "reaches under X% of scenarios," if the analysis later supports it).
- **Downstream travel and attenuation:** the hydrograph evolution will be interpreted in terms
  of peak attenuation and arrival timing along the Saluda→Congaree corridor.
- **Scenario dependence:** any difference between bounding and central scenarios will be
  interpreted as the uncertainty attributable to breach characterization and initial pool level.

## 9. Limitations and Improvements

### 9.1 Anticipated limitations
- Terrain resolution and roughness uncertainty will dominate the accuracy of inundation extent,
  especially in the narrow Rawls Creek valley.
- 1-D treatment of the main stem will under-represent lateral floodplain storage unless coupled
  to 2-D reaches.
- Empirical breach parameters carry large uncertainty; the earthquake-to-breach mapping is
  inherently scenario-based rather than deterministic.
- Baseline conditions, tributary inflows, and rainfall during the event may be simplified.
- Sediment transport, debris loading, and morphological change will be omitted in the base case.
- Urban infrastructure (bridges, culverts, embankments) may not be fully resolved by coarse
  terrain and could locally alter flow paths.

### 9.2 Planned improvements / extensions
- Mesh refinement in Rawls Creek and near the Capitol reach.
- Coupled 1-D/2-D (or fully 2-D) modeling in urban Columbia.
- Inclusion of an erosion/sediment module and debris effects.
- Ensemble-based probabilistic flood mapping with explicit confidence statements.
- Assimilation of any obtainable gauge, survey, or historical flood data for calibration.
- Formal report of uncertainty alongside every headline result.

### 9.3 Decision framing
The final study will be structured so that decision-makers can see, for each focal question,
the central estimate, the bounding scenarios, and the dominant uncertainty sources — supporting
robust planning rather than a single-point answer.
